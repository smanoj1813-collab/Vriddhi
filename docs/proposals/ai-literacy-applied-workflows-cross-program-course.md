# Cross-program course proposal: AI literacy and applied workflows

**Status:** concept proposal; not an approved or bundled course pack.
**Intended learners:** undergraduate and postgraduate students across Arts, Commerce, Business, Science, Technology, Education, Law, Health and other programmes.
**Prerequisite:** basic browser and document skills; no coding, paid subscription or statistics background required.
**Suggested size:** 42 notional hours. The college should map credits and delivery requirements to its own regulations before adoption.

## Why this can serve every programme

Every discipline works with information, evidence, decisions and communication. The course teaches those transferable practices with one common core, then lets each learner apply them to a domain problem they understand. It does not assume that every student wants to become an AI developer, nor that a single model or vendor is suitable for every discipline.

## Shared learning outcomes

By the end, students should be able to:

1. Explain common generative-AI capabilities and limitations in plain language and select an appropriate, approved tool for a defined task.
2. Use AI to support research and synthesis while tracing claims to credible sources and separating evidence from generated text.
3. Inspect and summarise data without confusing correlation, generated interpretation or a polished chart with proof.
4. Produce audience-appropriate written, visual or oral communication, with human review for accuracy, accessibility and attribution.
5. Map a repeated workflow, identify where AI is useful or harmful, and design a human-owned approval and failure path.
6. Assess privacy, consent, bias, security, intellectual-property and accessibility risks before sharing data or publishing output.
7. Demonstrate a small, tested solution to a real problem in the learner’s own discipline and explain its limits using an evidence log.

## Proposed structure (42 hours)

| Unit | Hours | Common activity | Discipline-specific application examples |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| 1. AI literacy, prompting and verification | 6 | Define a task, direct an AI tool, check claims and log corrections | Compare a generated explanation with a course text, lab protocol or policy |
| 2. Research, reading and evidence | 8 | Search approved sources, summarise with citations, identify unsupported claims | Literature scan, case-law reading list, market brief or environmental report |
| 3. Data sensemaking | 8 | Clean a small public or synthetic dataset, ask questions, visualise cautiously | Budget patterns, survey results, lab measurements or community indicators |
| 4. Communication and accessible content | 6 | Adapt one verified message for different audiences and formats | Public-health explainer, museum label, business memo or science poster |
| 5. Workflow design and responsible automation | 6 | Map a process, choose manual/AI steps, test exceptions and approval points | Enquiry triage, reading support, stock reconciliation or appointment reminders |
| 6. Cross-program applied project | 8 | Prototype a small workflow, test it with representative users, present evidence and limitations | A real, scoped problem proposed by a learner, department or community partner |

Examples are options, not requirements. Students may choose non-AI or low-tech solutions if those better fit their problem. Use public, synthetic or appropriately de-identified data for practice; do not upload sensitive records to an unapproved service.

## Assessment concept

- **Verification portfolio (25%):** prompt, source/evidence log, correction examples and a short reflection.
- **Data and communication artefact (20%):** a small analysis plus an accessible, cited explanation.
- **Workflow map and risk review (20%):** current process, proposed change, exception path, privacy/security checks and human owner.
- **Applied project (25%):** a working prototype or documented manual pilot, user feedback, test results and limitations.
- **Professional reflection (10%):** what the student will use, avoid and verify in their own field.

Apply one common rubric across programmes: problem fit, evidence quality, domain accuracy, usability/accessibility, responsible-data practice, testing and honest reflection. Mark the learner’s reasoning and contribution rather than the novelty of the AI tool. Offer equivalent formats and reasonable accommodations.

## Delivery and governance decisions before adoption

- Invite programme faculty and students to select the examples and review domain accuracy; keep the shared outcomes and common rubric intact.
- Confirm approved tools, institutional data terms, retention, account ownership, accessibility and offline alternatives before the first cohort.
- Have a named course owner, review current laws and institutional policies using authoritative sources, maintain a versioned source list and publish an incident/appeal route.
- Pilot with a small, representative mix of programmes. Evaluate task completion, learning evidence, errors, accessibility and staff/student workload—not just sign-ups or generated output volume.
- Avoid claiming that the course replaces disciplinary methods, professional judgement or regulated advice. Certificate eligibility should be explicit and distinguish app completion from college credit or formal award.

## Recommendation

Develop this as a shared foundational elective with a stable interdisciplinary core and a rotating set of local case studies. That approach allows Arts, Commerce, Science and professional-programme students to learn together while preserving meaningful, discipline-specific work. Author the full course pack only after a cross-programme faculty group approves outcomes, examples, assessment moderation and the governance plan.
