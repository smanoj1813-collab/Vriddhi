# 3.3 Copywriting fundamentals

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Write hooks that stop the scroll by naming the reader's situation or stake.
- Structure copy around the reader's self-interest: benefits before features.
- Write calls to action that name the exact next step.
- Apply the two professional tests: the "so what?" ladder and reading aloud.

## Opening scenario

Two ads ran for the same Bengaluru cold-press juice brand.

Ad 1: "Our juices are made from 100% natural ingredients with no added sugar and are rich in antioxidants. We use cold-pressed technology to retain nutrients. Try our wide range of delicious flavours!"

Ad 2: "2 PM. Your eyes are closing and the coffee has stopped working. A bottle of cold-press watermelon-ginger lasts you the whole afternoon — no sugar crash, no 5 PM headache. Tap 'Find a store near you'."

The first describes the product. The second puts the reader inside their own afternoon and hands them the exit. Copywriting is not describing — it is **engineering the reader's next thought**. Every principle in this lesson is a tool for that.

## Core ideas

### 1. The hook: earn the next second

Nobody reads the second line if the first does nothing. Four hooks that reliably work:

- **Recognition:** "2 PM. Your eyes are closing…" — the reader sees themselves.
- **Specificity:** "₹412. That is what one abandoned cart costs this store" — numbers beat adjectives.
- **Stakes:** "Most students discover the document list two days before the deadline" — gain or loss made immediate.
- **Curiosity with a promise:** "The fee structure nobody explains until you ask" — a gap the reader now wants closed.

Whatever the hook, it must be paid off honestly — a hook that overpromises earns one click and permanent distrust.

### 2. Features tell, benefits sell — the "so what?" ladder

A feature is what the product has; a benefit is what the reader's life becomes. You climb from one to the other by asking "so what?" until the answer is a feeling:

```
Feature:     Cold-pressed, no added sugar
So what?     → No sugar spike
So what?     → No 4 PM crash
So what?     → You finish the afternoon clear-headed
Benefit:     Energy that doesn't betray you at 4 PM
```

Write features in the spec sheet; write benefits in the copy. The classic structure pairs them: **benefit first, feature as proof**. "Energy that lasts the afternoon (cold-pressed, zero added sugar)" — the reader buys the afternoon, and the parenthetical makes it believable.

### 3. Structure: make skimming the intended experience

Readers skim; design for it:

- **One idea per paragraph**, and paragraphs of one to three sentences on mobile.
- **Subheads that carry the argument** — a skimmer who reads only subheads should still get the pitch.
- **Lists and tables** for anything enumerable (prices, steps, comparisons).
- **The inverted pyramid:** the point first, support after — exactly the "answer first" pattern from on-page SEO (Lesson 2.2). Copywriting and SEO converge because both serve a reader in a hurry.

### 4. The call to action: name the next step

Weak CTAs describe your availability ("Follow us for more"). Strong CTAs are **verbs with specifics**:

- "Tap 'Find a store near you'" — action + where.
- "Get the document checklist (free PDF) before the deadline" — action + reward + urgency that is real.
- "Book a free 15-minute counselling call this week" — action + duration + timeframe.

One CTA per piece. Two CTAs halve the response rate, because a confused reader does nothing. Urgency must be true — a fake countdown is a trust transaction you lose.

### 5. Clarity is the style

Short words, active voice, concrete nouns. "Utilise our comprehensive solutions" is a fog; "use the fee calculator" is a door. Read the draft aloud: every place you stumble, a reader stops. Every sentence that needs a second read is a sentence that will not get one. The professional's test for any line: **could a tired reader on a bus understand it in one pass?**

## Try it now

Find any advertisement on your phone right now — an Instagram ad, a banner, a WhatsApp forward. Run the three checks: does the hook earn the next second? Is there a benefit, or only features? Does the CTA name the exact next step? Rewrite the worst line.

## Hands-on lab: copy for your calendar piece (45 min)

Write the copy set for one cluster topic from your 3.1 calendar.

1. **Hook pass (10 min):** Write four hooks for the same piece — one recognition, one specificity, one stakes, one curiosity. Pick the strongest and note why in one line.
2. **Benefit ladder (10 min):** Take the product/service's top three features and run each up the "so what?" ladder to a felt benefit. Write the benefit-first lines.
3. **The piece (15 min):** Draft the full copy — hook, benefit-led body with skimmable structure, one CTA. If it is a social post, write the caption AND the first-comment CTA; if a blog section, write the H2s as the argument.
4. **The two tests (5 min):** Read aloud and mark every stumble. Apply the tired-reader-on-a-bus test to the first screen.
5. **Variant pass (5 min):** Produce three headline/subject-line variants of the same piece — you will use them in Module 4's ads and Module 6's email.

> **Deliverable:** four hooks + benefit ladders + the finished piece + three headline variants. These variants feed Project Block 2 and your ad lab in Module 4.

## Common mistakes

- **Feature lists in the headline.** Nobody has ever felt "100% natural" the way they feel "no 5 PM headache".
- **Multiple CTAs.** "Follow, share, and visit our website" is three ways of asking the reader to do nothing.
- **Fake urgency.** Countdowns that reset destroy the trust every future campaign needs.
- **Writing to impress other marketers.** The reader is a person on a bus, not a jury of copywriters.

## Key takeaways

- Hooks earn the next second: recognition, specificity, stakes or honest curiosity.
- Climb "so what?" from feature to felt benefit; lead with the benefit, prove with the feature.
- Design for skimming: one idea per paragraph, argumentative subheads, the point first.
- One specific, honest call to action per piece; clarity is the only style that matters.
