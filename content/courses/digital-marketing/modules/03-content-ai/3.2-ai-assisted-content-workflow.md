# 3.2 The AI-assisted content workflow

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Run the five-step AI-assisted workflow: brief → draft → verify → edit → disclose.
- Write content briefs that make AI drafts usable on the first pass.
- Fact-check AI output systematically: claims, sources, quotes and numbers.
- Keep brand voice consistent when machines draft and humans decide.

## Opening scenario

A student used a chatbot to write a blog post "10 best study-abroad scholarships for Indian students". It published beautifully — deadlines, amounts, eligibility, all in confident tables. Three readers emailed to say the numbers were wrong. Most were: the model had mixed years, invented one scholarship's amount, and cited a deadline that had passed eighteen months ago. The post was pulled; the consultancy's credibility took a year to rebuild.

The next post was written differently. The student wrote the brief herself (audience, the ten scholarships *she verified on official sites*, the angle), asked the AI to structure and draft around her verified facts, checked every claim against her sources, edited the opening, and disclosed AI assistance. Faster than writing alone, slower than pure AI — and completely safe to publish.

AI-assisted content is not a shortcut that removes the writer. It is a **workflow where the machine produces words and the human owns the truth**. This lesson is that workflow.

## Core ideas

### 1. Why AI drafts hallucinate — and why prompting cannot fix it

Generative models predict plausible text, not verified text. "Plausible" includes wrong numbers, mixed-up dates, invented programme names and citations that do not exist. Prompting harder ("only include verified facts") does not fix this — the model does not *know* which of its facts are verified. Therefore the professional rule is fixed regardless of model quality:

> **The AI writes; you own every claim.** Anything you cannot verify does not publish.

### 2. The five-step workflow

1. **Brief (you, 15 min).** Audience, topic, angle, the exact facts and sources to use, structure, tone, length, and the call to action. A good brief is half the work: it converts the model from a guesser into a formatter of *your* verified material. Attach your research: keyword map entries, interview quotes, price lists.
2. **Draft (AI, minutes).** Ask for the draft *from the brief*, not from the topic. Give it your verified facts explicitly: "Use these ten scholarships with these amounts from these URLs; do not add others." Request structure options, not just text — an outline first, then the draft.
3. **Verify (you, the non-negotiable half hour).** Line-by-line: every number, date, name, claim and quotation is either (a) already in your verified source list, or (b) checked against an authoritative source. Anything unverifiable is cut. Special attention to: statistics without sources, confident specifics (deadlines, prices, legal claims), and quotes attributed to real people.
4. **Edit (you, voice and structure).** Fix the opening (AI openings are usually generic), cut filler, tighten to your voice, reorder for the funnel stage. Read it aloud once — your ear catches what your eye forgives.
5. **Disclose (policy).** Where AI assistance is material, say so in the way your client or platform requires. Many publications now require it; honesty ages well, and in this course every project includes an **AI-use statement** listing what the machine drafted and what you verified.

### 3. Brand voice: the style sheet trick

AI output drifts generic. The fix is a one-page **voice sheet** you paste into every brief: three adjectives for the brand (e.g. plain-spoken, local, practical), words we use, words we never use ("unlock", "game-changer", "delve"), sentence-length feel, how we open (with a scene or a fact, never "In today's world"), and one rewritten example of good vs bad. With a voice sheet, drafts arrive 80% right; without one, you rewrite everything anyway.

### 4. Where AI genuinely accelerates marketing content

- **Structuring research you already have** into outlines, tables and drafts.
- **Variants:** ten subject-line options, five ad headlines, three social cuts of one long piece.
- **Repurposing:** turning your verified guide into a carousel script, email section or reel hook (Lesson 3.4).
- **First-pass editing:** "tighten this to 200 words, keep every fact".
- **Translation drafts** into Kannada/Hindi for bilingual campaigns — with a human speaker reviewing before publishing.

The pattern in every safe use: **you supply the truth, the machine supplies the words.** The pattern in every disaster: the machine supplies both.

## Try it now

Ask any chatbot: "What is the income limit for [a specific government scholarship]?" Then find the official site and check. Whatever happens — right, wrong or vague — you have now felt the verification instinct this lesson installs.

## Hands-on lab: one brief-to-publish cycle (45 min)

Produce one short, publishable piece (a 400–600 word blog section or carousel) using the full workflow.

1. **Brief (10 min):** Choose one cluster topic from your 3.1 calendar. Write the brief: audience, angle, verified facts (find and list two sources yourself), structure, voice-sheet adjectives, CTA.
2. **Draft (10 min):** Prompt a chatbot with the full brief plus your verified facts and explicit instruction not to add new factual claims. Ask for an outline first, then the draft.
3. **Verify (10 min):** Go claim by claim. Mark each: ✓ (in my sources), ✗ (wrong — fix or cut), ? (cannot verify — cut). Keep the marks; they go in your deliverable.
4. **Edit (10 min):** Rewrite the opening in your voice, cut filler by 20%, read aloud once, confirm the CTA matches the funnel stage.
5. **AI-use statement (5 min):** Write the three-line statement: what AI drafted, what you verified, what you changed.

> **Deliverable:** brief + AI draft + your claim-by-claim verification marks + final edited piece + AI-use statement. This cycle, repeated, is Project Block 2's core.

## Common mistakes

- **Publishing unverified drafts** because they read confidently. Confidence is a writing style, not a source.
- **Prompting instead of verifying.** "Be accurate" is not a control; a source list is.
- **Skipping the brief.** Without one, the model guesses content AND facts.
- **Hiding AI use.** Disclosure is becoming standard practice; being caught erasing it costs more than the sentence ever did.

## Key takeaways

- AI predicts plausible text; plausibility includes fabrication, so you own every claim you publish.
- The workflow — brief → draft → verify → edit → disclose — makes AI a speed multiplier without making it a risk multiplier.
- A one-page voice sheet keeps machine drafts on brand from the first sentence.
- Safe pattern: you supply the truth, the machine supplies the words.
