# 2.2 On-page SEO

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Write title tags and meta descriptions that earn clicks without misleading.
- Structure a page with one H1 and a logical heading hierarchy that serves readers first.
- Match page content to search intent using the "answer first, then explain" pattern.
- Build internal links that spread authority and guide both users and crawlers.

## Opening scenario

A Tumakuru tuition centre had a page titled "Home" with the words "Welcome to our website!" in giant letters, and every paragraph inside a single bold block. Its rival, run by a quieter teacher, had a page titled "B.Com Tuition in Tumakuru — 1st Year Accounts & Taxation" whose first sentence was "Batch timings: evening 6–8 PM, weekday and weekend options for B.Com 1st-year Accounts and Taxation, 10 minutes from Tumakuru bus stand." Same teaching quality, same city — but the rival's page told both the searcher and the search engine, in the first two lines, exactly what it was and for whom.

On-page SEO is the craft of writing pages that are **simultaneously excellent for the human reader and unambiguous for the machine**. There is no trick here — only clarity, structure and intent-matching, applied consistently.

## Core ideas

### 1. Title tags: the single highest-leverage 60 characters

The title tag is usually what shows as the blue link on the results page. Rules that earn clicks:

- **Lead with the primary keyword phrase, naturally** — "B.Com Tuition in Tumakuru | Accounts & Taxation" not "Welcome to Excellence Academy".
- **Stay under ~60 characters** so it is not cut off on mobile.
- **Add a differentiator** — subject, locality, price range, or next step.
- **Never mislead.** A title that promises "fees list" but opens a sales page earns a click and then a bounce, which teaches the engine to trust you less.

Every important page deserves a unique title. Duplicate titles are wasted real estate and confuse indexing.

### 2. Meta descriptions: your free ad copy

The meta description rarely affects ranking directly, but it is your 150-character advertisement on the results page. Write it like ad copy: what the searcher gets, for whom, and one proof point or reason to believe. Include the phrase (Google bolds matching words), a location if local, and no clickbait. If you leave it empty, Google invents a snippet — you would not let an intern write your ad copy unreviewed, so do not leave it to the algorithm either.

### 3. Headings: one H1, then a real hierarchy

- **Exactly one H1** per page — the page's headline, carrying the primary phrase naturally.
- **H2s for the page's main sections** (often phrased as the questions the searcher asks), **H3s** inside them.
- Headings are a table of contents for skimmers: a reader who reads only your headings should still understand the page.

Search engines read headings to understand structure; screen readers use them for navigation. Writing headings properly is SEO and accessibility at once — and it is the difference between a page that gets read and a wall of text.

### 4. Content that matches intent: answer first, then explain

Look at how the best pages are built: the question is answered in the first paragraph, then the detail follows. If the phrase is "eggless cake price list", the page *leads with the price list*, then explains sizes, delivery areas and ordering. The pattern:

1. **Direct answer** to the implied question, above the fold, including the keyword phrase naturally.
2. **Substance:** the table, the list, the steps — the thing the searcher came for.
3. **Depth:** details, FAQs (harvest these from People Also Ask), examples, local specifics.
4. **Next step:** one clear action — order, call, enquire.

Write for the reader who is *skimming on a phone*. Short paragraphs, lists, tables where tables belong. Keyword usage: the phrase in the title, the H1, the first paragraph and one or two headings is enough — "keyword stuffing" (mechanically repeating the phrase) is both a readability crime and a ranking risk.

### 5. Internal links: your own roads

Links between your pages do two jobs: they let crawlers discover everything, and they pass authority from strong pages to the pages you want to rank. Practical rules:

- Use **descriptive anchor text** — "B.Com 1st year syllabus and fees" not "click here".
- Link from your high-traffic pages (usually the home page and blog posts) to your money pages (order, enquiry, pricing).
- Every page should be reachable in **three clicks** from the home page.

## Try it now

Search a phrase from your keyword map and open the top organic result. Find its title tag, H1 and first paragraph. In ninety seconds, note: does the page answer the query in the first screen? Is the heading hierarchy clean? You have just reverse-engineered the standard you need to beat.

## Hands-on lab: optimise one real page (45 min)

Pick one important page of your lab business (or the rival from the opening scenario pattern).

1. **Audit (10 min):** Record today's title tag, meta description, H1 and heading structure. Grade each: clear/unclear, unique/duplicate, intent-matched/not.
2. **Rewrite the title + description (10 min):** Draft three title options under 60 characters containing the primary phrase; pick the best. Write a 150-character meta description with phrase + location + proof.
3. **Restructure (15 min):** Rewrite the H1, then outline the page as H2/H3 headings that mirror the searcher's questions. Move the direct answer into the first paragraph.
4. **Internal links (5 min):** List three existing pages that should link to this page, with the anchor text you would use.
5. **Before/after note (5 min):** Write two sentences on what changed and why, in client-friendly language.

> **Deliverable:** before/after of title, description, H1, heading outline, opening paragraph and internal-link plan for one page. This is the template you will apply across the whole site in Project Block 1.

## Common mistakes

- **"Welcome to our website."** Home-page titles and intros that name nobody and answer nothing.
- **Multiple H1s or no headings at all** — machines and skimmers both give up.
- **Burying the answer** under three paragraphs of company history.
- **Anchor text like "click here"** — it tells neither users nor crawlers what the destination is about.

## Key takeaways

- Title tag (≤60 chars, primary phrase, differentiator) is the highest-leverage on-page element; the meta description is your free ad.
- One H1, logical H2/H3 hierarchy, answer-first content structure — good for readers, rankings and accessibility together.
- Match the page to the intent behind the phrase; stuffing keywords neither reads nor ranks.
- Descriptive internal links connect strong pages to money pages and keep the whole site crawlable.
