# 2.4 Off-page SEO, links and authority

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain why search engines treat links as votes of confidence, and which votes actually count.
- Distinguish earned, built and bought links — and the risks of the third kind.
- Run a digital-PR link-earning play that a small business can realistically execute.
- Assess any backlink opportunity with a five-question quality filter.

## Opening scenario

Two Mysuru wedding photographers had equally good websites. One spent a year asking in Facebook groups, "Please exchange links with my site" — collecting 60 links from unrelated pages: a dental clinic in Punjab, a travel blog in Thailand, a shoe shop. The other did one thing: she wrote a genuinely useful guide, "Karnataka marriage registration: documents, timeline and fees (2026 update)", and sent it to five local news portals and two legal-aid blogs with a short note. Three wrote about it and linked to her guide.

A year later, her site ranked for "wedding photographer Mysuru"; the link-swap site did not. Sixty links lost to three — because search engines do not count links, they count **trust transferred by links**. That is the whole of off-page SEO in one sentence.

## Core ideas

### 1. Links are votes — but votes have different weights

When page A links to page B, it casts a vote for B. The value of the vote depends on:

- **Who votes:** a link from an established news site, university or government page outweighs a link from a brand-new blog. A link from a shoe shop to a photographer is near-worthless — relevance of the linking page matters.
- **How they vote:** the anchor text ("this Mysuru photographer's registration guide" transfers more meaning than "click here"), and whether the link is a real editorial choice rather than an auto-generated footer link.
- **Where they vote:** a link inside an article's body beats one buried in a blogroll, a comment or a directory that lists everyone.

Search engines also look at the **pattern of growth**: 60 identical links appearing in one week from unrelated sites looks like what it is. A steady drip of genuine mentions looks like a business becoming known — which is the signal the algorithm is actually trying to measure.

### 2. Earn, build — never buy

- **Earned links** (the gold standard): someone found your work and linked voluntarily. Useful tools, original data, genuinely helpful local guides, strong opinions backed by evidence.
- **Built links** (legitimate when manual and honest): you create the occasion — guest articles, podcast appearances, partnerships, sponsorships of local events, business-association directories, supplier "where to buy" pages.
- **Bought links** (the trap): paying for links on networks, link farms or "SEO packages" with 500 directory submissions. Search engines penalise manipulative patterns when they find them — and recovery is far more expensive than restraint. Any offer that sells links by the dozen is selling risk.

### 3. The small-business link plays that actually work

You do not need an agency to earn authority. Realistic plays, roughly in order of effort:

1. **Be genuinely local-newsworthy:** sponsor the college fest, run a free workshop, take a public stand on something your community cares about — then *tell local news portals about it* with a short, factual email.
2. **Publish the resource nobody else has:** the registration-documents guide, the local price survey, the annual "what students actually pay" data. Original, useful, citable — the three properties that earn links forever.
3. **HARO-style responsiveness:** journalists and newsletter writers constantly ask for local sources; being the fast, quotable expert earns news links.
4. **Partnerships and associations:** your chamber of commerce, industry association, alumni network — most list members with links.
5. **Reviews and testimonials:** give a genuine testimonial to a supplier whose product you use; many publish it with your name and link.

The common thread: **be worth linking to, then make sure the right people know.**

### 4. The five-question quality filter

Before chasing any link opportunity, ask:

1. Does the linking site have real traffic and a real audience (check roughly: does it appear in Google Trends/News, do its articles have real comments/shares)?
2. Is it topically relevant or locally relevant to the business?
3. Would a human actually click this link?
4. Is the link editorial (chosen by a writer) rather than auto-placed?
5. Would you want the link even if search engines did not exist?

Five yeses: pursue. Two noes: walk away. This filter saves you from every shady offer in your inbox for the rest of your career.

### 5. What else lives off-page

Off-page SEO is bigger than links: **reviews** (prominence for local ranking), **brand searches** (people typing your name directly — the strongest trust signal of all), **social mentions** (correlated with authority even where links are absent), and **citation consistency** (your NAP everywhere, matching). The healthiest off-page strategy sounds like a business strategy: be known, be findable, be worth recommending.

## Try it now

Pick any established local business and search its name plus "news". Find one news article that mentions (and ideally links to) it. Note what the business *did* to earn that coverage — an event, a milestone, a controversy handled well. That is a link play you can replicate for your lab business.

## Hands-on lab: the link-earning play (45 min)

1. **Asset inventory (10 min):** For your lab business (or a hypothetical one), list five things it could publish or do that a local journalist would find worth covering. Force yourself past the first two obvious ones.
2. **The citable resource (15 min):** Outline one genuinely useful local resource article — working title, the five questions it answers, and where the data would come from. Test: is anything this specific already on page one for its topic? If yes, sharpen further.
3. **The pitch list (10 min):** Find five real outlets or blogs that cover that locality/category — local news portals, community blogs, association sites. For each, note why their readers would care (one line).
4. **The pitch email (10 min):** Write a five-sentence pitch: who you are, what the resource is, why their readers care, the link, and an easy out. No attachment, no begging, no "dear sir/madam".

> **Deliverable:** resource outline + five-outlet pitch list + the pitch email. Include both in Project Block 1's "authority plan" section.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting links instead of weighing them.** Three relevant editorial links outrank sixty directory swaps.
- **Link exchange circles.** Reciprocal patterns are easily detected and cancel themselves out.
- **"500 directory submissions for ₹999."** You are buying a penalty, not a presence.
- **Forgetting the brand.** People searching your business name directly is the ranking signal money cannot buy — earned only by being good and being known.

## Key takeaways

- Links transfer trust; the value of a link depends on who, how and where it is placed.
- Earn links with genuinely useful, citable work — then tell the right people it exists.
- Never buy links; the five-question filter rejects every shady offer.
- Off-page SEO includes reviews, brand searches and citations: be known, be findable, be worth recommending.
