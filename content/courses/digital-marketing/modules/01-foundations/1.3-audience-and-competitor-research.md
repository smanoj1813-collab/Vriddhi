# 1.3 Audience and competitor research

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain why imagination-based personas fail and evidence-based personas work.
- Gather audience evidence from free sources: search data, reviews, social listening and simple interviews.
- Run a competitor teardown that finds gaps instead of listing features.
- Turn research into a one-page persona a whole team can actually use.

## Opening scenario

A student team in Mysuru was promoting a campus fitness challenge. Their persona, built in a brainstorm, read: "Rahul, 20, likes fitness and Instagram." Confident, they spent two weeks making gym reels. Sign-ups were flat.

Then they did the unglamorous thing: they stood at the canteen for two evenings and asked 25 students three questions — why they had not signed up, what would make them, and where they look for campus news. The answers were brutal and useful: most did not think they were "fitness people"; the timing clashed with internals; nobody checked Instagram for campus events — announcements travelled on class WhatsApp groups. The team rewrote the message ("7-day starter challenge — no gym needed, 20 minutes a day"), moved sessions after the exams, and posted the plan into the class groups. Sign-ups tripled.

Same audience, same budget of zero. The difference was that the second persona was **built from evidence**. Research is not paperwork before the real work; it is the work that makes everything else land.

## Core ideas

### 1. Evidence beats imagination

An imagined persona is a mirror: it describes the marketer, not the customer. Evidence-based personas are assembled from four free sources:

1. **Search data** — what people type is what they want. Google's autocomplete, "People also ask", related searches, and Google Trends (try comparing "online classes" vs "offline classes" in your state over five years).
2. **Reviews** — the most under-used research goldmine. Read the 1–3 star reviews of competitors: every complaint is a market gap. Read the 5-star ones too: that is the language of delight, in the customer's own words.
3. **Social listening** — comments on competitors' posts, questions in public groups and forums, the captions people use when they tag a category. Count recurring words with your own eyes, not vibes.
4. **Interviews** — five conversations of ten minutes beat a hundred survey responses you never read. Ask about the last time they did the thing, not whether they "would be interested in" a thing (they will say yes to be polite).

### 2. The persona skeleton

Keep it to one page with fields that change decisions:

- **Situation:** the job the customer is trying to get done, in their words.
- **Goals and fears:** what success looks like to them; what they are scared of wasting.
- **Watering holes:** where they actually spend attention (which app, which group, which shop).
- **Triggers and barriers:** what makes them start looking; what stops them buying.
- **Proof that moves them:** reviews, demos, referrals, certificates, price?

Leave out: name, age, photo, favourite colour. Those decorate; they do not decide. If a field does not change what you would write or spend, it is dead weight.

### 3. Competitor teardown: hunt for gaps, not features

Study three to five competitors and score each on what the *customer* experiences:

| Lens | Question you answer |
| --- | --- |
| Offer | What exactly do they sell, at what price, bundled with what? |
| Channels | Where are they present — and where are they visibly absent or weak? |
| Message | What promise do they lead with? What words do they repeat? |
| Proof | Reviews, testimonials, case studies — how strong, how recent, how specific? |
| Content | What do they publish, how often, and does it rank or get engagement? |

Then the two money questions: **What does every competitor say the same way?** (a cliché you can break) and **What does nobody say or do?** (a gap you can own). In the Mysuru example, every fitness page shouted "transform your body"; nobody was speaking to beginners who felt gym culture was not for them. That silence was the opportunity.

### 4. Where the evidence lives — a free toolkit

- **Google Trends:** compare terms over time, by region — Karnataka vs Kerala, city vs district.
- **Autocomplete and "People also ask":** raw customer language, free of charge.
- **Reviews:** Google Maps, Flipkart/Amazon, Justdial, Zomato/Swiggy — filter to the low stars.
- **Meta Ad Library:** every active ad any brand runs, searchable — you can read competitors' paid messaging, not just their public posts.
- **Your own channels:** questions in DMs and comments, sales-call notes, front-desk conversations. Most businesses sit on their best research and never read it.

## Try it now

Open Google, type "wedding photographer" plus your city, and read the autocomplete suggestions. Every completion is a demand signal ("prices", "near me", "with album"). Now read five 2-star reviews of any listed photographer. Note three complaints that appear more than once — congratulations, you have the outline of a better offer.

## Hands-on lab: research dossier for your lab business (45 min)

Continue with the business you mapped in Lesson 1.1.

1. **Audience evidence (15 min):** Collect (a) five autocomplete phrases customers type, (b) three recurring complaints from its (or a close competitor's) reviews, (c) two recurring words of praise.
2. **Competitor teardown (15 min):** Fill the five-lens table for its two closest competitors. Use the Meta Ad Library to see if either runs ads — screenshot one ad per competitor if so.
3. **Persona (10 min):** Write the one-page persona: situation, goals and fears, watering holes, triggers and barriers, proof that moves them. Every line must cite which evidence it came from — "(reviews)" or "(autocomplete)" is enough.
4. **The gap sentence (5 min):** One sentence: "Everyone says ___, nobody says ___, so we will ___."
5. Keep the dossier — Project Blocks 1 and 3 both build on it.

> **Deliverable:** evidence-cited persona + five-lens competitor table + your gap sentence.

## Common mistakes

- **Persona decoration.** Stock photos and "likes cricket" fields do not change campaigns; triggers and barriers do.
- **Researching once.** Audiences move; review the dossier before every campaign, not once a year.
- **Survey theatre.** "Would you use an app for this?" — everyone says yes. Ask about the last real instance instead.
- **Competitor envy.** The goal is not to copy the leader's features but to find what all of them are missing.

## Key takeaways

- Personas built from search data, reviews, listening and interviews beat imagined ones every time.
- A useful persona changes decisions: situation, goals and fears, watering holes, triggers and barriers, proof.
- Competitor teardown hunts for clichés to break and gaps to own — not features to copy.
- Free tools (Trends, autocomplete, reviews, Ad Library) already expose most of what you need.

## Reflection journal

Write 150–200 words: which of the four evidence sources felt most surprising to use, and what did it tell you that you would never have guessed? What will you do differently the next time someone says "our customer is everyone"?

## Going further (optional)

- Explore the Meta Ad Library for a national brand and a local brand in the same category. How do their ad strategies differ?
- Read three 1-star reviews of a product you love. Notice how quickly you can draft the positioning for a competitor that fixes those three things.
