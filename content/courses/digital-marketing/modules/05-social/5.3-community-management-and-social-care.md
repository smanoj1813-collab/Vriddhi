# 5.3 Community management and social care

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Treat comments and DMs as the actual product of social media, not a chore beside it.
- Use a reply matrix to respond with the right speed, tone and action.
- Handle negative comments publicly and kindly, with clear escalation rules.
- Build community rituals that turn customers into repeat participants.

## Opening scenario

Two identical restaurants in Mysuru, one busy Saturday night. At the first, a diner posted: "Waited 40 minutes, food cold, staff rude." The restaurant's page said nothing for two days, then posted a festival special. At the second, a similar complaint appeared; within an hour the page replied publicly: "So sorry, Vinay — that is not our standard. Please WhatsApp us at [number] with your table number so the manager can make it right." The complaint resolved privately; the public reply stayed, and it was doing quiet work: every future diner scanning reviews could see a business that answers.

Same problem, opposite outcomes. The first restaurant treated the comment as an embarrassment to ignore. The second understood the arithmetic of public replies: **you are never replying to one person — you are performing for the hundreds who will read it**. Community management is customer service conducted on a stage.

## Core ideas

### 1. Comments are the product

Organic social media's deepest value is not reach — it is **conversation**, which platforms reward with distribution and customers reward with trust. The practical commitments:

- **Reply to every comment** where scale allows; batch DMs into the daily engagement slot (Lesson 5.2).
- **Reply fast in DMs** — the industry's honest rule of thumb is within hours, not days. An unanswered DM after a "DM us for orders" post is a shop with a closed shutter.
- **Ask questions back** — "which flavour would you pick?" — because every answer is both engagement signal and market research.
- **Pin the best exchanges** — a pinned comment of a delighted customer outsells your best caption.

### 2. The reply matrix

Not all messages deserve the same response. Two axes — **sentiment** (positive/neutral/negative) and **type** (question / praise / complaint / spam) — give a matrix with pre-decided actions:

| Type | Public reply | Private action | Speed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Question about product/price | Answer fully, cheerfully | — | Hours |
| Praise | Thank personally, add a detail | Occasional surprise thank-you | Same day |
| Complaint, genuine | Acknowledge + take it private with a named next step | Resolve; then one-line public closure if appropriate | Within 1–2 hours |
| Troll / bad-faith | One calm factual reply (for the audience), then disengage | Hide/block if abusive | Once |
| Spam / scam | Hide, report | Never argue | Daily sweep |

Pre-deciding these removes the two failure modes: the panicked silence and the 2 a.m. angry reply.

### 3. The anatomy of a public complaint reply

The four parts, in order:

1. **Acknowledge the person by name** — "So sorry, Vinay" — because the other readers see a person, not a policy.
2. **Own the standard** — "that is not our standard" — without excuses ("we were busy that night" reads as blaming the customer for coming).
3. **Move to resolution privately** — a specific channel and a named actor ("WhatsApp the manager, Rahul").
4. **Follow through**, because a graceful reply over an unresolved problem is worse than no reply.

What is never in the reply: arguing facts in public, blaming staff by name, legal threats, sarcasm. The audience does not adjudicate your bad night; they only watch how you handle it.

### 4. Escalation rules — written before the crisis

Decide in advance, in one page: who may reply (and who may not), which topics go to the owner/legal immediately (safety, refunds above ₹X, allegations, media), what the NEVER list contains, and the after-hours protocol. A crisis is the wrong time to discover that three interns all believe they have authority to apologise on the brand's behalf. The one-page escalation card is the cheapest crisis insurance available.

### 5. Community rituals

Beyond service, community is built with **repeatable rituals** — the Friday poll ("this week's special: vote"), the monthly customer spotlight, the "regulars' wall", the festival UGC call ("tag us in your celebration photos; best one gets a voucher"), the predictable live Q&A hour. Rituals give people a reason to return and a shape to belong to; they also generate the UGC and social proof that Modules 3 and 6 feed on. Small, repeated, genuine — that is the entire formula.

## Try it now

Find a local business's most recent 1-star Google or Instagram review and its reply (or its silence). Score the reply against the four-part anatomy. Now write the reply they should have posted. Keep it — this is a portfolio skill and an interview favourite.

## Hands-on lab: the social care kit (45 min)

1. **Reply matrix (10 min):** Fill the matrix for your lab business — one example message per cell, and the exact public reply you would post for each.
2. **The complaint drill (15 min):** Write full public replies to three scenarios: (a) "waited 40 minutes, food cold", (b) "your prices increased, this is cheating", (c) "the product arrived damaged, no one is responding". Four parts each: name, standard, private channel + named actor, follow-through.
3. **Escalation card (10 min):** The one-pager: who replies, the immediate-escalation topics, the NEVER list, after-hours protocol.
4. **Ritual plan (10 min):** Design two repeatable rituals for your community — cadence, format, what you hope to learn from each. Write the first instance of one ritual (e.g., this Friday's poll question and options).

> **Deliverable:** completed reply matrix + three drilled complaint replies + escalation card + two rituals with their first instances. Goes into Project Block 3 as the operations annex.

## Common mistakes

- **Broadcast-only accounts.** Publishing without replying trains customers to stop talking to you.
- **Deleting genuine complaints.** Screenshots outlive deletions; the deletion becomes the story.
- **Arguing in public.** You cannot win; the audience only remembers the fight.
- **The unowned apology.** Three people replying with three different tones (or offers) turns service into chaos.

## Key takeaways

- Comments and DMs are the product: reply to everything, fast, and with questions that invite answers.
- The reply matrix pre-decides tone, action and speed by message type — no panicked silences, no angry midnights.
- Complaint replies have four parts: name, owned standard, private resolution channel, follow-through — performed for the audience, not the complainant.
- Escalation rules written before the crisis, and small repeated rituals, build communities that outlive any single campaign.
