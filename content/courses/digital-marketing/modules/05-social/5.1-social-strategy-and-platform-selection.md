# 5.1 Social strategy and platform selection

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain why "be everywhere" is a strategy for failing everywhere.
- Choose platforms from audience evidence and define each channel's specific job.
- Write a social strategy one-liner that passes the same test as a content strategy.
- Set social KPIs that connect to business outcomes, not applause.

## Opening scenario

A Kochi home-bakery was on five platforms. Instagram (last post 3 weeks ago), Facebook (festival greetings only), YouTube (two videos, both vertical and sideways), LinkedIn ("We are pleased to announce we exist"), Pinterest (password lost). The owner's summary: "Social media doesn't work for cakes."

A student cut it to two platforms. Instagram — because the audience research showed 70% of local cake searches on mobile started with Instagram or Google Maps, and cakes are visual. WhatsApp Business — because every single enquiry the bakery had ever received ended in a WhatsApp conversation anyway. The strategy one-liner: "Instagram shows the cakes; WhatsApp closes them; everything else waits until these two are boring." Three months later, orders came through Instagram DMs → WhatsApp, tracked by a simple "How did you hear about us?" question. The other three platforms? Not missed once.

Social strategy is mostly the discipline of **choosing what not to do**.

## Core ideas

### 1. Why "be everywhere" fails

Three forces make five-platform presence a losing bet for any small team:

1. **Attention fragmentation.** Each platform has its own grammar — what works on Instagram dies on LinkedIn. Five grammars, five content pipelines, five communities to answer.
2. **The recency penalty.** Platforms reward consistent presence and quietly bury accounts that post sporadically. Five half-alive accounts lose to one thriving one — customers check your last post date before they check your menu.
3. **Effort arithmetic.** One good reel costs half a day across ideation, shoot, edit and reply. The team that does four platforms does them all at 25%.

The correct question is never "which platforms should we be on?" It is "**where do our customers already make decisions like this?**" — answerable only with the 1.3 research: where the audience is, and where the purchase behaviour actually happens.

### 2. The platform jobs (2026, India-focused)

- **Instagram (Reels, carousels, Stories):** the discovery engine for visual categories — food, fashion, decor, events. Stories carry polls and countdowns; DMs are the conversion path.
- **WhatsApp Business:** not "social media" in the broadcast sense but India's actual closing channel — catalogue, broadcast lists (with consent), labels, quick replies. Where Indian small-business money actually changes hands.
- **YouTube:** the second-largest search engine; long-shelf-life explainers and reviews. Slower to grow, harder to kill. Strong for education, how-tos, trust-building.
- **LinkedIn:** B2B and careers. For a bakery: nothing. For a recruitment firm, a staffing brand, a B2B SaaS: everything.
- **Facebook:** still the strongest for 35+ audiences, local groups, and events in much of India — and Marketplace for categories like furniture and services.
- **Regional/newer platforms:** follow your audience research, not the tech press.

The pattern: platform choice = **audience evidence + category fit + your honest capacity**. Two platforms done properly beat five done nominally.

### 3. The social strategy one-liner

Same discipline as Lesson 3.1's content strategy. Fill this:

> "We will grow **[platform]** for **[audience]** by consistently publishing **[content pillars]**, converting attention in **[conversion path: DM / WhatsApp / link]**, measured by **[2–3 KPIs tied to the 1.4 goal]** — and we will ignore everything else until this works."

The "ignore everything else" clause is the strategy. Without it, every new platform announcement becomes a distraction budget item.

### 4. Social KPIs: beyond applause

Likes are applause metrics — pleasant, rarely decisive. The ladder from vanity to value:

| Tier | Metric | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vanity | Likes, followers | Popularity (weakly) |
| Engagement | Saves, shares, comments, DM replies | Did the content earn real attention? |
| Traffic/action | Link clicks, profile visits, direction requests | Did attention become intent? |
| Business | Qualified DMs, WhatsApp conversations, enquiries, orders | Did intent become money? |

Set one or two metrics per tier, connect them to the KPI tree from Lesson 1.4, and ask the attribution question ("how did you hear about us?") at every conversion. A 900-follower account that generates twenty WhatsApp conversations a month is outperforming a 90,000-follower account that generates none.

## Try it now

Open three local businesses in your category on any platform. For each, note: last post date, follower count, and whether their bio tells you what to do next (DM? WhatsApp? Order link?). You can already rank their social strategies — you have just proven strategy is visible from the outside.

## Hands-on lab: the platform decision (45 min)

For your lab business, make and defend the platform call.

1. **Evidence pull (10 min):** From your 1.3 dossier: where does the audience spend attention? Where do decisions in this category happen? Which platforms do the two best competitors actually work (posting frequency, replies, engagement)?
2. **The shortlist (10 min):** Score three candidate platforms against audience evidence, category fit (is the product/service visual? high-trust? B2B?) and your capacity (hours per week). One score sheet, three columns.
3. **The pick + jobs (10 min):** Choose your primary and (if justified) secondary platform. Write each one's job in one line: what it publishes, and the conversion path (e.g., "Instagram: Reels + carousel proof → DM → WhatsApp").
4. **The one-liner (10 min):** Write the strategy sentence with pillars, audience, conversion path and KPIs. Test it on a classmate: can they repeat it back? If not, it is not finished.
5. **The stop-list (5 min):** Name the platforms you are consciously ignoring and the condition under which you would revisit (e.g., "LinkedIn when we start B2B catering").

> **Deliverable:** platform score sheet + jobs + strategy one-liner + stop-list. Front page of Project Block 3's social section.

## Common mistakes

- **Presence theatre.** Five abandoned profiles impress nobody; one active one does.
- **Platform envy.** Choosing TikTok-style trends when your customers are in Facebook groups and WhatsApp lists.
- **No conversion path.** Publishing content with no defined next step — attention leaks away with nowhere to go.
- **Follower goals.** Bought followers corrupt your engagement rate and your self-knowledge; the algorithm then hides you from real humans.

## Key takeaways

- "Be everywhere" fails by grammar, recency and effort arithmetic; choose one or two platforms from audience evidence.
- Every platform has a job description: Instagram discovers, WhatsApp closes, YouTube explains, LinkedIn does B2B, Facebook reaches 35+ local.
- The strategy one-liner includes what you will ignore — that clause is the strategy.
- Measure the ladder: saves and shares over likes, conversations and orders over followers.

Write 150–200 words: audit one brand you follow on two platforms. How does its content differ by platform grammar — or does it copy-paste? Which platform is doing the real business work, and how can you tell? What would you tell its social manager to stop doing?
