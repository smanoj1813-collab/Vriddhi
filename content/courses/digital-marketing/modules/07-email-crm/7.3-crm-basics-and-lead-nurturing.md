# 7.3 CRM basics and lead nurturing

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain what a CRM is for, in terms of the problems it solves.
- Design a lightweight lead-management pipeline with stages that mirror reality.
- Build the lead-scoring and follow-up discipline that stops enquiries rotting.
- Choose the right CRM size for a small business — and resist over-tooling.

## Opening scenario

A tuition centre in Mangaluru ran admissions through a WhatsApp group, a diary, and the coordinator's memory. Enquiries arrived from ads, Instagram, walk-ins and referrals — then vanished. The owner's estimate: "We follow up with everyone." The evidence, once tracked: of 240 enquiries in a term, 96 got a callback. One hundred and forty-four people who raised their hands were never contacted again.

No channel problem, no creative problem — a **memory problem**. A CRM (Customer Relationship Management system) is simply a shared memory with discipline attached: every enquiry recorded, every conversation logged, every next step owned by someone with a date. This lesson builds that system at small-business scale.

## Core ideas

### 1. What a CRM is, honestly

Strip the enterprise vocabulary: a CRM is a **list of people with a status, a history, and a next action.** Columns like Name, Phone, Source, Stage, Value, Last touch, Next step, Owner. Everything else — dashboards, scoring, integrations — is decoration on those bones.

The problems it solves: enquiries lost in inboxes and DMs, follow-ups that depend on memory, the "did anyone call them?" mystery, and the owner as single point of failure. It is the manual layer from Lesson 6.3, systematised.

### 2. The pipeline: stages that mirror reality

A pipeline is the sequence of stages every lead travels. Design rule: **stages must match how the business actually thinks**, not a textbook. For the tuition centre:

```
New enquiry → Contacted → Qualified → Demo booked → Negotiating → Enrolled / Lost (reason)
```

Two disciplines make it work:

- **Stage definitions in writing.** "Qualified" = spoke to a parent AND the child's batch need is clear AND fee range accepted. Without definitions, the pipeline lies.
- **Lost-with-reason.** Every lost lead carries a reason (fee, timing, competitor, distance, no reason). After a term, the reason list is the marketing department's feedback loop — if "fee" dominates, the offer design is wrong, not the ads.

### 3. Lead scoring: attention where it pays

Not all leads deserve equal pursuit. A simple score, computed in a sheet or the CRM:

| Signal | Points |
|---|---|
| Source: referral or direct (brand search) | +20 |
| Source: organic content | +10 |
| Source: paid social | +5 |
| Opened 2+ nurture emails | +10 |
| Clicked pricing/demo page | +20 |
| Asked "when can we start?" in any channel | +25 |
| Demanded heavy discount immediately | −10 |
| Unreachable after 2 attempts | −20 |

Rules, not intuition: **A-leads (≥60): personal call within 24 hours. B-leads (30–59): nurture + weekly touch. C-leads (<30): automated nurture only.** The scoring table is reviewed monthly against who actually enrolled — Module 6's loop again: read, hypothesise, adjust weights, log.

### 4. The follow-up discipline

The research on follow-up is brutal and consistent: most enquiries that convert do so after the SECOND or later contact, yet most businesses stop after one. The discipline is mechanical:

- **Speed-to-first-touch:** first response within the hour during work hours; a template auto-reply at night ("We'll call you before 10 a.m.").
- **Cadence with an end:** day 0 call, day 1 WhatsApp, day 3 call + case study, day 7 email, day 14 final "should I close your file?" — then Lost-with-reason.
- **Multi-channel but one memory:** calls, WhatsApp, email all logged in the CRM record — the next person to touch the lead sees the whole story.
- **The 24-hour rule for A-leads:** speed beats polish; a decent call today outsells a perfect call Friday.

### 5. Tooling at the right size

- **Stage 0 (no budget, <50 leads/month):** a Google Sheet with pipeline columns, a status colour code, and a weekly review ritual. Honest, visible, free.
- **Stage 1 (small budget, a team of 2–10):** a lightweight CRM — Zoho Bigin (built for Indian SMBs), Freshsales, HubSpot free tier. WhatsApp integration matters in India; choose accordingly.
- **Stage 2+ (real sales team, product tours):** full-featured suites — only when the process has outgrown Stage 1, never before. Buying Stage 2 tooling for a Stage 0 process produces an expensive spreadsheet.

The resistance principle: the CRM should take LESS time than the diary it replaces, or it will be abandoned within a month. Adoption beats features.

## Try it now

Count your own "open loops" — pending replies, un-answered messages, half-made plans. Which CRM fields would capture them? You already run a CRM in your head; this lesson just externalises it.

## Hands-on lab: the lead machine (45 min)

1. **Pipeline design (10 min):** For your PB3 business, write the pipeline stages with a one-line definition each, plus the lost-reason picklist (6–8 reasons).
2. **The sheet (15 min):** Build the Stage-0 CRM in Google Sheets: columns Name, Phone, Source, Stage, Score, Value, Last touch, Next step + date, Owner, Notes. Add data-validation dropdowns for Stage and Source. Enter 5 realistic sample leads (vary sources and stages).
3. **Scoring (10 min):** Write your scoring table (8–10 signals, weights) and compute scores for the 5 sample leads; mark A/B/C and the required next action for each with a date.
4. **Cadence design (10 min):** Write the follow-up sequence (channel + day + asset for each touch, ending in the close-file message). Note the two metrics you would review weekly: speed-to-first-touch and stage-conversion rates.

> **Deliverable:** Pipeline with definitions + working sheet link + scoring table with scored samples + cadence. The capstone asks for this exact machine, wired to the campaign's enquiry flow.

## Common mistakes

- **The lying pipeline.** Undefined stages let everyone interpret "qualified" their way — definitions in writing or it's fiction.
- **Follow-up of one.** One attempt, then silence — while competitors take three.
- **Tooling as a substitute for discipline.** A ₹2,000/month CRM without a weekly review ritual is a slower spreadsheet.
- **No lost-reasons.** Losing quietly, a hundred times a term, with the pattern invisible.

## Key takeaways

- A CRM is a shared memory: person, status, history, next action with an owner and date.
- Stages must mirror how the business thinks; define each in writing and record every loss with a reason.
- Score leads with rules, route A-leads to a 24-hour human, and cadence with an end.
- Start with a sheet; buy tools when the process outgrows it — adoption beats features.
