# 7.4 Consent and privacy in marketing

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain the legal backbone of digital consent: DPDP Act 2023, GDPR principles, and CAN-SPAM essentials.
- Distinguish consent types, and build compliant signup, unsubscribe and data-request flows.
- Draft privacy and cookie notices that a small business can actually implement.
- Recognise the seven consent failures that most commonly sink young marketers.

## Opening scenario

A Bengaluru event-management startup growth-hacked its way to 40,000 "subscribers" — scraped from public WhatsApp groups, bought in part, collected from a form whose tiny text said "you agree to receive promotions from our partners." The first big campaign landed 2% opens, 900 unsubscribes, and a spam-complaint rate that got their domain throttled. Their next launch announcement never reached the inbox of anyone.

The shortcuts that built the list destroyed the channel — and in India, since the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP)**, they also carry statutory obligations. This lesson is the compliance layer under everything in this course: the ads, the pixels, the email journeys, the CRM.

## Core ideas

### 1. The legal backbone, in plain terms

- **India — DPDP Act 2023:** processes personal data with **notice and consent** for specified purposes; grants rights to access, correct and erase (**nomination** of heirs, grievance officer, penalties for breaches). Data may only be used for the purpose consented to — the "we'll also text them about anything" habit is now a liability, not a growth hack.
- **GDPR (EU/UK visitors):** lawful basis (consent or legitimate interest), purpose limitation, data minimisation, right to access/erasure, 72-hour breach reporting. If your site serves EU users, GDPR follows the user, not your office.
- **CAN-SPAM (US email):** honest headers/subject, physical postal address, working unsubscribe within 10 days, no harvested addresses.
- **Platform and mailbox rules:** Google/Yahoo bulk-sender requirements (authentication, one-click unsubscribe, spam rates under 0.3%) and advertising policies on personalised targeting effectively enforce stricter standards than many laws.

The practical takeaway: **build to the strictest common denominator — clear notice, opt-in consent, easy exit, purpose-limited use — and you are compliant almost everywhere.**

### 2. Consent types and what each permits

| Type | How obtained | What it permits | Marketing quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Explicit/opt-in** | Unticked box, form signup, double confirmation | Marketing to that purpose | Gold |
| **Implied** | Business necessity (order confirmations), soft opt-in for existing customers on similar services | Transactional/similar messages | Careful territory |
| **Bundled** | "Accept everything or leave" | Legally fragile; consent must be freely given and specific | Avoid |
| **Bought/scraped** | Third parties, harvesting | Nothing — toxic everywhere | Never |

Two extra rules: consent is **withdrawable** (unsubscribe must work, instantly, one click) and **recorded** (when, how, what text they saw — your email tool keeps this; a scraped list cannot prove any of it).

### 3. The compliant flows, built small

- **Signup:** a clear statement of what they will receive and how often, alongside an unchecked box (India/EU style). "Weekly offers + first dibs on workshops. No more than 2 emails a week. Unsubscribe anytime."
- **Welcome/double opt-in:** the confirmation email (Lesson 7.1) doubles as consent evidence.
- **Unsubscribe:** one click, honoured immediately, present in EVERY email (also a legal requirement under DPDP-era practice and mailbox rules). Do not force logins or "tell us why" walls before the exit works.
- **Data requests:** a named contact (hello@ / grievance officer for bigger firms) that can export, correct, or delete a person's data within a reasonable time. A spreadsheet and 48 hours fully satisfies a small business's duty.
- **Cookie/consent banner:** for sites using analytics/ads pixels: equal Accept / Reject choice, linked to a plain-language notice. Reject must genuinely stop the marketing cookies (analytics-only consent mode is the middle path for GA4 — Lesson 6.2's honesty clause, now explained).

### 4. The privacy notice a small business can actually post

The five-sentence structure: (1) who we are and what we collect (name, contact, enquiry details); (2) why (to respond to you and — only with your consent — send offers); (3) who else sees it (our email tool, our payment gateway — named); (4) how long and how to erase (write to hello@ and we delete within 30 days); (5) how to withdraw consent (unsubscribe link, always). No 4,000-word document nobody reads — a notice people can read is a notice that protects.

### 5. The seven failure modes

1. **Bundled consent** ("terms include marketing from partners") — invalid and channel-killing.
2. **Pre-ticked boxes** — consent must be a positive action.
3. **The working unsubscribe that doesn't work** — logins, slow processing, "please call us."
4. **Purpose creep** — collecting for orders, using for unrelated promotions.
5. **No records** — unable to show when/how consent was given.
6. **Scraped/bought lists** — unprovable consent plus spam traps.
7. **The invisible grievance route** — no named contact for access/erasure requests.

Each is avoidable with the flows above — and each, un-avoided, can erase a young marketer's results in one campaign (as the opening scenario showed).

## Try it now

Open three websites you use. Find each privacy notice and consent banner in under 30 seconds each. If you can't, you have experienced the compliance gap this lesson exists to close — and you now know what their marketers should have built.

## Hands-on lab: the compliance layer (45 min)

1. **Consent audit (15 min):** Take your PB3 business (or a real one you know). Audit against the seven failure modes: for each, write "clear / at risk / failing" with one line of evidence.
2. **The signup rewrite (10 min):** Write the compliant signup block: promise line, frequency line, unchecked box label, and the double opt-in welcome snippet that records consent.
3. **The privacy notice (10 min):** Draft the five-sentence notice with real details (name the email tool, the payment gateway, the hello@ address, the 30-day deletion commitment).
4. **The data-request runbook (10 min):** Write the procedure: request arrives → identity check → export/correct/delete within X days → confirmation reply. Add the log header (date, request, action, completed). One page, owner named.

> **Deliverable:** Seven-point audit + signup copy + five-sentence notice + runbook. The capstone requires the consent layer alongside the campaign.

## Common mistakes

- **"We're small, it doesn't apply."** DPDP applies to processing personal data in India — scale changes penalty risk, not principle.
- **Compliance theatre.** A 4,000-word policy nobody reads protects nobody; readable notices do.
- **Consent as a one-time event.** It is a standing capability: withdraw, export, erase, on demand.
- **Forgetting the platform layer.** Mailbox rules and ad policies bite faster than regulators — 0.3% spam rates throttle domains in days.

## Key takeaways

- Build to the strictest common denominator: notice, opt-in consent, easy exit, purpose limitation — compliant nearly everywhere.
- DPDP Act 2023 gives Indians enforceable rights over their data; record consent and honour withdrawal instantly.
- Explicit opt-in is gold; bundled, pre-ticked and bought consent are fragile or toxic.
- A readable five-sentence notice plus a working data-request runbook is full compliance for a small business.
