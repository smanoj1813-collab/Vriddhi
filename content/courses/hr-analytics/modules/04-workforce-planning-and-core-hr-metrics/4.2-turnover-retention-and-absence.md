# 4.2 Turnover, retention and absence

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Calculate workforce rates with explicit periods, eligible populations and limitations.
- Produce a reviewable turnover and absence metric card using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

A manager divides annual exits by closing headcount and compares it with another department’s monthly figure. An absence chart counts leave days without showing scheduled workdays. Both comparisons risk assigning meaning to inconsistent denominators.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

## Core ideas

### 1. Define turnover and retention independently

This course’s basic period turnover rate is exits during the period divided by average headcount, with opening/closing average used only when appropriate and disclosed. Voluntary and involuntary exits should be separated when relevant. Retention follows an opening cohort and counts how many remain at the end. It is not automatically one minus turnover when hires and different denominators are involved. Avoid casually annualising a volatile short period.

### 2. Use scheduled exposure for absence

An absence rate can use absent scheduled hours divided by total scheduled hours in the period. Define which absence types are included and how part-time schedules and approved leave are treated. Days may be appropriate under consistent work patterns but hours can be more comparable when schedules differ. Do not infer misconduct or health status from aggregate absence. Sensitive details require authorised handling.

### 3. Interpret rates with counts and context

Small groups can show large percentage swings from one event. Show counts, denominators and comparable periods, applying disclosure safeguards. Seasonal work, tenure mix and organisational changes can affect rates. A metric can trigger supportive investigation without identifying an individual as a problem. Record limitations and consult HR professionals before decisions affecting employment, health or accommodations.

## Worked example

Opening headcount is 100 and closing 120, giving an approximate average of 110. Eleven exits yield 10% turnover for that period. If 92 of the original 100 remain, opening-cohort retention is 92%, not 90%. In an absence example, 80 absent scheduled hours out of 2,000 scheduled hours equals 4%; the treatment of approved leave must be stated.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming retention always equals one minus turnover.
- Comparing monthly and annual rates as if equivalent.
- Using absence as a medical or disciplinary diagnosis.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Define turnover and retention independently:** This course’s basic period turnover rate is exits during the period divided by average headcount, with opening/closing average used only when appropriate and disclosed.
- **Use scheduled exposure for absence:** An absence rate can use absent scheduled hours divided by total scheduled hours in the period.
- **Interpret rates with counts and context:** Small groups can show large percentage swings from one event.
