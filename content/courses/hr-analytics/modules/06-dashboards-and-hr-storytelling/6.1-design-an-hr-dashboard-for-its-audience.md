# 6.1 Design an HR dashboard for its audience

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Choose dashboard measures and visual encodings based on a decision, not on the number of available fields.
- Produce a reviewable dashboard wireframe and metric map using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The HR Executive needs an action queue, the recruitment lead needs funnel bottlenecks, and a senior sponsor needs a small set of workforce decisions. One dense dashboard cannot serve all three equally well. Begin with audience and purpose.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Use a small decision-led metric set

For each audience, identify the recurring question, available action, cadence and sensitivity level. Select a handful of metrics with owners and definitions. An operational view may show open requisitions needing feedback; an executive view may show workforce gaps and trend context. More charts do not automatically provide more insight. Keep drill-down access separate from broadly shared summaries.

### 2. Choose appropriate visual forms

Use bars for category comparisons, lines for time trends and tables for exact values and action lists. Show denominators and periods beside rates. Avoid three-dimensional effects, dual axes without clear justification and truncated bar axes that exaggerate differences. Use accessible contrast and labels rather than colour alone to communicate status. A chart title should explain the measured question, not just name the tool.

### 3. Pair every signal with context

Display refresh time, source, definition link, filters and limitations. A threshold needs a rationale and an owner; red is not an explanation. Include a note describing what the viewer should do if the metric crosses a trigger. Prevent individual identification through tiny filtered groups. Design the wireframe before building so the team can challenge the information hierarchy without being distracted by software.

## Worked example

A recruitment view might show open requisitions by age band, offer acceptance for a mature cohort, and a table of pending interviewer feedback. Each serves a different action. A headcount trend without joiner and exit context may raise questions but cannot explain movement. A five-chart mock-up with explicit decisions is more useful than twenty unrelated numbers.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: dashboard wireframe and metric map (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Choose HR Executive, HR Analyst or Talent Acquisition Associate as the primary audience.

2. Write three recurring decisions and map one metric to each.

3. Sketch cards, a trend chart and an action table with labels and denominators.

4. Add refresh, filter and small-group disclosure notes.

5. Ask a peer to identify the required action without your narration and revise the wireframe.

**Deliverable:** submit your dashboard wireframe and metric map, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Starting from every field available instead of an audience question.
- Using colour alone to signal meaning.
- Hiding denominator and refresh information.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Use a small decision-led metric set:** For each audience, identify the recurring question, available action, cadence and sensitivity level.
- **Choose appropriate visual forms:** Use bars for category comparisons, lines for time trends and tables for exact values and action lists.
- **Pair every signal with context:** Display refresh time, source, definition link, filters and limitations.

## Reflection journal

1. What did you change after checking the example or receiving a review comment? Name the exact input, statement or calculation.
2. Which assumption has the greatest effect on your recommendation, and how would you test it before a real decision?
3. How would you explain this work in a two-minute interview for HR Executive without claiming a classroom simulation was paid experience?

Write 80–120 words in English, Kannada or Hindi with clear technical labels. Keep a specific correction or decision in your portfolio journal.
