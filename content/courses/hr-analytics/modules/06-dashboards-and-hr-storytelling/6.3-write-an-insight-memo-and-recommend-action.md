# 6.3 Write an insight memo and recommend action

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Communicate a people-analytics finding with evidence, uncertainty, alternatives and a proportionate next step.
- Produce a reviewable one-page HR insight memo using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

A dashboard shows slower hiring in one department. The sponsor asks for a recommendation before tomorrow’s meeting. A useful memo should not jump from a chart to blame; it should explain what is known, what remains uncertain and which action is justified now.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Separate observation from interpretation

State the metric, period, population and comparison first. Then identify plausible explanations and which evidence supports each. Use language such as “associated with” when causation is not established. Avoid implying an individual caused an aggregate pattern. A memo can recommend checking missing timestamps or interviewing process owners before changing policy. Good analysis sometimes narrows the question rather than delivering an instant answer.

### 2. Quantify the practical difference

Show absolute counts and relevant differences, not only relative percentages. A rate rising from 2% to 4% doubles but changes by two percentage points; the practical significance depends on denominator, impact and context. Round consistently without obscuring small samples. Explain whether a difference is stable across periods or driven by a few cases. Do not equate statistical language with certainty.

### 3. Make the recommendation reviewable

Provide options, expected benefit, risk, owner, timeline and success measure. Prefer a bounded process pilot when evidence is preliminary. Include a balancing measure for unintended effects, such as candidate experience alongside faster hiring. State what evidence would cause the organisation to stop or revise the action. Keep the memo accessible to a non-technical HR manager and link detailed calculations separately.

## Worked example

A late-feedback rate rises from 2 of 100 interviews to 4 of 100. That is a two-percentage-point increase and a 100% relative increase, but only two additional events. A proportionate recommendation is to inspect the four cases and test a reminder workflow, not declare a systemic staffing failure. If role mix or timestamp capture changed, investigate that before attributing the rise to behaviour.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: one-page HR insight memo (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Choose one dashboard finding and write its observation with period and denominator.

2. Calculate absolute and relative change for the late-feedback example.

3. List two plausible explanations and evidence needed to distinguish them.

4. Compare a process pilot with doing nothing and with a larger intervention.

5. Write a one-page memo with owner, review date, success measure and stop condition.

**Deliverable:** submit your one-page HR insight memo, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Using a dramatic relative change without absolute counts.
- Treating a plausible explanation as a proven cause.
- Recommending a permanent policy from a tiny preliminary sample.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Separate observation from interpretation:** State the metric, period, population and comparison first.
- **Quantify the practical difference:** Show absolute counts and relevant differences, not only relative percentages.
- **Make the recommendation reviewable:** Provide options, expected benefit, risk, owner, timeline and success measure.

## Reflection journal

1. What did you change after checking the example or receiving a review comment? Name the exact input, statement or calculation.
2. Which assumption has the greatest effect on your recommendation, and how would you test it before a real decision?
3. How would you explain this work in a two-minute interview for HR Executive without claiming a classroom simulation was paid experience?

Write 80–120 words in English, Kannada or Hindi with clear technical labels. Keep a specific correction or decision in your portfolio journal.
