# 3.3 Structured selection and candidate experience

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Use job-related scorecards and process evidence while avoiding unsupported candidate ranking.
- Produce a reviewable selection scorecard and experience audit using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

Two interviewers assess support-role candidates with different questions. One gives a low score for “not a culture fit” without examples. Candidates receive no update for two weeks. The recruitment analyst can improve process consistency without automating who should be hired.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Define job-related evidence

Start with the role’s actual tasks and essential competencies. Use a structured scorecard with observable behavioural anchors, consistent core questions and a relevant work sample where appropriate. Vague labels invite inconsistent judgement and bias. An analyst can monitor score completeness and agreement, but a numeric score is not inherently objective. Review accommodations and accessibility through the authorised HR process.

### 2. Calibrate and document judgement

Interviewers should record evidence before group discussion to reduce anchoring on a senior person’s opinion. Calibration compares interpretations of scoring anchors, not a requirement that everyone gives identical scores. Investigate large disagreements using the evidence and job requirements. Do not infer protected or sensitive characteristics from names, photographs or social-media proxies. Automated ranking is outside this beginner course’s practice.

### 3. Measure candidate experience responsibly

Useful measures include response timeliness, withdrawal reasons and voluntary feedback with a defined denominator. Survey respondents may differ from nonrespondents. Separate process feedback from selection decisions so criticism does not penalise a candidate. Use aggregate reporting and avoid revealing identifiable free-text comments. Improvement could mean a clear update schedule or accessible interview instructions, not merely higher satisfaction scores.

## Worked example

On a 1–5 anchored communication scale, one interviewer scores a work sample 2 and another 4. The correct first step is to compare evidence against the anchors, not automatically average to 3 and call it objective. If 18 of 30 invited candidates answer a feedback survey, response rate is 60%; satisfaction among those 18 does not necessarily represent all 30.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: selection scorecard and experience audit (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Define three job-related competencies for an entry-level support role.

2. Write behavioural anchors for low, adequate and strong performance on one competency.

3. Score a fictional work sample independently, then reconcile disagreement using evidence.

4. Calculate the feedback response rate and record nonresponse limitations.

5. Propose a candidate-update standard and an aggregate monitoring measure.

**Deliverable:** submit your selection scorecard and experience audit, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Using “culture fit” without job-related evidence.
- Assuming an average removes interviewer bias.
- Linking negative process feedback to an adverse selection decision.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Define job-related evidence:** Start with the role’s actual tasks and essential competencies.
- **Calibrate and document judgement:** Interviewers should record evidence before group discussion to reduce anchoring on a senior person’s opinion.
- **Measure candidate experience responsibly:** Useful measures include response timeliness, withdrawal reasons and voluntary feedback with a defined denominator.
