# 2.3 Pivots, distributions and analytical notes

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Summarise HR data with suitable distributions and write a note that preserves context and limitations.
- Produce a reviewable pivot analysis and insight note using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

Five filled requisitions took 10, 12, 14, 16 and 60 days. A manager wants a single number and another wants the slow case removed. The analyst must describe typical experience while keeping the difficult case visible.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Mean and median answer different questions

The mean uses every value and is affected by extreme observations. The median is the middle ordered value and can better describe a typical case in a skewed distribution. Neither is universally superior. Show sample size and, where useful, the range or a percentile. Investigate an extreme value before deciding it is erroneous; a genuine difficult vacancy may be operationally important.

### 2. Segment with a purpose

Pivots can compare department, role, source or period, but groups must use consistent definitions. Avoid slicing until every cell has one person. Small samples create unstable rates and disclosure risks. Distinguish a mix shift from within-group change: overall time-to-fill can rise because the organisation hired more hard-to-fill roles even if each role category improved. Keep the decision question central.

### 3. Write observations before explanations

An analytical note should separate the observed result, plausible explanations, checks still needed and recommended action. “Median is fourteen days across five cases” is an observation; “the recruiter is inefficient” is an unsupported attribution. Use cautious language for associations and specify the evidence needed to investigate. A recommendation can be to collect better data rather than make an immediate personnel decision.

## Worked example

The five durations sum to 112 days, so mean time is 22.4 days. The median is 14 days and the range is 10–60. Removing the 60-day case changes the mean to 13 days but also changes the population. Unless it is demonstrably invalid or outside the agreed scope, keep it and explain why it differs; compare both summaries transparently if useful.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: pivot analysis and insight note (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Create a pivot showing filled requisition counts and durations by role group.

2. Compute mean, median and range for the five-case example.

3. Investigate a synthetic explanation for the 60-day case without deleting it automatically.

4. Compare an overall summary with segmented summaries and note sample sizes.

5. Write a six-sentence note separating observation, hypothesis, limitation and action.

**Deliverable:** submit your pivot analysis and insight note, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Deleting a genuine outlier because it is inconvenient.
- Calling a plausible explanation a proven cause.
- Publishing tiny employee subgroups without disclosure review.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Mean and median answer different questions:** The mean uses every value and is affected by extreme observations.
- **Segment with a purpose:** Pivots can compare department, role, source or period, but groups must use consistent definitions.
- **Write observations before explanations:** An analytical note should separate the observed result, plausible explanations, checks still needed and recommended action.
