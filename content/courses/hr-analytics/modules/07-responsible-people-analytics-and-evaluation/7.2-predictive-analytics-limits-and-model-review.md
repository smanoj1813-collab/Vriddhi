# 7.2 Predictive analytics limits and model review

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Interpret a simple classifier’s errors and identify why employee-risk predictions require strict governance.
- Produce a reviewable model limitations and review checklist using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

A vendor offers a model that is “90% accurate” at predicting exits. In the company’s synthetic sample, 90% of employees stay. A model that predicts everyone stays would also be 90% accurate while identifying no exits. The analyst must ask better questions.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Compare with a meaningful baseline

Accuracy is correct predictions divided by all evaluated cases. With imbalanced outcomes it can hide poor detection of the less common event. A confusion matrix separates true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives. Precision asks how many positive predictions were correct; recall asks how many actual positives were detected. The positive class and evaluation period must be stated.

### 2. Errors have different consequences

A false positive may wrongly label someone likely to leave; a false negative misses an actual exit. Neither error type should be reduced to a technical score without considering harm. Threshold changes trade off errors. A model score is not an explanation of a person’s intentions. This course reviews synthetic aggregate examples only and does not build individual employment-risk deployment or automated adverse-action workflows.

### 3. Check leakage, drift and authority

Training data must not include information only known after the predicted event, such as an exit-processing date when predicting future exits. Evaluate on data not used for training, with appropriate time separation. Performance can drift when roles, policies or populations change. Require authorised purpose, privacy review, fairness assessment, human accountability and a contestability route before any real high-impact use; a vendor accuracy claim is insufficient.

## Worked example

In 100 synthetic records, 10 people exit. A model flags 8; 4 of those actually exit. TP=4, FP=4, FN=6 and TN=86. Precision is 4/8=50%, recall 4/10=40%, and accuracy 90/100=90%. The same accuracy as an all-stay baseline conceals very different errors. No metric by itself justifies using the model on real employees.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: model limitations and review checklist (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Build the four-cell confusion matrix and verify it sums to 100.

2. Calculate accuracy, precision and recall with labelled denominators.

3. Compare the model with an all-stay baseline.

4. Identify two potential leakage fields and one drift risk.

5. Write a review note explaining why the classroom analysis does not authorise individual deployment.

**Deliverable:** submit your model limitations and review checklist, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Using accuracy alone on an imbalanced outcome.
- Treating a risk score as a person’s intention.
- Using post-exit fields to predict an earlier outcome.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Compare with a meaningful baseline:** Accuracy is correct predictions divided by all evaluated cases.
- **Errors have different consequences:** A false positive may wrongly label someone likely to leave; a false negative misses an actual exit.
- **Check leakage, drift and authority:** Training data must not include information only known after the predicted event, such as an exit-processing date when predicting future exits.
