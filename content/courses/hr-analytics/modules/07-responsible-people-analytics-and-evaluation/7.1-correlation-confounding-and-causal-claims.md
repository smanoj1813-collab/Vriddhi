# 7.1 Correlation, confounding and causal claims

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Recognise alternative explanations and design a more credible evaluation of an HR intervention.
- Produce a reviewable causal-question and evaluation sketch using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

Employees who attend an optional development workshop have lower turnover. A sponsor wants to claim the workshop caused retention. Participants may already be more motivated, have supportive managers or work different shifts. The association alone cannot separate these explanations.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Association is not an intervention effect

Correlation describes variables moving together; it does not identify which caused which or whether a third factor explains both. Selection into training can differ from random assignment. Reverse causality is also possible: people intending to stay may be more likely to attend. Draw a simple causal sketch with the proposed intervention, outcome and plausible shared causes before recommending a causal statement.

### 2. Design comparison before launch

Where ethical and practical, a randomised or phased rollout can improve the comparison, with appropriate consent, governance and no denial of essential support. Where randomisation is not suitable, use a careful comparison group and document remaining differences. Collect a baseline and define the outcome window in advance. Do not choose the favourable metric after looking at results. Evaluation design should be proportionate to the decision.

### 3. Interpret uncertainty honestly

Small samples, missing follow-up and changing conditions limit conclusions. A before-and-after difference can be informative but is vulnerable to external changes. Report what was observed and what assumptions would be needed for a causal interpretation. Do not claim that a statistical adjustment automatically removes every bias. A sensible result can be “promising enough for a better-designed pilot”.

## Worked example

Before an onboarding change, a pilot group has 20% early exits and a comparison group 10%. Afterwards they have 12% and 8%. The pilot change is −8 percentage points and comparison change −2, giving a difference-in-differences estimate of −6 points. This illustrative estimate requires assumptions such as comparable underlying trends; the arithmetic alone does not prove causation.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: causal-question and evaluation sketch (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Draw a causal sketch linking workshop attendance, manager support and retention.

2. List selection and reverse-causality explanations for the observed association.

3. Calculate the illustrative difference-in-differences value with signs shown.

4. Propose an ethical comparison design and an outcome window before data collection.

5. Write a conclusion that states assumptions and avoids a guaranteed causal claim.

**Deliverable:** submit your causal-question and evaluation sketch, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Calling every correlation a cause.
- Assuming a before-and-after change isolates the intervention.
- Treating adjustment as proof that all bias disappeared.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Association is not an intervention effect:** Correlation describes variables moving together; it does not identify which caused which or whether a third factor explains both.
- **Design comparison before launch:** Where ethical and practical, a randomised or phased rollout can improve the comparison, with appropriate consent, governance and no denial of essential support.
- **Interpret uncertainty honestly:** Small samples, missing follow-up and changing conditions limit conclusions.
