# 8.2 HR interviews and stakeholder conversations

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Explain an HR analysis clearly and respond to ethical or metric questions within an entry-level role’s authority.
- Produce a reviewable interview response and stakeholder briefing using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

An interviewer asks why turnover and retention do not add to 100%, then asks whether you would share raw employee data with a manager who requests it urgently. The answer needs both metric fluency and responsible judgement.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Explain definitions before numbers

When a question is ambiguous, clarify the population, period, unit and denominator. Demonstrate arithmetic with a small example instead of hiding behind jargon. If you do not know the organisation’s convention, say so and ask for its dictionary. Entry-level credibility comes from careful reasoning, not pretending every company uses the same definition.

### 2. Use evidence-led interview structure

STAR can organise an example: situation, task, action and result, followed by learning if useful. Use a course artefact honestly and label it as a simulation. Explain a correction you made, such as a denominator error or a misleading chart. For a hypothetical question, describe what you would do rather than inventing a past event. Keep the result bounded to the evidence.

### 3. Respect access and escalation boundaries

Urgency does not automatically authorise raw-data sharing. Confirm purpose, recipient access and the minimum necessary information; an aggregate response may meet the need. Escalate legal, health, discrimination or employment decisions to qualified roles under policy. An analyst can identify a discrepancy and recommend review without issuing a legal verdict or deciding someone’s employment outcome.

## Worked example

A strong response is “I would clarify whether the manager needs an aggregate turnover trend or identifiable case records. I would check authorisation, use the minimum necessary data and share through the approved channel. For the trend I would include period, numerator and denominator.” A weak response is “I would email the full spreadsheet immediately because the manager is senior.”

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: interview response and stakeholder briefing (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Prepare a two-minute explanation of turnover versus opening-cohort retention.

2. Use STAR to describe a cleaning or dashboard correction from the course.

3. Roleplay an urgent raw-data request and propose a minimum-data alternative.

4. Map three tasks from a target job description to portfolio evidence.

5. Record feedback on clarity, authority boundaries and honesty, then revise one answer.

**Deliverable:** submit your interview response and stakeholder briefing, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming seniority alone authorises every data request.
- Inventing workplace achievements for interview impact.
- Giving a legal or clinical conclusion beyond the role’s competence.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Explain definitions before numbers:** When a question is ambiguous, clarify the population, period, unit and denominator.
- **Use evidence-led interview structure:** STAR can organise an example: situation, task, action and result, followed by learning if useful.
- **Respect access and escalation boundaries:** Urgency does not automatically authorise raw-data sharing.

## Reflection journal

1. What did you change after checking the example or receiving a review comment? Name the exact input, statement or calculation.
2. Which assumption has the greatest effect on your recommendation, and how would you test it before a real decision?
3. How would you explain this work in a two-minute interview for HR Executive without claiming a classroom simulation was paid experience?

Write 80–120 words in English, Kannada or Hindi with clear technical labels. Keep a specific correction or decision in your portfolio journal.
