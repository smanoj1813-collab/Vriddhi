# 1.2 Employee lifecycle and a useful metric dictionary

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Map the employee lifecycle and document consistent definitions for HR reporting.
- Produce a reviewable lifecycle map and metric dictionary using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The recruitment team reports offer acceptances, payroll reports paid employees, and operations reports active staff. Their totals differ. The difference may be legitimate because the systems use different events and dates, or it may signal missing or duplicated records.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Map stages and owners

The lifecycle includes workforce planning, attraction, selection, offer, joining, onboarding, development, movement and exit. Each stage has operational owners and records. A candidate who accepts an offer has not necessarily joined. A payroll payment is not always proof of active employment during the reporting date. Mapping the events helps reconcile systems and identify where responsibility changes.

### 2. Build a metric dictionary before a dashboard

For each metric record its business question, numerator, denominator, time basis, inclusion rules, source, owner, refresh cadence and caveats. A dictionary turns an ambiguous label such as attrition into a reproducible measure. It also reveals when two departments use different conventions. Resolve differences explicitly; do not average incompatible numbers or choose the one that makes performance look better.

### 3. Snapshots and flows answer different questions

A snapshot counts a state at a point in time, such as active headcount on 30 September. A flow counts events over an interval, such as joiners during September. Specify treatment of transfers, contractors, leave and same-day joins/exits. At organisation level, internal transfers do not change total headcount; at department level, they do. Reconciliation depends on matching boundaries and event rules.

## Worked example

Opening headcount is 80, there are 8 joins and 5 exits, and there are no other boundary changes. Closing headcount is 80 + 8 − 5 = 83. A transfer between two departments changes their local counts but not the organisation total. If the snapshot shows 85, investigate timing, missing exits, duplicate records or population differences before publishing.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: lifecycle map and metric dictionary (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Draw the lifecycle with one record and responsible role at each stage.

2. Define headcount, joiners, offer acceptances and exits using separate time rules.

3. Reconcile the 80-person opening balance to 83 closing and list possible explanations for 85.

4. Add contractor and transfer inclusion rules to the dictionary.

5. Ask a peer to calculate a metric from your definition without verbal clarification.

**Deliverable:** submit your lifecycle map and metric dictionary, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Counting offer acceptances as joined employees.
- Adding internal transfers to organisation-level growth.
- Publishing different totals without documenting population differences.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Map stages and owners:** The lifecycle includes workforce planning, attraction, selection, offer, joining, onboarding, development, movement and exit.
- **Build a metric dictionary before a dashboard:** For each metric record its business question, numerator, denominator, time basis, inclusion rules, source, owner, refresh cadence and caveats.
- **Snapshots and flows answer different questions:** A snapshot counts a state at a point in time, such as active headcount on 30 September.
