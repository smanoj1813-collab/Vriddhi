# 1.1 From HR activity to a decision question

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Connect recruitment and workforce measures to a practical decision without treating people as scores.
- Produce a reviewable people-analytics question brief using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The fictional Bengaluru service company Namma People Services plans to expand its support team. A manager asks for “an HR dashboard” but cannot say whether the immediate decision concerns hiring speed, staffing capacity or early attrition. An analyst must define the decision before choosing a chart.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Start with the decision and stakeholder

People analytics uses workforce evidence to inform decisions, not merely to produce attractive reports. Identify the decision maker, available action, deadline and consequence of being wrong. HR activity counts such as interviews held can describe workload but do not prove better hiring. A useful question links a population and period to an action: which stage delays approved support-role hiring this quarter, and what process change should the recruitment lead test?

### 2. Separate levels of analysis

Descriptive analysis asks what happened; diagnostic analysis explores possible explanations; predictive analysis estimates what may happen; prescriptive work evaluates possible actions. These are not a ladder that every problem must climb. A well-defined count may be more useful than a complex model. Diagnostic associations do not establish causes, and a prediction does not authorise action against an individual. Human accountability remains essential.

### 3. Use operational definitions

Define a vacancy, applicant, employee, joiner, exit and reporting period before counting. Applicant rows may represent applications rather than unique people; one person can apply to two roles. An employee snapshot differs from a table of employment events. Write the unit of analysis and exclusions in a data dictionary. Align definitions with the decision and keep them stable during comparison.

## Worked example

The recruitment lead receives 120 applications from 100 distinct candidates for two vacancies. Reporting “120 candidates” overstates unique people; reporting “100 applications” understates workload. Both measures can be useful if labelled correctly. A question about processing effort may use applications, while one about reach may use distinct candidates. Neither count alone establishes hiring quality.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: people-analytics question brief (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Write three possible HR questions and identify the decision each would support.

2. Choose one question and specify population, period, unit and action owner.

3. Classify the required analysis as descriptive, diagnostic or predictive and justify the simplest sufficient method.

4. Build a short dictionary distinguishing candidate, application, offer and joiner.

5. Write a limitation explaining what your proposed measure cannot conclude.

**Deliverable:** submit your people-analytics question brief, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with a chart before knowing the decision.
- Confusing applications with unique candidates.
- Treating predictive scores as permission to act on people.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Start with the decision and stakeholder:** People analytics uses workforce evidence to inform decisions, not merely to produce attractive reports.
- **Separate levels of analysis:** Descriptive analysis asks what happened; diagnostic analysis explores possible explanations; predictive analysis estimates what may happen; prescriptive work evaluates possible actions.
- **Use operational definitions:** Define a vacancy, applicant, employee, joiner, exit and reporting period before counting.
