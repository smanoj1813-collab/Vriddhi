# 5.1 Engagement surveys and response bias

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Design a concise survey and interpret response distributions with confidentiality and nonresponse limits.
- Produce a reviewable survey design and response summary using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

A survey receives enthusiastic replies from office staff but few from night-shift workers. The HR team wants to publish an overall engagement score and identify the “least engaged” team. The analyst must inspect participation and disclosure risk before comparing results.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Measure a defined construct

Engagement, satisfaction and intention to stay are related but distinct. Write questions that address one idea at a time, use a consistent response scale and avoid leading language. Pilot wording for clarity and accessibility. A course-built survey is a practice instrument, not a validated psychological test. Do not claim clinical or personality conclusions from a few workplace questions.

### 2. Participation shapes interpretation

Response rate is responses divided by eligible invitations under the stated rule. Nonrespondents may differ systematically from respondents, especially if access, trust or shift timing varies. High response is helpful but does not prove unbiased results. Report group participation where safe and consider an alternative collection window. Do not pressure individuals to reveal how they answered or link criticism to performance assessment.

### 3. Report distributions and protect trust

Show counts or proportions across the scale rather than only a mean. Averages can hide polarised experiences. Suppress small groups and review free text for identifying details before sharing. Explain who can see raw responses and how results will be used. If the organisation cannot guarantee anonymity, do not promise it; describe the actual confidentiality controls and limitations accurately.

## Worked example

Of 80 eligible employees, 48 respond: a 60% response rate. Thirty of the 48 choose favourable responses, yielding 62.5% favourable among respondents. It is not correct to say 62.5% of all 80 employees are favourable. At least 30 are known favourable; the views of 32 nonrespondents remain unknown. Explore participation by shift without exposing small groups.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: survey design and response summary (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Draft four single-idea survey questions with a consistent response scale.

2. Write a truthful participant note covering purpose, access and voluntary participation.

3. Calculate response and favourable-response rates from the worked case.

4. Sketch a distribution chart and a safe participation breakdown.

5. Recommend an accessible follow-up method that does not coerce individual disclosure.

**Deliverable:** submit your survey design and response summary, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a practice survey a validated psychological test.
- Generalising respondent percentages to every employee.
- Promising anonymity when identifying information is retained.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Measure a defined construct:** Engagement, satisfaction and intention to stay are related but distinct.
- **Participation shapes interpretation:** Response rate is responses divided by eligible invitations under the stated rule.
- **Report distributions and protect trust:** Show counts or proportions across the scale rather than only a mean.
