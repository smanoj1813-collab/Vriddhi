# 5.2 Retention cohorts and fair investigation

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Compare retention at matched observation windows and develop process-level hypotheses without labelling individuals.
- Produce a reviewable cohort retention analysis using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The company compares a January hiring cohort after six months with an August cohort after one month and concludes retention improved. The August group has simply had less time at risk. A fair comparison needs aligned windows and transparent treatment of incomplete follow-up.

Namma People Services is a fictional organisation created for this course. All employee IDs, applications, costs, survey responses and workforce events are synthetic. The exercises model decisions an entry-level HR team might support, but they do not authorise live selection, dismissal, health inference or individual employee-risk scoring. Use the supplied practice records rather than collecting real personal data.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Define cohort entry and follow-up

A hiring cohort groups people by an entry event such as joining month. Retention at ninety days asks which eligible joiners remain at the agreed day ninety boundary. Recent joiners who have not reached the window should not be counted as fully observed successes. Document exits, rehires and transfers consistently. Different exposure time is a measurement issue, not proof of improvement.

### 2. Segment carefully and generate hypotheses

Compare like roles, locations or shifts only where the sample and confidentiality rules allow. A higher exit rate may relate to workload, manager changes, compensation, commute or cohort mix; the dataset may not contain enough evidence to distinguish them. Exit interviews can add context but may have nonresponse and social-desirability bias. Treat themes as hypotheses for investigation rather than unquestionable causes.

### 3. Act on systems, not speculative labels

Retention analysis should support improvements such as clearer job previews, onboarding support or scheduling review. Avoid marking employees as “likely leavers” in public or using speculative risk scores for exclusion. Individual employment decisions need authorised HR processes and evidence beyond aggregate patterns. Frame recommendations with an owner, a safe pilot and a measure of unintended consequences.

## Worked example

A January cohort has 40 joiners with complete ninety-day follow-up; 34 remain, so retention is 85%. A recent cohort has 30 joiners, but only 10 have reached ninety days; 9 of those remain. Its observed eligible retention is 90% for n=10, not 29/30 or a claim that the whole cohort achieved ninety-day retention. The small completed window limits comparison.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: cohort retention analysis (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Define a ninety-day retention metric including eligibility and transfer rules.

2. Calculate both observed cohort rates and show complete-follow-up counts.

3. List three alternative explanations for a difference without claiming a cause.

4. Design a supportive onboarding investigation using aggregate evidence.

5. Recommend a pilot and explicitly rule out individual adverse decisions from the classroom analysis.

**Deliverable:** submit your cohort retention analysis, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

## Common mistakes

- Treating recent employees as fully observed survivors.
- Comparing cohorts with different follow-up windows.
- Using a group pattern to label a specific employee.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Define cohort entry and follow-up:** A hiring cohort groups people by an entry event such as joining month.
- **Segment carefully and generate hypotheses:** Compare like roles, locations or shifts only where the sample and confidentiality rules allow.
- **Act on systems, not speculative labels:** Retention analysis should support improvements such as clearer job previews, onboarding support or scheduling review.
