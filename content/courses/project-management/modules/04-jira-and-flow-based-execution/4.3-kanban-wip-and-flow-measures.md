# 4.3 Kanban, WIP and flow measures

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Use work-in-progress limits, ageing and cycle time to diagnose a delivery bottleneck.
- Produce a reviewable flow analysis and improvement experiment using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

Five items wait in review while developers start six more. The board shows activity everywhere, but nothing reaches learners. The coordinator must help the team finish work rather than celebrate how much it has started.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Visualise the actual system

Kanban uses a visible workflow, explicit policies and control of work in progress to improve flow. Represent queues and blocked work instead of hiding them outside the board. A WIP limit is a signal to collaborate and remove impediments; it is not a reason to delete cards or mislabel active work. Start where the work is, then inspect the system. A board without shared policies is only a visual list.

### 2. Distinguish the flow measures

Throughput is the number of items completed per period. Cycle time is elapsed time from a defined work-start point to completion; lead time may start earlier at request arrival. State boundaries and calendar conventions. Work-item age describes still-open work and can reveal trouble before completion averages do. Report distributions or percentiles where useful: a single average can hide long delays. Do not compare unlike item types without context.

### 3. Use Little’s Law carefully

Under stable conditions and consistent system boundaries, average WIP equals average throughput multiplied by average cycle time. It is a relationship about averages, not a guarantee for the next item. A changing backlog, inconsistent item definitions or a short observation period can make naive forecasts misleading. Reducing unnecessary WIP can expose constraints and shorten waiting, but investigate the actual bottleneck rather than merely imposing a smaller number.

## Worked example

With average WIP of 12 items and average throughput of 4 items per week in a stable system, implied average cycle time is 12/4 = 3 weeks. This does not mean every item takes exactly three weeks. If review is the queue, trial a review WIP limit of three and arrange a daily review slot; compare ageing and throughput before and after without claiming causality from one week alone.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: flow analysis and improvement experiment (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Sketch a board with Ready, Doing, Review and Done, including blocked markers.

2. Calculate the stable-system example and specify the measurement boundaries.

3. Create ages for five waiting-review items and identify the oldest two.

4. Propose a WIP policy that triggers help at Review rather than more starts.

5. Design a two-week experiment with baseline, outcome measure, owner and rollback condition.

**Deliverable:** submit your flow analysis and improvement experiment, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a WIP limit as a staff utilisation target.
- Reading average cycle time as a delivery promise for every item.
- Ignoring blocked or unfinished work when assessing delay.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Visualise the actual system:** Kanban uses a visible workflow, explicit policies and control of work in progress to improve flow.
- **Distinguish the flow measures:** Throughput is the number of items completed per period.
- **Use Little’s Law carefully:** Under stable conditions and consistent system boundaries, average WIP equals average throughput multiplied by average cycle time.
