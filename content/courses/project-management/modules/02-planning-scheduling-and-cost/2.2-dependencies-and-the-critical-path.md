# 2.2 Dependencies and the critical path

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Calculate a simple critical path and recognise resource constraints that a network alone misses.
- Produce a reviewable dependency network and milestone plan using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The team can configure the form while preparing staff training, but testing needs both a configured form and approved test cases. The sponsor asks whether speeding up training will necessarily bring launch forward. The answer depends on the network, not on which task feels most visible.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Dependencies describe necessary order

A finish-to-start dependency means the successor cannot start until the predecessor finishes. Record the real reason: approval, technical input, shared resource or external delivery. Do not add a dependency merely because two tasks were adjacent in a spreadsheet. Distinguish a hard constraint from a preferred order; unnecessary constraints make the schedule less flexible. Use working days consistently and state the calendar used.

### 2. Find the longest dependent path

In a simple deterministic network without resource limits, the critical path is the longest-duration path and determines the earliest project finish. Tasks on it have zero total float under the stated assumptions. A shorter path may have float, but that float is not automatically spare time for unrelated work. Multiple critical paths are possible. Recalculate when a duration, dependency or deadline changes.

### 3. Resource levelling can change the answer

A network can assume two tasks run together even when the same person must do both. Check named resource availability after the dependency calculation. Levelling may move the finish beyond the unconstrained critical-path date. Fast-tracking overlaps activities and may increase rework; crashing adds suitable resources at a cost. Neither technique is a magic promise. Present schedule recovery options with consequences and obtain approval where needed.

## Worked example

Using day zero as the start: A takes 2 days; B takes 4 after A; C takes 3 after A; D takes 2 after both B and C. Paths A–B–D and A–C–D take 8 and 7 days. The finish is day 8, B is on the critical path, and C has one day of total float if resources are unconstrained. Saving one day on C alone does not shorten the finish.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: dependency network and milestone plan (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Draw the four-task network with start and finish points; label every dependency.

2. Calculate earliest starts and finishes, then identify the longest path.

3. Move C from three to five days and recalculate the finish and critical path.

4. Assume one specialist performs B and C; propose a feasible non-overlapping sequence.

5. Prepare a milestone brief showing the original plan, revised forecast and recovery trade-off.

**Deliverable:** submit your dependency network and milestone plan, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the busiest person the critical path.
- Ignoring shared-resource constraints.
- Reporting a baseline date as if it were the latest forecast.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

- **Dependencies describe necessary order:** A finish-to-start dependency means the successor cannot start until the predecessor finishes.
- **Find the longest dependent path:** In a simple deterministic network without resource limits, the critical path is the longest-duration path and determines the earliest project finish.
- **Resource levelling can change the answer:** A network can assume two tasks run together even when the same person must do both.
