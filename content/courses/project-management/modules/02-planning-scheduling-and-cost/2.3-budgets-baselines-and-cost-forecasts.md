# 2.3 Budgets, baselines and cost forecasts

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Build a cost baseline and explain variance without confusing spend with progress.
- Produce a reviewable cost baseline and forecast note using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The placement pilot has spent less than expected, so a weekly note calls it “under budget”. Yet testing has barely started. The sponsor needs to know whether the project is efficient or simply late, and what completing the remaining work is now likely to cost.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Separate cost categories and assumptions

List labour effort and rates, materials, licences, vendor charges and any taxes or reimbursements relevant to the institution’s rules. Separate one-time costs from recurring operational costs so handover does not conceal an unfunded service. Show the estimate date and exclusions. A classroom rate is a modelling assumption, not a market-price claim. Budget approval belongs to authorised people, not to the spreadsheet author.

### 2. Baseline, actual and forecast have different jobs

The cost baseline is the approved reference for control. Actual cost is incurred cost as of a stated cut-off; committed but unpaid spend may also matter for forecasting. Estimate to complete is the current expected remaining cost. Estimate at completion equals actual cost plus estimate to complete in the bottom-up method used here. Updating a forecast does not silently replace the approved baseline. Keep a change history.

### 3. Relate spend to accepted work

Low spend can signal delay rather than efficiency. In a simplified earned-value example, planned value is budgeted scheduled work, earned value is budgeted completed work, and actual cost is what that work cost. Cost variance is EV minus AC; schedule variance is EV minus PV in cost units, not days. Use these only where completion is defined credibly. A percentage invented to make status look green gives misleading indicators.

## Worked example

At the reporting date PV is ₹20,000, EV ₹15,000 and AC ₹18,000. CV = −₹3,000 and SV = −₹5,000: the work completed cost more than its budget and less work was completed than planned. CPI = 15,000/18,000 ≈ 0.83. If a fresh bottom-up remaining estimate is ₹22,000, EAC is ₹40,000. Do not add EV to actual cost to forecast spend.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: cost baseline and forecast note (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Create cost lines for the pilot using declared classroom rates and quantities.

2. Separate setup costs from three months of ongoing support costs.

3. Compute CV, SV and CPI from the worked example, labelling units.

4. Prepare a bottom-up EAC and compare it with a hypothetical ₹35,000 baseline.

5. Recommend a cost decision with an impact on scope or schedule and an approver.

**Deliverable:** submit your cost baseline and forecast note, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Equating underspend with good performance.
- Reading schedule variance in rupees as days late.
- Overwriting the baseline when the forecast changes.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Separate cost categories and assumptions:** List labour effort and rates, materials, licences, vendor charges and any taxes or reimbursements relevant to the institution’s rules.
- **Baseline, actual and forecast have different jobs:** The cost baseline is the approved reference for control.
- **Relate spend to accepted work:** Low spend can signal delay rather than efficiency.
