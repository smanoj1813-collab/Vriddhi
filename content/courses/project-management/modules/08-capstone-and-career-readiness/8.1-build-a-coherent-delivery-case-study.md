# 8.1 Build a coherent delivery case study

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Assemble a portfolio that traces a decision from problem through evidence to outcome without overstating experience.
- Produce a reviewable project case study using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

A learner has a charter, Jira screenshots and a status report, but the dates contradict one another. A recruiter cannot tell what the learner personally did or whether the project was real. A coherent case study is more persuasive than a large collection of disconnected files.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Tell a verifiable delivery story

Use problem, constraints, actions, evidence, result and learning. Explain the initial scope and any approved changes. Distinguish a delivered output from a measured outcome and a forecast benefit. Label the course case as a simulation. Employers can value simulated work when it demonstrates sound reasoning; inventing a client or business result undermines credibility. Keep the story short enough to discuss in an interview.

### 2. Trace claims to artefacts

Give each key claim an evidence reference, version and date. A timeline should reconcile charter approval, baseline, change decisions, testing and handover. Screenshots are illustrations, not substitutes for a readable explanation or editable calculation. Remove names, account identifiers and confidential content. If working in a group, state your contribution and identify shared outputs without claiming sole authorship.

### 3. Show correction and judgement

A useful portfolio includes a mistake discovered, how it was corrected and what evidence changed the decision. It can include an option rejected on cost or risk grounds. Do not edit the history to make the project appear frictionless. Select artefacts relevant to the role: coordination evidence for a Project Coordinator, reconciled reporting for a PMO Analyst, and a sustainable SOP for an Operations Associate.

## Worked example

A defensible bullet is “Built a synthetic six-week pilot plan with eight work packages, a dependency network and a risk register; revised the forecast after a two-day test delay.” An indefensible version is “Managed a successful college transformation saving ₹5 lakh” when no live project or measured savings exist. Specific, bounded evidence is stronger than invented scale.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: project case study (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Create an evidence index linking five claims to the correct artefact versions.

2. Reconcile milestone dates across charter, schedule, report and closure note.

3. Draft a one-page case study and label every synthetic result.

4. Write two role-specific portfolio bullets without inventing employment experience.

5. Ask a reviewer to challenge one claim and improve its evidence reference.

**Deliverable:** submit your project case study, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Calling simulated benefits real savings.
- Including unreadable screenshots without context.
- Claiming all group work as individual work.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Tell a verifiable delivery story:** Use problem, constraints, actions, evidence, result and learning.
- **Trace claims to artefacts:** Give each key claim an evidence reference, version and date.
- **Show correction and judgement:** A useful portfolio includes a mistake discovered, how it was corrected and what evidence changed the decision.

## Reflection journal

1. What did you change after checking the example or receiving a review comment? Name the exact input, statement or calculation.
2. Which assumption has the greatest effect on your recommendation, and how would you test it before a real decision?
3. How would you explain this work in a two-minute interview for Project Coordinator without claiming a classroom simulation was paid experience?

Write 80–120 words in English, Kannada or Hindi with clear technical labels. Keep a specific correction or decision in your portfolio journal.
