# 7.3 Handover, closure and continuous improvement

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Close a project with accepted evidence and transfer ownership before testing a small operational improvement.
- Produce a reviewable handover pack and improvement charter using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The pilot has launched, but staff cannot find the support contact or restore a lost booking export. The project lead wants to close immediately. A launch is not a complete handover if the receiving operation cannot run the service safely.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Transition is a deliverable

Handover should include the service owner, support route, access arrangements, training evidence, operating instructions, unresolved issues and agreed acceptance. Transfer credentials through approved secure mechanisms, not through a public portfolio. Verify that the receiving team can perform important tasks. A meeting invitation or emailed manual is not proof of operational readiness. Make the transition criteria part of the plan early.

### 2. Closure makes obligations visible

Confirm acceptance, outstanding contractual and financial actions, records retention and ownership of residual risks. Capture lessons as context, observed effect and actionable recommendation rather than blame. Close access no longer needed and archive records according to institutional policy. If some work remains, name an owner and decision authority. “Closed” must not mean that unresolved responsibilities have become invisible.

### 3. Improve with a small learning cycle

Use a Plan–Do–Check–Act cycle: specify a problem and prediction, test a bounded change, compare evidence, and adopt, revise or stop. Improvement does not require a large software project. A booking reminder script or clearer triage instruction can be tested safely with synthetic or approved data. Avoid claiming causation from a simple before-and-after comparison without considering demand, staffing and seasonal changes.

## Worked example

Before a triage change, 30 of 100 requests need rework; after a small trial, 18 of 90 do. Rework proportions are 30% and 20%, a reduction of ten percentage points. The trials have different denominators and may differ in case mix. Treat the result as promising evidence to investigate, not proof that the script caused all improvement. Record adoption criteria and a review date.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: handover pack and improvement charter (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Build a handover checklist with service owner, support, training and access evidence.

2. List three residual issues with owners and acceptance of responsibility.

3. Write one lesson learned using observation, effect and recommendation.

4. Calculate both rework proportions and distinguish percentage points from relative change.

5. Plan a bounded improvement trial with a balancing quality measure and a stop rule.

**Deliverable:** submit your handover pack and improvement charter, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Equating go-live with complete handover.
- Leaving residual risks with a disbanded project team.
- Claiming causation from an uncontrolled before-and-after change.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Transition is a deliverable:** Handover should include the service owner, support route, access arrangements, training evidence, operating instructions, unresolved issues and agreed acceptance.
- **Closure makes obligations visible:** Confirm acceptance, outstanding contractual and financial actions, records retention and ownership of residual risks.
- **Improve with a small learning cycle:** Use a Plan–Do–Check–Act cycle: specify a problem and prediction, test a bounded change, compare evidence, and adopt, revise or stop.

## Reflection journal

1. What did you change after checking the example or receiving a review comment? Name the exact input, statement or calculation.
2. Which assumption has the greatest effect on your recommendation, and how would you test it before a real decision?
3. How would you explain this work in a two-minute interview for Project Coordinator without claiming a classroom simulation was paid experience?

Write 80–120 words in English, Kannada or Hindi with clear technical labels. Keep a specific correction or decision in your portfolio journal.
