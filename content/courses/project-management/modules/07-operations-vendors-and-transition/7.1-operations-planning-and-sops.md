# 7.1 Operations planning and SOPs

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Design a repeatable process with service measures, capacity assumptions and exception handling.
- Produce a reviewable SOP and service-capacity worksheet using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

After the pilot, the placement desk receives 180 requests in a three-hour morning window. The project team is moving on. Operations needs a process that staff can follow during both routine work and exceptions, not a folder of development notes.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Describe a service from request to resolution

Map the trigger, inputs, activities, decisions, outputs and customer of the process. Identify handoffs and waiting, not only active work. An SOP should specify role ownership, steps, checks and exception routes in language a new staff member can follow. Keep a revision date and owner. A document that no one can locate or maintain is not an operational control.

### 2. Capacity is constrained by actual availability

Compare arrival demand with service capacity using consistent time units. At three minutes per request, one fully available desk can process at most twenty per hour before breaks, variability and other duties. Average demand below theoretical capacity does not guarantee no queues, especially when arrivals bunch together. Identify peaks and bottlenecks before recommending staff increases or automation. State what the simple model leaves out.

### 3. Measures should balance speed and quality

A response-time target should not encourage staff to close unresolved requests. Pair timeliness with first-contact resolution, rework or quality checks. Define SLA targets as agreed service commitments, with clock boundaries and exclusions; do not call an informal wish an enforceable contract. Exceptions such as missing documents need a visible queue, owner and customer communication. Review trends rather than punishing a single difficult case.

## Worked example

At three minutes per request, one desk has a theoretical capacity of 60 requests over three hours. Handling 180 requests requires at least three fully available desks at that rate, leaving no buffer for variability or breaks. A practical plan must add capacity, spread arrivals or reduce handling effort while protecting quality. Simply writing “three desks” is not a robust service commitment.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: SOP and service-capacity worksheet (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Map the request-to-resolution process with at least one decision and one exception.

2. Calculate theoretical capacity and list three reasons actual capacity may be lower.

3. Draft an SOP with trigger, roles, steps, checks and escalation path.

4. Define one speed measure and one quality measure with precise boundaries.

5. Propose an appointment or triage experiment and explain how to check unintended effects.

**Deliverable:** submit your SOP and service-capacity worksheet, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Using theoretical maximum capacity as a guaranteed service level.
- Closing requests merely to improve response statistics.
- Omitting an exception route from the SOP.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Describe a service from request to resolution:** Map the trigger, inputs, activities, decisions, outputs and customer of the process.
- **Capacity is constrained by actual availability:** Compare arrival demand with service capacity using consistent time units.
- **Measures should balance speed and quality:** A response-time target should not encourage staff to close unresolved requests.
