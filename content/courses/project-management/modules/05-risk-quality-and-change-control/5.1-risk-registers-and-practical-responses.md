# 5.1 Risk registers and practical responses

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Distinguish a future risk from a current issue and choose an owned, reviewable response.
- Produce a reviewable risk and issue register using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The pilot depends on a vendor demonstration next Friday. The vendor has not confirmed availability. Separately, the test account already fails to open. One is uncertainty about a future event; the other is a condition that exists now.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Write cause, event and impact

A useful risk statement links a cause to an uncertain event and an effect on objectives: because the vendor has one specialist, absence may delay testing and threaten the launch milestone. An issue has already occurred and needs an action or decision now. Risks can concern opportunities as well as threats. Avoid labels such as “vendor risk” that do not explain what might happen or why the team should act.

### 2. Prioritise consistently, not mechanically

Define probability and impact scales before scoring. Multiplying ordinal scores can help triage but does not create a precise financial probability. Review high-consequence risks even if their likelihood is low. Record an owner, trigger, response, due date and residual exposure. Ownership means someone monitors and coordinates the response; it does not mean that person caused the risk.

### 3. Responses change exposure

Threat responses include avoidance, mitigation, transfer and acceptance. A backup demonstrator mitigates availability risk; a contract may transfer some financial exposure but does not eliminate delivery risk. Active acceptance can include contingency and triggers. Distinguish preventive action before the event from contingency after a trigger. Escalate risks outside the team’s authority rather than disguising them as routine tasks.

## Worked example

On a classroom 1–5 scale, probability 3 and impact 4 produce a priority score of 12, not a 12% probability. A separate expected monetary value example uses an actual probability assumption: 0.25 × ₹20,000 impact = ₹5,000 expected exposure. Neither number means the loss will definitely be ₹5,000. State the uncertainty and assumptions.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: risk and issue register (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Write four cause–event–impact risks and two current issues for the pilot.

2. Define probability and impact scales, then score the risks with reasoning.

3. Assign an owner role, trigger and response to each high-priority risk.

4. Distinguish one preventive action from its contingency response.

5. Calculate the EMV example and explain why it is not the ordinal priority score.

**Deliverable:** submit your risk and issue register, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing issues and risks without indicating status.
- Calling a 1–5 score a probability percentage.
- Assuming transfer removes all accountability.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Write cause, event and impact:** A useful risk statement links a cause to an uncertain event and an effect on objectives: because the vendor has one specialist, absence may delay testing and threaten the launch milestone.
- **Prioritise consistently, not mechanically:** Define probability and impact scales before scoring.
- **Responses change exposure:** Threat responses include avoidance, mitigation, transfer and acceptance.

## Reflection journal

1. What did you change after checking the example or receiving a review comment? Name the exact input, statement or calculation.
2. Which assumption has the greatest effect on your recommendation, and how would you test it before a real decision?
3. How would you explain this work in a two-minute interview for Project Coordinator without claiming a classroom simulation was paid experience?

Write 80–120 words in English, Kannada or Hindi with clear technical labels. Keep a specific correction or decision in your portfolio journal.
