# 1.2 Charters, scope and acceptance

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Write a charter that makes authority, boundaries and acceptance testable.
- Produce a reviewable one-page charter and acceptance checklist using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The placement sponsor says “make registration smooth”. A volunteer interprets that as automated SMS, another adds a payments screen, and the college has not approved collection of phone numbers. Without a boundary, everyone can be busy while building different projects.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. A charter creates a shared starting point

A lightweight charter states the problem, objective, sponsor, project lead, deadline, budget limit, high-level scope and major assumptions. It is not a detailed task list and is not a substitute for approval. Distinguish a proposed charter from an authorised one by showing the approver, decision and date. Where funding is not yet approved, record that uncertainty explicitly. A coordinator prepares the evidence and routes it through the institution’s actual authority structure.

### 2. Scope includes exclusions

Scope describes the deliverables and necessary work. Exclusions tell stakeholders what the project will not provide, such as payment collection or integration with an external employer portal. Exclusions should be negotiated, not hidden in small print. Clarify who owns requests after the baseline is approved. A new request can be valuable and still require a change decision. Keeping a request in a log is not the same as approving it.

### 3. Acceptance is evidence, not enthusiasm

Acceptance criteria should be observable by a named reviewer. “Easy to use” is insufficient; “a learner can book, correct and cancel a slot in a supervised test without staff entering data for them” can be tested. Define the test conditions, sample, expected result and unresolved defects. Acceptance of the project deliverable and achievement of long-term benefits happen at different times. Record any conditional acceptance and the owner of remaining actions.

## Worked example

A pilot includes 40 volunteer users and three basic journeys: create, amend and cancel a booking. If 37 complete all journeys without assistance, the observed success proportion is 37/40 = 92.5%. Whether that passes depends on the acceptance threshold agreed before the test. Do not invent a 90% threshold after seeing the result. Small volunteer samples also limit generalisation.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: one-page charter and acceptance checklist (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Draft a charter with sponsor, purpose, milestone, cost limit and approval status.

2. Write three in-scope deliverables and three exclusions; explain why payment collection is excluded.

3. Create an acceptance table containing journey, condition, evidence, threshold and reviewer.

4. Evaluate the 37-of-40 result against a proposed 95% target and identify a corrective action.

5. Log a request for SMS as pending, with impact questions rather than a promised delivery date.

**Deliverable:** submit your one-page charter and acceptance checklist, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing a charter with permission already granted.
- Setting thresholds after seeing results.
- Equating a requested feature with approved scope.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **A charter creates a shared starting point:** A lightweight charter states the problem, objective, sponsor, project lead, deadline, budget limit, high-level scope and major assumptions.
- **Scope includes exclusions:** Scope describes the deliverables and necessary work.
- **Acceptance is evidence, not enthusiasm:** Acceptance criteria should be observable by a named reviewer.

## Reflection journal

1. What did you change after checking the example or receiving a review comment? Name the exact input, statement or calculation.
2. Which assumption has the greatest effect on your recommendation, and how would you test it before a real decision?
3. How would you explain this work in a two-minute interview for Project Coordinator without claiming a classroom simulation was paid experience?

Write 80–120 words in English, Kannada or Hindi with clear technical labels. Keep a specific correction or decision in your portfolio journal.
