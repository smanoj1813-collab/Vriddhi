# 1.1 Projects, operations and value

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Distinguish a temporary project from ongoing operations and separate outputs from intended benefits.
- Produce a reviewable project opportunity brief using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

A fictional Bengaluru college wants to replace a manual placement-registration queue with a simple booking service in six weeks. The principal asks for an app, the placement coordinator asks for fewer duplicate registrations, and students ask for shorter queues. These are related but not identical requests.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Temporary change versus repeatable service

A project creates a specific change and has an identifiable end. Operating the placement desk every day is an ongoing service; designing and introducing a new registration process is a project. Both need planning, but their control questions differ. A project needs acceptance and handover; operations need stable ownership, recurring capacity and service measures. Calling routine work a project does not make it more valuable. Classify the work to choose the right management method.

### 2. Outputs, outcomes and benefits

A booking form is an output. Students completing registration without a second visit is an outcome. Staff hours saved while maintaining equitable access is a benefit. A delivered form does not prove either outcome. Write a chain linking the output to a changed behaviour and then to a measurable benefit. Identify who will measure the benefit after handover. Do not promise a result when you control only one contributor to it.

### 3. Constraints and a minimum useful change

Time, cost, scope, quality and risk interact. A fixed event date may mean reducing optional features rather than silently removing accessibility checks. Define the smallest useful change, its acceptance evidence and exclusions. The coordinator gathers options and obtains decisions; they do not commit someone else’s budget by declaring a deadline. A sponsor authorises the project and resolves trade-offs beyond the team’s authority. Record assumptions before estimating work.

## Worked example

The desk handles 240 registrations per event. At six minutes each, handling effort is 1,440 minutes. A pilot target of four minutes implies 960 minutes, a potential saving of 480 minutes or eight staff-hours. This is a forecast, not a measured benefit. Check the pilot’s time per registration, error rate and student access before claiming the saving.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: project opportunity brief (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Separate the request into one project and two ongoing operational responsibilities.

2. Draft a problem statement using 240 registrations and the six-minute baseline; label both synthetic.

3. Describe one output, one outcome and one benefit, each with an owner and measurement point.

4. Offer a minimal pilot and a larger alternative; list a trade-off and an exclusion for each.

5. Recommend a pilot decision and name the evidence required before a full rollout.

**Deliverable:** submit your project opportunity brief, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Treating installation as proof of a benefit.
- Claiming forecast savings as actual results.
- Leaving operations without an owner after handover.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Temporary change versus repeatable service:** A project creates a specific change and has an identifiable end.
- **Outputs, outcomes and benefits:** A booking form is an output.
- **Constraints and a minimum useful change:** Time, cost, scope, quality and risk interact.
