# 6.2 Meetings, minutes and negotiation

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Facilitate an evidence-led meeting and record decisions without manufacturing agreement.
- Produce a reviewable meeting pack and decision log using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The vendor wants an extra week, the placement team cannot move the event, and the project lead says “everyone agreed” after a discussion with no written decision. The next meeting should clarify choices and authority, not repeat the argument.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Prepare a decision-centred agenda

State the purpose, required decision, evidence to read and who must attend because of authority or expertise. Separate information items from decisions. Timebox discussion and circulate concise options early enough to review. If a necessary approver is absent, distinguish a recommendation from a decision. Accessibility includes readable materials and a way for quieter or remote participants to contribute.

### 2. Negotiate interests and constraints

A position is a stated demand; an interest is the underlying need. A vendor demanding an extra week may need an available tester; the college insisting on a date may need a safe event workflow rather than every optional feature. Explore alternatives before assuming one side must lose. Do not negotiate away mandatory privacy or safety controls. Confirm who can make any cost or contract commitment.

### 3. Minutes are a durable record

Capture the decision, authority, rationale, dissent where relevant, actions, owners and dates. Minutes need not transcribe every sentence. Read back important decisions and distribute the record for correction using an agreed response window. Silence should not be invented as approval if policy requires explicit authorisation. Keep sensitive details in appropriately restricted records and link to them rather than copying them into broad circulation.

## Worked example

The meeting considers three options: move the event, remove optional reminders, or bring in a qualified tester at added cost. The sponsor approves deferring reminders while retaining core safety testing. The record says what was approved, why, by whom and which baseline version changes. The vendor’s concern about later support remains an open action rather than disappearing from the minutes.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: meeting pack and decision log (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Create an agenda with one decision and the minimum required participants.

2. List each party’s position and likely underlying interest without pretending to know it for certain.

3. Prepare three options with consequences and authority requirements.

4. Roleplay the meeting and read back the proposed decision before closing.

5. Publish minutes with actions and a correction route; separate pending approvals clearly.

**Deliverable:** submit your meeting pack and decision log, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Writing recommendations as if they were authorised decisions.
- Treating silence as consent when explicit approval is required.
- Recording every spoken sentence but omitting action owners.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Prepare a decision-centred agenda:** State the purpose, required decision, evidence to read and who must attend because of authority or expertise.
- **Negotiate interests and constraints:** A position is a stated demand; an interest is the underlying need.
- **Minutes are a durable record:** Capture the decision, authority, rationale, dissent where relevant, actions, owners and dates.

## Reflection journal

1. What did you change after checking the example or receiving a review comment? Name the exact input, statement or calculation.
2. Which assumption has the greatest effect on your recommendation, and how would you test it before a real decision?
3. How would you explain this work in a two-minute interview for Project Coordinator without claiming a classroom simulation was paid experience?
