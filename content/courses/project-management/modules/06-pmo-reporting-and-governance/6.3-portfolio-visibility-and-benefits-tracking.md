# 6.3 Portfolio visibility and benefits tracking

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Compare project proposals and track benefits without confusing PMO reporting with authorisation.
- Produce a reviewable portfolio comparison and benefits register using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The college has capacity for one of three initiatives: placement booking, library stock verification or an attendance reminder process. Each proposer claims urgency. The PMO analyst needs a transparent comparison and a realistic view of shared staff capacity.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Projects, programmes and portfolios differ

A project delivers a bounded change. A programme coordinates related work to obtain benefits that require combined management. A portfolio groups investments to support strategy and resource decisions, even when projects are unrelated. A PMO can provide standards, reporting and governance support, but its authority varies. Never assume an analyst can approve a project merely because they maintain the portfolio spreadsheet.

### 2. Scoring is decision support

A weighted score can compare strategic fit, expected benefit, urgency, effort and risk, using agreed definitions. Avoid double counting the same benefit under several labels. Show uncertainty and capacity constraints alongside the ranking. A high score does not make a mandatory compliance initiative optional or make an unfunded project feasible. Test whether small weight changes reverse the ranking and discuss the sensitivity.

### 3. Benefits require operational ownership

A benefits register records baseline, target, measure, owner, review date and external influences. The owner is often in the receiving operation, not the temporary project team. Distinguish forecast benefits from measured benefits and avoid counting the same staff-hour saving in two initiatives. Schedule post-handover reviews. A benefit that depends on adoption needs adoption evidence, not only an installation record.

## Worked example

With weights of 50% for strategic fit and 50% for feasibility, Project A scores 4 and 2, giving 3.0 out of 5. Project B scores 3 and 4, giving 3.5. If strategic fit becomes 80%, A scores 3.6 and B 3.2. The ranking reverses, showing sensitivity rather than proving either project is objectively best. Check constraints and evidence before recommending an investment.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: portfolio comparison and benefits register (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Create three fictional proposals with expected benefits and resource needs.

2. Define two scoring criteria and apply the worked weighting calculation.

3. Run a sensitivity check by changing weights and explain the ranking change.

4. Identify a shared specialist who creates a capacity conflict between proposals.

5. Write a benefits-register entry with baseline, owner and a post-handover review date.

**Deliverable:** submit your portfolio comparison and benefits register, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a weighted score as an automatic approval.
- Double counting shared benefits.
- Leaving benefits ownership with a team that has disbanded.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Projects, programmes and portfolios differ:** A project delivers a bounded change.
- **Scoring is decision support:** A weighted score can compare strategic fit, expected benefit, urgency, effort and risk, using agreed definitions.
- **Benefits require operational ownership:** A benefits register records baseline, target, measure, owner, review date and external influences.
