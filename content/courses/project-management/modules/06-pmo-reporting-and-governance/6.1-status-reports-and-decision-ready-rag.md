# 6.1 Status reports and decision-ready RAG

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Produce a concise status report that separates baseline, forecast, evidence and decisions.
- Produce a reviewable weekly status report using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The PMO asks for a one-page update. The team wants to report “80% done” because eight of ten tasks are closed, but the two remaining tasks contain most of the testing risk. The sponsor needs a decision, not a reassuring colour.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Define status before reporting it

RAG means red, amber and green only within an agreed policy. Define thresholds for milestone delay, cost forecast and critical unresolved risks before the reporting cycle. A green schedule does not imply green quality or risk. State the reporting cut-off, baseline version and data owner. Do not change the threshold because the current project would otherwise look uncomfortable.

### 2. Use evidence and forecasts

Distinguish baseline dates from actuals and current forecasts. Progress should reflect accepted deliverables or a defined weighting method rather than equal task counts when sizes differ. Explain the cause and consequence of a variance, not only its number. If information is missing, say so and assign an action to obtain it. A status report is a controlled summary, not a place to bury unresolved assumptions.

### 3. Make the decision request visible

A sponsor brief should show achievements, next milestones, top risks/issues and the decisions needed by a stated date. Use plain language and link to detailed records. A request such as “approve moving the pilot by two days or defer reminders by Thursday” is actionable. Avoid asking the sponsor to reconstruct the decision from ten separate attachments. Escalate consistently even when the news is unwelcome.

## Worked example

Ten tasks have equal counts but not equal weights: eight minor tasks represent 40% of planned effort and two testing tasks represent 60%. Closing the eight does not establish 80% delivery completion. If the policy is amber for a forecast delay of one to three working days, a two-day delay is amber on schedule, while a critical security defect may make the quality dimension red.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: weekly status report (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Define RAG thresholds for schedule, cost and risk in a classroom reporting policy.

2. Compare task-count and weighted-progress views using the worked example.

3. Write baseline, forecast and variance for three milestones.

4. Create a one-page update with one decision request and a response deadline.

5. Have a reviewer locate the main concern in thirty seconds; revise for clarity.

**Deliverable:** submit your weekly status report, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Averaging away a red critical risk.
- Using closed task counts as universal percent complete.
- Making a status report a list of activity with no decisions.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Define status before reporting it:** RAG means red, amber and green only within an agreed policy.
- **Use evidence and forecasts:** Distinguish baseline dates from actuals and current forecasts.
- **Make the decision request visible:** A sponsor brief should show achievements, next milestones, top risks/issues and the decisions needed by a stated date.

## Reflection journal

1. What did you change after checking the example or receiving a review comment? Name the exact input, statement or calculation.
2. Which assumption has the greatest effect on your recommendation, and how would you test it before a real decision?
3. How would you explain this work in a two-minute interview for Project Coordinator without claiming a classroom simulation was paid experience?

Write 80–120 words in English, Kannada or Hindi with clear technical labels. Keep a specific correction or decision in your portfolio journal.
