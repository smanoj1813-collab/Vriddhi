# 3.1 Agile values and choosing an approach

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Choose predictive, adaptive or hybrid planning based on uncertainty and feedback needs.
- Produce a reviewable delivery-approach decision note using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The event date is fixed, but the best student booking workflow is uncertain. A vendor wants every screen signed off immediately; students want to try a small prototype first. The team needs a reasoned delivery approach, not a contest over whether “Agile” is fashionable.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. Agile values are trade-offs, not bans

The Agile Manifesto values individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration and responding to change more highly than their paired items. It does not say that tools, documentation, contracts or plans have no value. In a college project, concise documentation can support continuity and accountability. The useful question is which practice helps the team learn and deliver value in this situation, not how many ceremonies it can perform.

### 2. Match planning to uncertainty

Predictive planning can suit well-understood work with stable acceptance conditions. Adaptive delivery suits uncertain needs where short feedback cycles can change the solution. A hybrid may fix procurement and safety approvals while developing the user workflow iteratively. Fixed dates do not eliminate learning, but learning does not eliminate constraints. Compare consequences explicitly instead of claiming one method works for every project.

### 3. Inspect evidence and adapt deliberately

An iteration should produce something that can be inspected, not just a presentation of activity. Use feedback to change the ordered work, assumptions or process. Agree what will be tested and with whom, then record what was learned. Retaining an unchanged plan after contradictory evidence is not disciplined delivery. Changing direction every hour without a goal is not agility either. Make changes visible and preserve a useful decision trail.

## Worked example

The team spends one week testing two booking flows with eight volunteer users, rather than building all requested screens. Six can complete Flow A without help; seven can complete Flow B. This small pilot suggests questions for the next iteration, not a statistically proven winner. The event-date and privacy constraints remain fixed while the workflow design can adapt.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: delivery-approach decision note (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. List what is fixed and what is uncertain in the placement pilot.

2. Compare predictive, adaptive and hybrid approaches against three case-specific criteria.

3. Design a one-week learning experiment with a feedback source and decision rule.

4. Explain how a short written decision log supports rather than contradicts agility.

5. Recommend an approach and state the evidence that would cause you to revisit it.

**Deliverable:** submit your delivery-approach decision note, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Claiming Agile means no documentation.
- Treating a small convenience sample as proof.
- Using changing priorities as an excuse to abandon a goal.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **Agile values are trade-offs, not bans:** The Agile Manifesto values individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration and responding to change more highly than their paired items.
- **Match planning to uncertainty:** Predictive planning can suit well-understood work with stable acceptance conditions.
- **Inspect evidence and adapt deliberately:** An iteration should produce something that can be inspected, not just a presentation of activity.
