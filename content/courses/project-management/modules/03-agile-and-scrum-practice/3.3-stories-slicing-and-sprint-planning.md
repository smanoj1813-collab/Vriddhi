# 3.3 Stories, slicing and Sprint planning

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read and discuss 25 min · Worked example 10 min · Hands-on lab 40 min · Quiz and reflection 15 min.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Create small valuable backlog items with acceptance criteria and plan within realistic capacity.
- Produce a reviewable ordered backlog and Sprint forecast using the supplied practice case.
- Explain one limitation, one verification check and the next decision the evidence supports.

## Opening scenario

The backlog contains “complete student portal” estimated as a single item. The team cannot inspect progress meaningfully until the final week. Meanwhile one of three members is away for training, but the Sprint forecast assumes everyone is available.

The running organisation, Namma Campus Services, is fictional. All dates, costs, workloads and stakeholders in these exercises are synthetic. Work with role names rather than real student details. The case is deliberately small enough to model in a spreadsheet, but its approval, scheduling and communication problems resemble those encountered in entry-level coordination roles.

Write your initial recommendation before analysing the case. Keep it for comparison after the lab: what did the evidence change, and why?

## Core ideas

### 1. A story is a conversation starter

A common user-story format names a user, a need and a reason. It is not a requirement to force all technical work into a fictional persona. Acceptance criteria clarify item-specific behaviour; the Definition of Done provides shared quality expectations across items. Include negative paths such as invalid input and cancelled bookings. An elegant sentence with no agreed examples is not ready merely because it follows the template.

### 2. Slice by value rather than only layers

A thin end-to-end slice lets a user achieve something and can be inspected. Splitting a feature into database, interface and report tasks may help implementation planning but can leave no usable outcome until all layers are complete. Reduce the breadth of a workflow while preserving the necessary quality. Ordering considers value, risk, dependencies and learning, not just the loudest requester. The Product Owner remains accountable for backlog ordering.

### 3. Forecast with capacity and past evidence

Story points are relative estimates within a team, not universal hours or measures of individual productivity. Velocity can help a stable team forecast; it should not be used to compare different teams. Account for leave, support work and uncertainty when selecting work. The Sprint Goal creates focus even if the exact scope is adjusted. A forecast is not a guarantee that justifies hiding unfinished work or sacrificing Done.

## Worked example

Three members have ten working days, but one is away for two days. There are 28 person-days before other commitments. If each person expects only five focused project hours per available day, capacity is 140 hours, not 240. This is a planning input, not a conversion formula from story points. Slice “portal” into a usable booking flow, cancellation flow and staff visibility rather than isolated technology layers.

### Reproduce before interpreting

Recreate the example from its inputs. Label units, show formulas or decision rules and keep an unchanged source copy. Change one assumption and identify which conclusion changes. Check one calculation or classification manually.

Test a relevant edge case: a missing date, zero denominator, incomplete observation or absent approval. Unknown information is not automatically zero or permission to invent a replacement. Record how the uncertainty affects your recommendation.

## Try it now

In pairs, explain the worked example without reading it aloud. The reviewer asks, “What evidence would change this recommendation?” Name the specific record, owner or observation you would request and how it could affect the decision. Swap roles and compare answers.

## Hands-on lab: ordered backlog and Sprint forecast (40 min)

Use the fictional case in this lesson. Do not contact real employees, candidates, customers or suppliers for these exercises. A spreadsheet, document and paper sketches are sufficient; named software is a convenience, not a requirement for marks.

1. Write six backlog items for booking, cancellation and staff oversight.

2. Add three acceptance examples to the highest-priority item, including a negative path.

3. Split one oversized item into two usable slices and justify the order.

4. Calculate available hours with the absence and an additional ten hours of support work.

5. Choose a Sprint Goal and forecast scope, leaving a clear explanation of uncertainty.

**Deliverable:** submit your ordered backlog and Sprint forecast, an assumptions note and a short recommendation. Use a descriptive filename containing the lesson number, your learner identifier and a revision number. Include the case period, units, source label “synthetic classroom case”, author and review date inside the artefact, not only in its filename.

### Lab review and acceptance

Reserve eight lab minutes for partner review. Trace one conclusion to its input, reproduce a calculation or classification and identify a possible misunderstanding. Keep the review comment and your correction or explanation in a two-column change log.

| Acceptance check | Evidence to leave in the submission |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is explicit | The question being answered and what is outside the exercise |
| Work can be reproduced | Source values, rules or formulas, and a manual check |
| Recommendation is actionable | Named role, next action and review trigger |
| Uncertainty is visible | An assumption and the effect if it proves false |
| Sharing is appropriate | Synthetic-data label and removal of unnecessary identifiers |

Working alone? Reopen the file as a reviewer. Check the version, evidence basis and distinction between observed facts and proposed actions.

## Common mistakes

- Converting every story point into a fixed hour.
- Comparing velocity across unrelated teams.
- Using capacity arithmetic as a promise of perfect utilisation.

Do not improve the appearance of a result by removing inconvenient records or changing an agreed definition halfway through the exercise. If the rule is wrong, propose a revision and show both versions. The goal is a defensible decision, not a perfect-looking chart or an artificially green status.

## Key takeaways

- **A story is a conversation starter:** A common user-story format names a user, a need and a reason.
- **Slice by value rather than only layers:** A thin end-to-end slice lets a user achieve something and can be inspected.
- **Forecast with capacity and past evidence:** Story points are relative estimates within a team, not universal hours or measures of individual productivity.
