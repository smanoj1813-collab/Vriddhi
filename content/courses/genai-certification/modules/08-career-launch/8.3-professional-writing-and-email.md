# 8.3 Professional writing & email with AI

> **Lesson plan** · 75 minutes — Read 25 · Lab 40 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Write clear workplace emails, reports, proposals and meeting minutes using structures that Indian workplaces expect.
- Use AI as a drafting and editing partner while keeping your own voice, judgement and accountability.
- Adapt tone for hierarchy, formality and audience — including bilingual and cross-cultural situations.
- Apply a pre-send discipline that prevents the most common professional-writing failures.

## Opening scenario

A new management trainee at a logistics firm in Hubballi is asked to "send the client an update on the delayed consignment". His first draft, generated in ten seconds, is 400 words, begins with "I hope this email finds you well", buries the new delivery date in paragraph four, and promises compensation nobody authorised. His manager rewrites it in five lines: what happened, the new date, what the firm is doing, one apology, one contact. The trainee learns the most important lesson of professional writing: **the reader's next action decides everything, and the writer is accountable for every word — including the ones an assistant wrote.**

## Core ideas

### 1. Writing for the reader's next action

Before writing anything, answer three questions: **Who is reading? What do they need to do or decide after reading? What is the one thing they must not miss?** Then put that thing first.

The universal structure for workplace messages — **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)**:

1. **Purpose/decision in the first line** ("The consignment will now arrive on Friday 3 October; no action needed from you.")
2. **Context** in two or three lines.
3. **What we are doing / what we need** as a short list.
4. **Close** with the next step, owner and date.

Indian workplaces vary in formality; hierarchy matters more in government, PSUs and traditional firms, less in start-ups. When in doubt, be slightly more formal, always polite, never obsequious. Use names and designations correctly (spellings of Indian names are a courtesy test). Avoid "revert back", "do the needful" and "kindly" chains — not because they are wrong, but because clear, direct requests are respected everywhere.

### 2. Email patterns you will use weekly

| Type | First line | Body | Close |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Request** | What you need and by when | Why (one line); what you have already done; options if any | "Could you confirm by Thursday? Happy to call if easier." |
| **Update / status** | Current status in one line (on track / delayed to X / done) | What changed; risks; what you need | Next update date |
| **Follow-up** | Reference the earlier message and date | Restate the ask in one line | A cheap yes ("Reply 1 for option A, 2 for B") |
| **Bad news** | The fact, plainly, with the new date/number | Cause (briefly, no blame); what you are doing; what you are not able to do | Apology once; contact; next update |
| **Thank-you / report-back** | What they did and what it led to | One specific result | Offer to help in return |
| **Escalation** | The issue and its impact in one line | What was tried; what decision is needed | Deadline for the decision and the consequence of no decision |

Subject lines: specific and searchable — "Consignment 4471: new ETA Fri 3 Oct" beats "Update".

### 3. Documents: reports, proposals, minutes

- **Report (1–3 pages):** Summary (three lines: question, answer, recommendation) → Background → Findings (with numbers and sources) → Options → Recommendation → Next steps. Charts from real data (Lesson 3.3). Sentence headings.
- **Proposal:** Lesson 5.4's structure — current state, problem, proposed change, return, risks and controls, pilot plan, ask. One page if possible.
- **Meeting minutes:** Date, attendees, decisions (numbered), action items (owner, date), open questions, next meeting. Circulate within 24 hours. If a meeting assistant drafted them, verify every decision and action against the recording or your notes before sending — invented action items are common (Lesson 4.3).
- **SOPs and how-tos:** numbered steps, one action per step, screenshots, "if this goes wrong" notes; test on a colleague who was not present.

### 4. AI as a writing partner — the discipline

**Drafting:**

- Give the assistant the BLUF answers, the facts, the reader and the constraints; ask for a draft *in your register*: "plain, direct, polite; no 'I hope this finds you well'; under 120 words."
- Provide two of your own past emails as style examples (few-shot, Lesson 2.3) to keep your voice.
- For sensitive messages (bad news, escalation, anything legal or financial), draft the facts yourself first; use the assistant only to tighten.

**Editing (often more valuable than drafting):**

```text
Edit this email for a busy [role]. Put the decision in line one. Cut to under
120 words. Keep my wording where it is clear; do not add promises, dates or
facts I have not written. Flag anything that could be read as a commitment.
```

Other useful editing prompts: "make this more formal for a government officer without making it longer"; "rewrite for a colleague who reads English as a second language"; "list every claim in this report that needs a source"; "what would a sceptical CFO ask after reading this?"

**Bilingual work:** draft in the language you think best in; translate with an assistant; have a native reader check anything going to a customer or an official; keep technical terms consistent (a glossary in your playbook). For Kannada/Hindi official correspondence, formal registers differ from spoken forms — ask specifically for the register.

**What the assistant must never do on its own:** make commitments (dates, money, discounts), state facts you have not verified, apologise on behalf of the organisation in ways policy does not allow, or add "warm" filler that changes the tone of a serious message.

### 5. Pre-send discipline

Sixty seconds before sending anything that matters:

- [ ] First line = the reader's most important information.
- [ ] Every date, number, name and designation verified (fact-check routine, Lesson 2.6).
- [ ] No commitments beyond your authority.
- [ ] Right recipients; reply vs reply-all; attachments attached; links tested.
- [ ] Tone read aloud — polite, direct, no AI tells, no sarcasm that will not survive a screenshot.
- [ ] Confidential or personal data appropriate for this channel and audience.
- [ ] AI disclosure where the context requires it (formal reports, academic work, when asked).

### 6. Accountability and voice

Whatever tool drafted it, **you signed it**. Over time, keep a personal style file: phrases you use, openings and closings you like, the register for each audience. Feed it to the assistant; edit toward it. Colleagues should be unable to tell which of your emails had AI help — because all of them sound like you.

## Try it now

Take the delayed-consignment scenario. Write the five-line BLUF email yourself in three minutes. Then ask an assistant to edit it with the prompt above. Did it try to add a promise or a "hope this finds you well"? Remove anything you did not authorise.

## Hands-on lab: five documents, one voice (40 min)

1. **Style file (5 min):** paste two of your real emails; ask the assistant to describe your register in five lines; save it in your playbook.
2. **Emails (15 min):** write a request, a bad-news update and a follow-up for a realistic situation in your target role; AI edits with your style file; pre-send checklist on each.
3. **Minutes (8 min):** record (or take notes on) a five-minute group discussion; produce minutes with decisions and action items; verify against notes.
4. **Bilingual (7 min):** produce a formal Kannada/Hindi/Tamil/Telugu version of the bad-news email for an official or a customer; native-reader check where possible.
5. **Report summary (5 min):** write the three-line summary (question, answer, recommendation) of your capstone or PB4 report.

**Deliverable:** style file, three emails with before/after edits, minutes, bilingual email, report summary. These are writing samples for your portfolio (anonymised).

## Common mistakes

- **Burying the decision.** Line one, always.
- **Letting the assistant promise things.** Flag commitments; remove what you cannot authorise.
- **Sending AI-drafted minutes unverified.**
- **Over-formality that becomes unclear** ("kindly do the needful at the earliest").
- **One register for everyone.** Officer, customer, peer, professor — adapt.

## Key takeaways

- Write for the reader's next action; BLUF: decision first, context, actions, close with owner and date.
- Learn the six email patterns and the structures for reports, proposals, minutes and SOPs.
- AI drafts from your facts and edits toward your style file; it never adds commitments, facts or tone you did not authorise; bilingual output gets a native check.
- Sixty-second pre-send discipline prevents most professional-writing failures.
- You sign it; you are accountable; keep your voice.
