# 7.1 AI ethics: principles & real failures

> **Lesson plan** · 75 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 35 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- State the FATE principles — Fairness, Accountability, Transparency, Ethics (including privacy, safety and human autonomy) — and apply them to a concrete system.
- Analyse five categories of real-world AI failure: hiring, lending, policing, exam proctoring and content moderation.
- Identify who bears harm, who holds power and where a human should have intervened in each case.
- Use a structured ethics analysis that you can run on any AI system, including the ones you have built in this course.

## Opening scenario

A final-year student applies to forty companies through a job portal. She is rejected within minutes by thirty-one, before any human could have read her application. She has no idea why. Later she learns from a placement officer that several companies use automated screening that scores resumes against "successful past employees" — most of whom, at those companies, were engineering graduates from a handful of cities. She is a B.A. graduate from Kalaburagi. Nobody decided to exclude her. The system learned to.

This module is not about making you afraid of AI. It is about making you the person in the room who asks the right questions — because most organisations have nobody in that seat, and it is increasingly a job.

## Core ideas

### 1. FATE, in plain language

| Principle | The question it asks | Looks like in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fairness** | Does the system treat people or groups differently in ways that are unjustified? | Testing outcomes across gender, caste, region, language, disability, age; fixing gaps; not using proxies for protected traits |
| **Accountability** | Who is responsible when it goes wrong, and can the affected person get a remedy? | A named owner; an appeal route to a human; logs; consequences |
| **Transparency** | Do people know an AI is involved, what it does, and why it produced this result? | Disclosure; understandable explanations; documentation (model cards, Lesson 7.3) |
| **Ethics** (privacy, safety, autonomy, dignity) | Does the system respect people's data, safety, choice and dignity — even when it is legal? | Data minimisation; consent; refusing harmful uses; letting people opt out; not manipulating |

These overlap deliberately. A hiring model that cannot be explained (transparency) cannot be checked for bias (fairness), which means nobody can be held responsible (accountability), which harms dignity (ethics).

Indian framing worth knowing: NITI Aayog's "Responsible AI for All" principles (safety and reliability, equality, inclusivity and non-discrimination, privacy and security, transparency, accountability, protection of positive human values) map closely onto FATE; the DPDP Act adds legal weight on the privacy side (Lesson 7.4).

### 2. Where harm comes from

Three sources recur in almost every failure:

1. **Data** — historical records encode historical discrimination; some groups are under-represented; labels were assigned by biased humans; proxies (pin code, college name, language) stand in for protected traits.
2. **Design** — the objective was the wrong thing to optimise (clicks, speed, cost) or the deployment context differs from the training context; no appeal route; no human checkpoint where the stakes demanded one.
3. **Use** — automation bias (humans defer to the machine), scale (one wrong rule applied to millions instantly), mission creep (a tool built for one purpose used for another), and absence of monitoring after launch.

Note which are *technical* and which are *organisational*. Most are organisational. That is why non-technical professionals matter here.

### 3. Five failure categories

**Hiring.** Resume screeners trained on past hires have learned to down-rank women's colleges, non-traditional career paths and non-English names; video-interview tools have scored candidates on facial expressions and accents with no proven link to job performance. Harm: exclusion at scale, invisible to the excluded. Where a human should have intervened: before deployment (outcome testing by group), and with a mandatory human review of rejections above a threshold.

**Lending and insurance.** Credit-scoring models using phone metadata, shopping patterns or location have penalised people from poorer neighbourhoods and informal workers — proxies for caste, religion and class in India. Harm: denial of credit without explanation, feedback loops (no credit → no credit history → no credit). Intervention: prohibition of proxy variables, explanation rights, human appeal.

**Policing and surveillance.** Predictive policing sends officers to areas with more *recorded* crime, producing more recordings — a self-fulfilling loop; facial recognition has higher error rates for darker skin and women, leading to wrongful detentions; mass CCTV with recognition chills lawful assembly. Harm: liberty, dignity, disproportionate impact on already-policed communities. Intervention: independent audits, strict use limits, human verification before any action, and sometimes not deploying at all.

**Exam proctoring.** Automated proctoring during online exams has flagged students for "suspicious" behaviour that included looking away, having a family member walk past in a one-room home, poor lighting on darker skin, or unstable internet — and required intrusive access to bedrooms and devices. Harm: false accusations, stress, privacy invasion, disproportionate impact on poorer students. Intervention: human review of every flag before consequences, alternatives for students without private space, minimal data capture.

**Content moderation and recommendation.** Moderation systems trained mostly on English miss abuse in Kannada, Tamil or Hinglish while over-removing legitimate speech in those languages; recommendation systems optimise for engagement and amplify outrage and misinformation; moderators (often in India) absorb the psychological cost. Harm: silenced communities, polarisation, worker trauma. Intervention: language-specific evaluation, appeal routes, objective functions that include harm, worker protections.

### 4. A structured ethics analysis

Use this on any system — including your capstone:

1. **System and purpose.** What does it do, for whom, deciding or influencing what?
2. **Stakeholders.** Who uses it, who is subject to it, who profits, who is excluded, who cleans up?
3. **Harms.** For each stakeholder: what could go wrong, how severe, how reversible, how likely, and does it fall on those already disadvantaged?
4. **Sources.** Data, design or use — for each harm.
5. **FATE check.** One sentence per principle: how the system does or does not meet it.
6. **Human intervention points.** Where should a person review, appeal, override or stop it?
7. **Alternatives.** Could a simpler, less automated or no-AI approach achieve the purpose with less harm?
8. **Decision and conditions.** Proceed, proceed with conditions, redesign, or do not build — and who is accountable.

Write the result as a one-page memo. The ability to produce this memo is a hireable skill in compliance, HR, operations, product and public administration.

### 5. Your own systems

Turn the analysis on this course: the Placement Coach (5.1), the registration triage automation (5.3), the mess-issue app (5.5), your capstone. Who could be harmed by a wrong classification, a leaked photo, a confident wrong answer about eligibility? Where is the human? The honest answer is often "we did not think about it" — which is the beginning of doing better.

## Try it now

Pick one automated decision that has affected you or someone you know (a loan app, a job portal, an exam proctor, a social media takedown, a ride or delivery algorithm). Run steps 1–3 of the analysis in ten minutes. Who bore the harm? Could they appeal to a human?

## Hands-on lab: case analysis and memo (35 min)

1. **Choose a case (5 min):** one of the five categories; find one real, documented incident (search, then open primary sources — news reports, regulator findings, company statements). Note the source.
2. **Analyse (20 min):** complete all eight steps of the structured analysis. Use an assistant to draft the stakeholder map and to argue the *opposite* position ("defend the deployment") so your memo answers the strongest counter-argument.
3. **Memo (10 min):** one page: what happened, who was harmed, why (data/design/use), FATE assessment, what should have been different, what you would recommend to a manager considering a similar system in India.

**Deliverable:** the memo with sources. Bring it to the next session — pairs will swap and critique for missing stakeholders and unsupported claims. Keep the eight-step template in your playbook; PB5 uses it.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating ethics as "be nice".** It is an analysis of harm, power and remedy, with a decision at the end.
- **Blaming "the algorithm".** Name the organisational decisions: objective, data, deployment, absence of appeal.
- **Only listing harms to users.** Excluded people, workers and communities matter.
- **Using an assistant's case summary without opening sources.** AI ethics case descriptions are frequently garbled or invented.
- **Concluding "ban AI".** The useful conclusion is conditions: testing, human checkpoints, appeal, monitoring — or a documented decision not to deploy.

## Key takeaways

- FATE: Fairness, Accountability, Transparency, Ethics (privacy, safety, autonomy, dignity); India's Responsible AI principles align with it.
- Harm comes from data, design and use — mostly organisational choices, which non-technical professionals can influence.
- Hiring, lending, policing, proctoring and moderation failures share patterns: proxies, wrong objectives, no appeal, scale, no monitoring, disproportionate impact.
- The eight-step ethics analysis produces a one-page memo with a decision and conditions — a hireable skill.
- Run it on your own builds first.
