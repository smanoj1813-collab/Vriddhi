# 7.3 Explainability, transparency & model cards

> **Lesson plan** · 75 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 35 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain why "black box" decisions are a business, legal and ethical risk, and what kinds of explanation different stakeholders need.
- Describe, without mathematics, how feature-importance methods such as LIME and SHAP produce explanations — and their limits.
- Distinguish a language model's *explanation of its answer* from an actual account of how it produced it.
- Read a model card or system card and extract what matters for a decision to adopt a tool.
- Write a simple system card for something you built.

## Opening scenario

A cooperative bank in Shivamogga adopts an AI credit-scoring service. A long-standing customer, a woman running a tailoring unit, is refused a working-capital loan. The branch manager cannot tell her why — the vendor says the model is proprietary. She complains to the ombudsman; the bank cannot produce an explanation; the case becomes a local news story about "computer says no". The bank's problem was never the model's accuracy. It was that nobody in the bank could explain, contest or correct a decision the bank was legally responsible for.

## Core ideas

### 1. Why black boxes are a business risk

- **Legal:** consumer-protection and data-protection frameworks increasingly expect that consequential automated decisions can be explained and contested; regulators in finance ask for model governance; the EU AI Act requires transparency for many uses (Lesson 7.4).
- **Operational:** if you cannot see why the model decides, you cannot debug it, detect drift, or know when it stopped working.
- **Trust:** customers, employees and partners accept decisions they understand — even negative ones — far more than opaque ones.
- **Accountability:** "the vendor's model did it" is not a defence; the deploying organisation is responsible.

### 2. Who needs what kind of explanation

| Stakeholder | Needs | Form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Affected person** | Why this outcome, what would change it, how to appeal | Plain language, specific, actionable: "Your application was declined mainly because of irregular deposits in the last six months; regular deposits for three months would change this." |
| **Operator / reviewer** | Which factors drove the output; confidence; when to override | Ranked factors, confidence, similar past cases |
| **Manager / auditor** | Does the system behave as intended across groups and over time | Aggregate reports, bias tests, drift monitoring |
| **Regulator** | Compliance with law and internal policy | Documentation: model/system cards, test records, incident logs |

A good explanation is not a technical dump; it is the right information for that stakeholder to act.

### 3. How explanation methods work (intuition)

For prediction models (credit, fraud, churn), two well-known families:

- **LIME** ("local" explanations): to explain one decision, the method makes many small changes to that one input — nudging income up, changing employment length — and watches how the prediction moves. It then fits a simple rule *around that case*: "for this applicant, deposit regularity mattered most, then loan size." It explains one decision at a time, and different runs can give slightly different answers.
- **SHAP** (contribution scores): borrows an idea from game theory — each input feature is a "player", and the method computes how much each contributed to moving the prediction away from the average, across all combinations of features. It gives consistent per-feature contributions for one decision and can be aggregated to show which features matter most overall.

What to remember without the maths: **these methods tell you which inputs moved the output, not whether the inputs were legitimate.** If pin code is the top factor, the explanation has revealed a proxy problem, not solved it. And they explain the *model*, not the *world*: "pin code mattered" is a statement about the model's behaviour, not a reason the applicant is risky.

Simpler tools are often better: **interpretable models** (a short list of rules, a points-based scorecard) are used in regulated lending precisely because everyone can read them. Ask vendors why a black box is necessary at all.

### 4. Language models "explaining themselves"

When you ask a chatbot "why did you say that?", it generates a *plausible explanation text* — it is not reading out its internal process. The explanation can be fluent and wrong, and can differ from the factors that actually drove the answer. Consequences:

- Treat self-explanations as **hypotheses to check**, not evidence.
- For grounded systems (RAG), the meaningful explanation is the **retrieved sources** — click them.
- For classification steps in automations, use **structured outputs with reasons tied to input fields** ("category: complaint; because: contains 'refund' and 'not working'") and validate that the cited words exist in the input.
- **Visible step-by-step reasoning** (Lesson 2.3) helps you check logic and inputs; it is still a generated text, not a trace.

### 5. Reading model cards and system cards

A **model card** documents a model: intended uses, out-of-scope uses, training data description, evaluation results (including by group and language), known limitations, safety testing, and ethical considerations. A **system card** documents a product built on models: components, safeguards, red-teaming results, monitoring, incident response. Most major model providers publish them; many no-code tools do not, which is itself information.

Reading checklist for adoption decisions:

- [ ] **Intended and out-of-scope uses** — is our use listed, or excluded?
- [ ] **Evaluations** — on which tasks, which languages (is Kannada/Hindi/Tamil/Telugu evaluated at all?), which groups; are the numbers recent?
- [ ] **Known limitations** — hallucination rates, failure modes, sensitive topics.
- [ ] **Safety and bias testing** — what was tested, what was found, what was mitigated.
- [ ] **Data** — what training data is described; is customer data used for training; retention.
- [ ] **Monitoring and incidents** — how problems are reported and fixed; is there a contact?
- [ ] **Gaps** — what is not said. Silence about Indian languages, children, or high-stakes uses is a red flag.

Write a half-page "adoption note" from the checklist: fit, risks, conditions, decision. Managers will thank you.

### 6. Transparency in your own builds

For every assistant, automation or app you deploy:

- **Disclose** that AI is involved and what it does (interface text, message footer).
- **Explain outputs** in the stakeholder's terms (sources for answers; input-tied reasons for classifications; "AI-suggested, editable" labels).
- **Document** with a simple system card (below).
- **Provide a route** to a human and to correction.

## Hands-on lab: adoption note and a system card for your build (35 min)

**Part A — Adoption note (15 min)**

1. Choose a tool your capstone or PB4 depends on.
2. Read its card or documentation with the checklist; note gaps.
3. Write the half-page adoption note: fit, risks, conditions (tests you will run, uses you will exclude), decision.

**Part B — System card for your build (20 min)**

Complete this template for your capstone, PB4 or Placement Coach:

```text
SYSTEM CARD — [name], version [x], date

Purpose and users: what it does; for whom; what it must not be used for.
Components: models/tools used; data store; automations; knowledge sources.
Data: what is collected; what reaches AI steps; retention; access.
How outputs are produced: prompt summary; validation; fallbacks.
Evaluation: accuracy on [n] test cases; language review; bias test summary
(Lesson 7.2) with date.
Known limitations: where it fails; what it cannot do.
Safeguards: human checkpoints; blocked outputs; kill switch; rate limits.
Transparency to users: disclosure text; explanation of outputs; appeal route.
Monitoring and incidents: what is logged; who reviews; how to report a problem.
Owner and review date.
```

**Deliverable:** the adoption note and the system card. Both go into the capstone evidence pack and PB5.

## Common mistakes

- **Accepting "proprietary" as an answer** to "why did it decide this?" The deploying organisation is responsible.
- **Treating a chatbot's self-explanation as a trace.** It is generated text; check it.
- **Reading a model card for reassurance instead of gaps.** The silences matter most.
- **Confusing "which inputs mattered" with "the decision was justified".** Explanations can reveal proxies.
- **Skipping the system card because the build is small.** Small systems get adopted and grow.

## Key takeaways

- Opaque decisions are legal, operational, trust and accountability risks; the deployer is responsible.
- Different stakeholders need different explanations — the affected person needs plain, specific, actionable reasons and an appeal route.
- LIME and SHAP show which inputs moved a prediction; they reveal proxies rather than legitimacy; interpretable models are often the better choice.
- A language model's self-explanation is generated text; rely on sources, input-tied reasons and validation instead.
- Read model/system cards with a checklist, looking for gaps (especially Indian languages); write adoption notes and system cards for your own builds.
