# 2.5 Research & writing assistants

> **Lesson plan** · 75 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 30 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Distinguish three kinds of research tool — general chat, search-grounded answer engines, and source-grounded notebooks — and pick the right one.
- Run a research workflow that ends with sources you have personally opened and verified.
- Use a notebook tool (NotebookLM or equivalent) to interrogate your own readings, generate study guides and produce audio overviews.
- Cite AI assistance and sources honestly, and explain the difference between paraphrase, plagiarism and fabrication.

## Opening scenario

A BA student is asked for a 1,500-word review of the literature on women's self-help groups in Karnataka. She asks a chat assistant for "ten recent studies" and receives ten confident citations. Following Lesson 1.4, she checks them: four are real, three are real papers with the wrong year or journal, and three do not exist. She then switches approach: an answer engine that searches and cites gives her eleven live links; she opens each, keeps six that are actually about Karnataka, uploads those PDFs into a notebook tool and asks questions *only of those documents*. Her review is now built on things she has read. It takes an extra hour and saves her degree.

## Core ideas

### 1. Three kinds of research tool

| Kind | Examples | What it does | Trust level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **General chat assistant** | ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot (without search) | Answers from training patterns. | Good for explanation and structure; **do not use for citations**. |
| **Search-grounded answer engine** | Perplexity, ChatGPT/Gemini/Copilot with search on, Google's AI features | Searches the live web, synthesises, shows links. | Good for discovery and recency; **open every link you rely on**. Synthesis can still misread a source. |
| **Source-grounded notebook** | NotebookLM, chat-with-PDF features, Claude/ChatGPT Projects with files | Answers *only* from documents you upload, with citations to passages. | Highest — but only as good as the documents you chose. It can still misinterpret a passage. |

The professional workflow moves left to right: **understand** with chat → **discover** with search → **verify and analyse** with a notebook.

### 2. A research workflow that survives scrutiny

1. **Frame the question** with a chat assistant: "Help me turn this broad topic into three answerable research questions and list the key terms and synonyms I should search."
2. **Discover** with a search-grounded tool: ask for recent, credible sources; ask it to prioritise peer-reviewed work, government data (MoSPI, RBI, NITI Aayog, Karnataka government portals) and reputable Indian institutions.
3. **Open every source.** Read at least the abstract, the method and the conclusion. Discard anything that is not what the summary claimed. Keep a source log: *title · authors · year · link · what it actually says · quality (peer-reviewed? data-based?)*.
4. **Load the keepers into a notebook tool.** Ask questions of the collection: "Where do these sources disagree about X?", "Which source has the most recent data on Y?", "Quote the passage that supports Z, with the source name."
5. **Draft with a chain** (Lesson 2.3): extract claims with references → structure themes → write with in-text citations *only from your log*.
6. **Cross-check the draft**: "List every claim in this draft that is not directly supported by the uploaded sources." Fix or remove each one.

### 3. Getting the most from a notebook tool

NotebookLM (free with a Google account) is the reference example; the ideas transfer to similar features elsewhere.

- **Add sources deliberately:** PDFs, Google Docs, slides, web pages, YouTube transcripts, your own typed notes. Up to a generous limit per notebook — but quality beats quantity.
- **Ask grounded questions.** Every answer carries numbered citations that open the exact passage. Click them; that is the whole point.
- **Generate study assets:** study guide, FAQ, briefing document, timeline, table of contents. These are drafted only from your sources and are excellent for exam preparation.
- **Audio Overview:** two AI hosts discuss your sources as a podcast-style conversation. Useful for revision on a bus; not a substitute for reading. Module 4 returns to this.
- **Mind maps and notes:** save good answers as notes and build a map of the topic.

Limitations to remember: it answers from what you gave it, so a bad or biased source set produces confident, well-cited nonsense; scanned PDFs with poor text layers may be misread; and it will not tell you what *exists outside* your sources.

### 4. Deep-research modes

Several assistants now offer a "deep research" mode that browses dozens of pages for several minutes and returns a long, cited report. Treat it as a fast research assistant who has not yet earned your trust:

- Read the report *as a map*: it tells you what sources exist and how the topic is structured.
- Open the sources that matter. Reports occasionally cite pages that do not say what the report claims.
- Ask it to separate "well-established", "contested" and "single-source" claims.
- Never submit it. Use it to plan what you will read.

### 5. Citing honestly

Three different things that get confused:

- **Paraphrase** — restating a source's idea in your words, with a citation. Legitimate.
- **Plagiarism** — presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own, without attribution. Includes AI-generated text passed off as your analysis.
- **Fabrication** — citing sources that do not exist or do not say what you claim. The AI-era version of the oldest academic offence.

Rules that keep you safe:

1. Cite only what you have opened and read.
2. Quote exactly, from the source, never from the assistant's memory.
3. Disclose AI assistance in an AI-use statement (Lesson 1.4). Some universities and journals now specify a format; use theirs when one exists.
4. Keep your source log and your chat links — if a claim is questioned, you can show your trail.

## Try it now

Pick a topic from your current coursework and run the first two steps of the workflow:

```text
(Chat assistant)
My broad topic is "[topic]". Turn it into 3 specific research questions suitable
for a 1,500-word undergraduate review. For each, list 5 search terms including
Indian and Karnataka-specific variants. Suggest 3 types of credible source
(e.g. government portals, peer-reviewed journals, institutional reports) and
name one example of each that I should look for.
```

Then, in a search-grounded tool:

```text
Find recent (last 5 years) credible sources on "[research question 1]" with a
focus on India / Karnataka. Prefer peer-reviewed articles, government data and
institutional reports. For each: title, authors, year, one-line summary, link,
and why it is credible. Mark anything that is a news article or blog as such.
```

Open at least three links. Do they say what the summary claimed?

## Hands-on lab: build a verified source set (30 min)

1. **Discover (10 min).** Using the research question you just framed, collect 8–10 candidate sources with an answer engine.
2. **Verify (10 min).** Open each. Keep only those you have actually read the abstract/summary of and that are relevant. Fill your source log. Note how many candidates were discarded and why.
3. **Interrogate (10 min).** Upload the keepers to a notebook tool. Ask three questions: where they agree, where they disagree, and which single passage best supports your main point (quote it with the citation). Generate a study guide.

**Deliverable:** your source log (candidates vs keepers with reasons), the three notebook answers with citations, and a two-line note on how the notebook's answers compared with what you read yourself.

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping at the search engine's summary.** The summary is a pointer, not a source.
- **Filling a notebook with anything that mentions the topic.** Curate first; the notebook amplifies whatever you give it.
- **Copying the audio overview's phrasing into an essay.** It is a revision aid, not a source.
- **Citing the AI tool itself as a source** for a fact. Cite the underlying document; disclose the tool separately.

## Key takeaways

- Chat for understanding, search-grounded tools for discovery, source-grounded notebooks for verification and analysis.
- Open every source you rely on; keep a source log with your own reading notes.
- Notebook tools give cited answers from your documents — excellent for study guides, FAQs and cross-source comparison; only as good as the curation.
- Deep-research reports are maps, not submissions.
- Paraphrase with citation is legitimate; plagiarism and fabrication are not; disclose AI assistance.

## Reflection journal

Write 150 words: *How many of your candidate sources survived verification? What does that ratio tell you about relying on summaries? How will this change your next assignment?*

## Going further (optional)

- Look up your university's or a target journal's stated policy on citing AI tools and note the required format.
- Try the same research question in two answer engines and compare the overlap in sources — a rough measure of how complete either one is.
