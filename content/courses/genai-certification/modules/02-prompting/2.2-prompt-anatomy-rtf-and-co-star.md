# 2.2 Prompt anatomy: RTF and CO-STAR

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Name the six building blocks of a strong prompt and explain what each one changes in the output.
- Write quick prompts with **RTF** (Role – Task – Format) and professional prompts with **CO-STAR** (Context – Objective – Style – Tone – Audience – Response).
- Run the iterate-and-diagnose loop instead of rewriting a prompt from scratch.
- Convert a weak prompt into a strong one and explain each change.

## Opening scenario

A placement cell asks two volunteers to draft an email inviting alumni to a mentoring evening.

Volunteer A: *"Write an email inviting alumni to a mentoring event."* Result: a generic, slightly American email with "Dear Alumni" and a made-up date.

Volunteer B: *"You are the placement coordinator of a government first-grade college in Shivamogga. Write a warm, concise email (under 180 words) inviting alumni who graduated between 2015 and 2022 to a Saturday-evening mentoring session on 18 October, 5–7 pm, in the seminar hall. Objective: get them to fill the RSVP form (link placeholder). Tone: respectful but friendly, Indian English, no exclamation marks. Include a subject line and a two-line PS about the tea and networking afterwards."*

The second prompt took ninety seconds longer to write and saved forty minutes of editing. Prompt engineering is simply the habit of writing the second kind on purpose.

## Core ideas

### 1. The six building blocks

Almost every effective prompt is some combination of these:

| Block | Question it answers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Role** | Who should the model be? | "You are an experienced Karnataka GST practitioner." |
| **Context** | What background does it need? | "The client is a small saree retailer with ₹40 lakh turnover." |
| **Task** | What exactly should it do? | "Explain whether they must register under GST and list the steps." |
| **Constraints** | What limits apply? | "Under 200 words, plain English, cite the threshold." |
| **Format** | What should the output look like? | "A numbered list followed by a two-line summary." |
| **Examples** | What does good look like? | "Here is a previous explanation the client liked: …" |

You do not always need all six. The skill is knowing which ones the task requires. A caption needs Task + Constraints + Format; a client report needs all six.

### 2. RTF — for quick, everyday prompts

**Role → Task → Format.** Three lines, thirty seconds. Use it for anything low-stakes.

```text
Role: You are a careful English editor for Indian college students.
Task: Fix grammar and tighten this paragraph without changing its meaning or my voice.
Format: Return the corrected paragraph, then a 3-bullet list of the main changes.

[paragraph]
```

RTF prompts are ideal for the "Automate" zone from Lesson 1.4: fast, cheap, easy to check.

### 3. CO-STAR — for professional deliverables

CO-STAR is a framework developed for government communications work in Singapore and widely adopted since. It forces you to think about the reader, which is where most prompts fail.

- **C — Context:** the situation and background the model cannot know.
- **O — Objective:** what the output must achieve (not just what it is).
- **S — Style:** the writing style — analytical, narrative, bullet-led, in the manner of a policy brief.
- **T — Tone:** the emotional register — formal, warm, urgent, neutral.
- **A — Audience:** who reads it and what they already know.
- **R — Response:** the exact format and length.

Worked example — a BBA student preparing a market summary:

```text
# CONTEXT
I am a second-year BBA student doing a summer project with a Mysuru-based
handloom cooperative that sells silk sarees online and through two shops.
Sales to buyers under 30 are falling. I have interviewed 12 customers
(notes below).

# OBJECTIVE
Produce a one-page summary of what the interviews reveal about younger buyers,
so the cooperative's management committee can decide whether to invest in
Instagram marketing.

# STYLE
Analytical and evidence-led. Quote the interviews where useful. No marketing
jargon.

# TONE
Neutral and respectful — the committee members are weavers and elders, not
MBA graduates.

# AUDIENCE
A 7-member management committee; comfortable in Kannada and English; sceptical
of social media.

# RESPONSE
Three headed sections: "What younger buyers told us", "What it means",
"Two options with rough costs". Maximum 350 words. Then a 5-line Kannada
summary of the key finding.

# INTERVIEW NOTES
[paste notes]
```

Compare this with "summarise my interviews". The CO-STAR version produces something you could actually hand over.

### 4. The iterate-and-diagnose loop

A first output is a draft. Professionals improve it with *specific* follow-ups rather than rewriting the prompt:

1. **Diagnose** — what exactly is wrong? Too long? Wrong audience? A missing point? A factual doubt?
2. **Instruct the fix** — "Cut section 2 by half and replace the jargon in section 3 with plain words."
3. **Ask for alternatives** — "Give me two different openings: one with a statistic, one with a customer quote."
4. **Lock what works** — "Keep sections 1 and 3 exactly as they are; only revise section 2."
5. **Save the final prompt** — once you are happy, ask: *"Rewrite my original prompt so that it would have produced this final version in one go."* That rewritten prompt goes into your library (Lesson 2.6).

### 5. Small techniques with big effects

- **Delimiters.** Wrap pasted material in clear markers (`"""`, `---`, or headed sections as above) so the model knows what is instruction and what is data.
- **Say what to avoid.** "Do not invent statistics. If a figure is unknown, write [DATA NEEDED]."
- **Ask for structure before content.** "First give me the outline; wait for my approval before writing."
- **Specify the reader's knowledge.** "Assume the reader has never heard of a balance sheet" changes everything.
- **Define length precisely.** "Under 150 words" beats "short". "Three bullet points of one line each" beats "a few points".
- **Bilingual output.** "Write in English, then give the key sentence in Kannada" works well across assistants.

## Try it now

Take this weak prompt and rebuild it as RTF, then as CO-STAR:

> *"Make a presentation about climate change for my class."*

Suggested CO-STAR reconstruction to compare with yours:

```text
CONTEXT: I am a first-year B.Sc student presenting to 40 classmates in Belagavi;
we have covered basic atmospheric science but not policy.
OBJECTIVE: The class should leave able to explain three local impacts of climate
change on North Karnataka agriculture and one adaptation strategy.
STYLE: Clear, example-driven, one idea per slide.
TONE: Serious but hopeful.
AUDIENCE: Science undergraduates, mixed English comfort.
RESPONSE: An outline of 8 slides: title + 3 bullet points + one suggested
visual per slide. Flag any statistic I must verify with [VERIFY].
```

## Hands-on lab: prompt makeover clinic (45 min)

1. **Collect three weak prompts (5 min)** — your own from last week's chats. Everyone has them.
2. **Rebuild each (20 min):** one as RTF, two as CO-STAR. Run the before and after versions.
3. **Diagnose and iterate (10 min)** on the CO-STAR outputs with at least two specific follow-ups each. Use "lock what works".
4. **Extract the reusable prompt (10 min)** by asking the assistant to rewrite your prompt for one-shot use. Save all three finals with a one-line note on when to use them.

**Deliverable:** a document with, for each prompt: weak version → strong version → final output → the one-shot prompt → a two-line note on what changed the result most. This is the first entry in your Project Block 1 playbook.

## Common mistakes

- **Role without context.** "You are an expert" does little on its own; the expert still needs the situation.
- **Objective confused with task.** The task is "write an email"; the objective is "get 30 RSVPs". Stating the objective lets the model make better choices.
- **Over-long prompts for tiny jobs.** CO-STAR for a two-line caption is wasted effort; use RTF.
- **Rewriting from scratch every time.** Iterate; the conversation is the tool.

## Key takeaways

- Six blocks: Role, Context, Task, Constraints, Format, Examples — choose the ones the task needs.
- **RTF** for quick, low-stakes prompts; **CO-STAR** for anything a reader will judge you by.
- Iterate with specific diagnoses; lock what works; extract a one-shot prompt at the end.
- Delimiters, "what to avoid", outline-first, reader knowledge, precise length and bilingual output are cheap, high-impact habits.
