# 2.3 Advanced prompting patterns

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 35 · Lab 40 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Apply seven named prompting patterns — few-shot, step-by-step reasoning, step-back, persona panel, self-critique, output templating and prompt chaining — and explain when each helps.
- Choose the right pattern for a task using the decision table.
- Build a three-step prompt chain for a realistic assignment.
- Recognise when a pattern is overkill or actively harmful.

## Opening scenario

Kavya needs to classify 60 customer complaints from her father's mobile-repair shop into categories so he can see what goes wrong most. Her first attempt — "categorise these complaints" — produces categories that change halfway through the list. Her second attempt shows the assistant five complaints already labelled the way she wants, asks it to reason briefly before each label, and demands the output as a table with fixed category names. The result is consistent, and she can paste it straight into a spreadsheet.

Kavya used three patterns without knowing their names: **few-shot**, **step-by-step reasoning** and **output templating**. This lesson gives you the names, the mechanics and the judgement about when to use them.

## Core ideas

### Pattern 1 — Few-shot examples

Show, do not just tell. Two to five examples of input → desired output teach the model your standard better than a paragraph of description.

```text
Classify each complaint as one of: SCREEN, BATTERY, SOFTWARE, WATER, OTHER.

Complaint: "Phone shuts off at 30% battery." → BATTERY
Complaint: "Cracked after falling from bike." → SCREEN
Complaint: "Apps keep crashing since update." → SOFTWARE
Complaint: "Dropped in a bucket while washing." → WATER

Now classify:
1. "Touch not working on the left side."
2. "Charging takes 5 hours."
...
```

**Use when:** you need consistent format, style or categories; the task is hard to describe but easy to demonstrate (tone, labelling, data extraction).
**Watch for:** examples that are too similar (the model over-copies) and examples that accidentally contain the answer pattern you want to avoid.

### Pattern 2 — Step-by-step reasoning ("think before you answer")

Asking the model to work through a problem before giving the final answer improves accuracy on multi-step tasks: calculations, logic, policy interpretation, planning.

```text
A trader in Karnataka sells goods worth ₹12,50,000 in a year, all within the
state. Decide whether GST registration is mandatory. Work through it step by
step: (1) the relevant threshold, (2) whether any exception applies, (3) the
conclusion. Only then give a one-line answer. If you are unsure of a threshold,
say so instead of guessing.
```

Many current models reason internally by default ("thinking" modes). Asking for visible steps still helps *you*: you can check the reasoning, which is the point.

**Use when:** the answer depends on several facts or rules; you need to verify the logic; the stakes are medium to high.
**Watch for:** reasoning that *looks* rigorous but starts from a wrong fact — verify inputs, not just steps.

### Pattern 3 — Step-back prompting

Before the specific question, ask the general principle. It pulls the model toward fundamentals and away from surface pattern-matching.

```text
First, explain the general principles that determine whether a marketing
message works for first-time buyers of any product. Then, using those
principles, evaluate this Instagram caption for a Mysuru silk brand: "..."
```

**Use when:** evaluating or designing something; the model keeps giving shallow, generic feedback.

### Pattern 4 — Persona panel

Ask for several distinct perspectives on the same input. Excellent for finding weaknesses.

```text
Review my business plan summary below from three perspectives, each in 80 words:
(a) a sceptical bank loan officer in Hubballi,
(b) a 22-year-old target customer,
(c) a chartered accountant worried about compliance.
End with the single biggest risk all three would agree on.
```

**Use when:** you need critique, not agreement; you are preparing for questions from a committee, an interviewer or a client.
**Watch for:** caricatures. Give each persona a realistic concern.

### Pattern 5 — Self-critique and revision

Have the model evaluate its own output against explicit criteria, then improve it. This directly counters sycophancy (Lesson 1.4).

```text
Here is your draft. Score it 1–5 on each criterion: (1) answers the question,
(2) evidence for every claim, (3) under 200 words, (4) tone appropriate for a
principal. For any criterion below 4, explain the gap and rewrite the draft to
fix it. Show the scores, then the revised draft only.
```

**Use when:** quality matters and you have clear criteria (a rubric, a brief, a style guide).
**Watch for:** the model giving itself 5/5 on everything — tighten the criteria or ask for "the harshest reasonable reviewer".

### Pattern 6 — Output templating

Give the exact skeleton you want filled. It removes ambiguity and makes outputs machine-readable — essential when the output goes into a spreadsheet, a form or an automation (Module 5).

```text
Return ONLY this template, filled in, with no extra text:

Title:
One-line summary:
Key points (exactly 3, one line each):
-
-
-
Risks or open questions:
Confidence (High / Medium / Low) and why:
```

For data, ask for CSV or JSON with named columns/keys and "no commentary". Then paste directly into Sheets.

**Use when:** consistency, comparison across many items, or downstream processing matters.

### Pattern 7 — Prompt chaining

Break a big job into steps, each with its own prompt, and feed the output of one into the next. Quality rises because each step is simple and checkable.

A three-step chain for a seminar paper:

1. **Extract** — "From the attached three sources, list every claim relevant to *X* with a page reference. No interpretation."
2. **Structure** — "Group these claims into 4–5 themes. For each theme, note where sources agree and disagree."
3. **Draft** — "Using ONLY the themes and claims above, write the literature-review section (600 words) with in-text citations in APA style. Do not add claims that are not in the list."

You verify after each step, which is far easier than verifying a finished 600-word section with invisible sources.

**Use when:** the task is long, needs verification, or combines research and writing.
**Watch for:** context loss — carry forward the outputs explicitly (paste them in) rather than assuming the model remembers.

### Choosing a pattern

| If your problem is… | Try |
| --- | --- |
| Inconsistent format or categories | Few-shot + output templating |
| Wrong answers on multi-step questions | Step-by-step reasoning (+ verify inputs) |
| Shallow, generic evaluation | Step-back or persona panel |
| Output sounds fine but you doubt it | Self-critique with a rubric |
| Task is too big to check | Prompt chaining |
| Output needs to feed a sheet or workflow | Output templating (CSV/JSON) |

### When patterns hurt

- Chain-of-thought on a one-line factual question wastes time and can *introduce* errors.
- Five personas on a caption produces noise.
- A rigid template can suppress a genuinely better structure — use it when consistency matters more than creativity.
- Chaining a task that takes two minutes to do yourself is procrastination with extra steps.

## Try it now

Run a mini-chain on something you are studying this week:

```text
Step 1: Here are my notes on [topic]. List every distinct concept as a one-line
bullet. Do not explain. Mark any that appear incomplete with [?].
```

```text
Step 2: Take the list above. For each concept, write one exam-style question
that would test understanding rather than recall, and label its difficulty
(easy / medium / hard). Return as a 3-column table.
```

```text
Step 3: Pick the three "hard" questions. For each, write a model answer of
80–100 words that a strict examiner would give full marks, then list the two
most common mistakes students make on it.
```

## Hands-on lab: pattern practice (40 min)

Work through four stations; spend about ten minutes on each.

1. **Few-shot station.** Take 15 real items (product reviews, complaints, student feedback, news headlines). Label 4 yourself, then have the assistant label the rest in a fixed template. Check every label.
2. **Reasoning station.** Pick a rule-based question from your discipline (a tax threshold, a grammar rule, a physics formula application, a citation rule). Ask once directly and once step-by-step. Compare correctness — and check the inputs.
3. **Critique station.** Paste a paragraph of your own writing. Run a persona panel of three realistic readers, then a self-critique against a four-point rubric. Revise.
4. **Chain station.** Build a three-step chain for a task you actually have due. Save each prompt.

**Deliverable:** for each station, the prompt(s), the output, and one sentence on whether the pattern helped, hurt or made no difference. Add the useful prompts to your playbook.

## Common mistakes

- **Stacking every pattern in one prompt.** Choose one or two that address the actual problem.
- **Examples with errors.** The model learns your mistakes faithfully. Check your few-shot examples twice.
- **Trusting the reasoning display.** Visible steps are for *your* verification, not proof of correctness.
- **Chains with no checkpoints.** The value of chaining is checking between steps. If you skip the checks, you have just made one long prompt slower.

## Key takeaways

- Seven patterns: few-shot, step-by-step, step-back, persona panel, self-critique, output templating, prompt chaining.
- Match the pattern to the problem: consistency, accuracy, depth, doubt, size, downstream use.
- Patterns are tools for *verification* as much as generation — chains and visible reasoning exist so you can check.
- Know when a pattern is overkill.
