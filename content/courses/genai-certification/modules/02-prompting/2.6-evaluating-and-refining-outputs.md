# 2.6 Evaluating & refining GenAI outputs

> **Lesson plan** · 60 minutes — Read 20 · Lab 30 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Run a five-minute fact-check routine on any AI output before you use it.
- Score outputs with a simple rubric and use the score to drive revision.
- A/B test two prompts fairly and keep the winner.
- Set up and maintain a personal prompt library that grows with your work.

## Opening scenario

An intern at a Bengaluru start-up is asked to prepare a one-page brief on a competitor. He uses an assistant, edits lightly, sends it. His manager replies within ten minutes: two of the "facts" were from a 2022 article and one product name was wrong. The brief was 90% good; the 10% cost him credibility. What his manager wanted was not a perfect first draft — it was evidence that the intern had *checked*. This lesson gives you that habit as a repeatable routine.

## Core ideas

### 1. The five-minute fact-check routine

Run this on anything that leaves your hands:

1. **Highlight every specific.** Numbers, dates, names, places, laws, prices, citations, quotations. These are where errors live.
2. **Rank by damage.** Which specifics would embarrass you most if wrong? Check those first.
3. **Verify against an independent source.** Official site, the original document, a spreadsheet recalculation, a second tool. Never the same tool's reassurance.
4. **Check the omissions.** Ask: "What did this leave out that the reader would expect?" — or ask the assistant that question directly.
5. **Read it aloud once.** Catches wrong tone, repeated phrases, and sentences that are grammatical but meaningless.

Five minutes for a one-page output. Scale up for longer work; never skip it entirely.

### 2. A rubric you can use on anything

Score each criterion 1–5. Anything below 4 gets a targeted revision prompt.

| Criterion | 5 looks like | Revision prompt if low |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Correct** | Every checkable specific verified; no invented sources. | "Remove or flag every claim you cannot support from the material I gave you." |
| **Complete** | Answers the whole brief; nothing the reader would expect is missing. | "List what a [reader] would expect here that is missing, then add it." |
| **Constraint-compliant** | Length, format, language, tone all as specified. | "You exceeded 200 words and used three exclamation marks. Fix both without losing point 2." |
| **Clear** | A reader with the stated knowledge understands it first time. | "Rewrite for a reader who has never heard of X; define terms on first use." |
| **Credible** | Sounds like a competent person, not a brochure or a bot. | "Cut generic phrases ('in today's fast-paced world'), hedge only where genuinely uncertain, prefer concrete examples." |

Ask the assistant to score itself with this rubric (self-critique, Lesson 2.3), then score it yourself. Where your scores differ, your judgement wins.

### 3. Spotting AI tells

Readers — including examiners and hiring managers — are increasingly good at spotting unedited AI text. Common tells to remove:

- Openers such as "In today's rapidly evolving landscape…" or "Certainly! Here is…"
- Triplets everywhere ("efficient, effective and engaging").
- Balanced non-answers ("there are both advantages and disadvantages").
- Over-hedging ("it is important to note that…").
- Bulleted lists where a paragraph was needed, or the reverse.
- Words the writer would never use in speech: "delve", "tapestry", "leverage", "utilise", "robust".
- Perfect grammar with no specific detail — no names, numbers, places or examples.

The fix is not to hide AI use (you disclose it anyway); it is to make the work *yours*: add your examples, your data, your opinion, your voice.

### 4. A/B testing prompts

When two prompt versions compete, test them fairly:

1. **Change one thing.** Role, or examples, or format — not all three.
2. **Same input, same tool, same session type** (fresh chats).
3. **Same rubric, scored before you know which is which** if you can (have a friend shuffle).
4. **Run it more than once.** Outputs vary; three runs each is a minimum for anything you will reuse often.
5. **Keep the winner, note why.** "Adding two examples raised constraint-compliance from 3 to 5" is knowledge you own.

### 5. Your prompt library

A prompt library is a living document of prompts that have *earned* their place. Keep it simple — a Google Doc or Sheet with one row per prompt:

| Field | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Name** | "Journal entries from narrative", "Bakery 90-day plan template". |
| **Use when** | The trigger situation. |
| **Prompt** | The final, one-shot version (Lesson 2.2). |
| **Pattern(s)** | Few-shot, chain step 2 of 3, etc. |
| **Best tool** | From your evidence log. |
| **Check afterwards** | The discipline check (Lesson 2.4). |
| **Last verified** | Date — prompts decay as tools change. |
| **Example output** | A link or a snippet that shows the standard. |

Rules that keep it useful:

- Only add prompts you have used at least twice successfully.
- Review monthly; delete what you no longer use; re-test what matters.
- Write prompts in a way a classmate could use without you — that is also how you will know they are clear.
- Version them: "v2 — added Kannada summary line".

By the end of Project Block 1 you will have ten. By the end of the course, thirty to fifty. That library is a portfolio piece in Module 8 and a genuine asset in your first job.

## Try it now

Take any output you produced this week and run the rubric on it — first your own scores, then the assistant's:

```text
Score the text below 1–5 on: Correct, Complete, Constraint-compliant (limits:
[list them]), Clear (reader: [describe]), Credible (no generic filler). For each
score below 4, quote the exact sentence that caused it and propose a fix. Then
list every specific fact, number or name in the text that I should verify, in
order of how damaging an error would be.

[text]
```

Compare the two sets of scores. Where do you disagree, and why?

## Hands-on lab: the evaluation sprint (30 min)

1. **Fact-check (10 min).** Take the longest AI output you have used so far in this course. Run the five-minute routine. Record every specific, whether you verified it, how, and the result.
2. **Rubric and revise (10 min).** Score it; write targeted revision prompts for anything under 4; revise; re-score.
3. **A/B test (10 min).** Choose one prompt from your playbook. Create a variant that changes exactly one thing. Run each twice on the same input; score with the rubric; keep the winner and record why.

**Deliverable:** the fact-check table, the before/after rubric scores, and the A/B result. Update your prompt library with the winner and its "check afterwards" field.

## Common mistakes

- **Verifying the easy specifics and skipping the hard ones.** Rank by damage first.
- **Letting the assistant's self-score stand.** It is a starting point for your own judgement.
- **A/B testing with two variables changed.** You will not know what worked.
- **A library that is a dump of every prompt you ever wrote.** Curate; it should fit on a few pages you actually open.

## Key takeaways

- Fact-check routine: highlight specifics → rank by damage → verify independently → check omissions → read aloud.
- Five-criterion rubric: Correct, Complete, Constraint-compliant, Clear, Credible — with a revision prompt for each.
- Remove AI tells by adding what is yours: examples, data, opinion, voice.
- A/B test one change at a time, more than once, with the same rubric.
- Maintain a curated, versioned prompt library — it is both a productivity tool and a portfolio asset.
