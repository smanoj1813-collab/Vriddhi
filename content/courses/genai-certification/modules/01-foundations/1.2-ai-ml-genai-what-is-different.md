# 1.2 AI, ML, deep learning & GenAI — what is actually different

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 35 · Lab 40 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Place artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning and generative AI in the correct relationship to each other.
- Explain how a large language model produces text, using the "next-word prediction" idea, and why that explains both its strengths and its mistakes.
- Distinguish between *discriminative* AI (that classifies or predicts) and *generative* AI (that creates), with everyday examples.
- Use the right vocabulary — model, prompt, token, context window, training data, fine-tuning — in a conversation with a colleague.

## Opening scenario

Arjun, a B.Sc student, asks a chat assistant for the boiling point of ethanol and gets the correct answer, 78.37 °C. Encouraged, he asks for five research papers on ethanol fuel blends in India, with authors and years. The assistant produces five neatly formatted citations. Three of them do not exist.

Same tool, two minutes apart, one flawless answer and one confident fabrication. If you understand *how* the tool works, this is not a mystery — it is exactly what you would expect. This lesson gives you that understanding, without any mathematics.

## Core ideas

### 1. Four nested circles

Think of four circles, each inside the previous one.

| Circle | What it means | Everyday example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Artificial Intelligence (AI)** | Any technique that makes a computer do something we would call "intelligent". | A chess program, a spam filter, Google Maps choosing a route. |
| **Machine Learning (ML)** | AI that learns patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. | A bank's model that scores loan applications from past repayment data. |
| **Deep Learning (DL)** | ML that uses very large layered networks ("neural networks") that can learn from raw text, images and sound. | Face unlock on your phone; voice recognition in Google Assistant. |
| **Generative AI (GenAI)** | Deep learning models trained to *produce* new content — text, images, audio, video, code. | ChatGPT writing an email; an image tool drawing a poster. |

The important line is between the third and fourth circles. Most AI you have used for years — recommendations, fraud alerts, autocorrect — is **discriminative**: it looks at an input and answers a narrow question (*spam or not? which product next?*). Generative AI is different in kind: given a prompt, it creates something that did not exist before.

### 2. How a language model actually works (the honest, non-mathematical version)

A **large language model (LLM)** is a program that has read an enormous quantity of text — a large part of the public internet, books, articles, code — and has learned one skill extraordinarily well: **given some text, predict what comes next.**

That is it. When you type *"Write a leave application to my HOD for a family function"*, the model does not look up a template. It generates the reply one small piece at a time (each piece is called a **token**, roughly three-quarters of a word), each time choosing a likely next token given everything so far — your prompt plus what it has already written.

Three consequences follow, and they explain almost everything you will see in this course:

1. **Fluency is guaranteed; truth is not.** The model is optimised to produce text that *reads* like the text it was trained on. When the truthful continuation and the plausible-sounding continuation are the same, you get a correct answer. When they differ — an obscure citation, a specific statistic, a recent event — you can get a **hallucination**: confident, well-formatted, wrong. Arjun's fake papers are the model doing its job perfectly; the job was just not "tell the truth".
2. **It knows patterns, not facts in a database.** Ask it the boiling point of ethanol and the pattern is so common in training data that it is reliable. Ask it your college's fee structure and there is no pattern to draw on, so it will improvise unless you give it the document.
3. **Context is everything.** The model can only use what is in front of it: your prompt, the conversation so far, any files you attach, and whatever it learned during training. This is why a detailed prompt with the right background produces dramatically better results — you are literally giving it more to predict from.

### 3. Vocabulary you will use every week

- **Model** — the trained system itself (for example, the current models behind ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude). New versions ship every few weeks; the skills in this course transfer across them.
- **Prompt** — everything you send to the model: instructions, context, examples, questions.
- **Token** — the unit of text the model reads and writes. Long documents cost more tokens; free tiers limit how many you can use.
- **Context window** — the maximum amount of text the model can consider at once. Older chats and very long files can fall outside it, which is why an assistant sometimes "forgets" what you said earlier.
- **Training data / cut-off** — what the model learned from and when that learning stopped. Anything after the cut-off is unknown unless the tool can search the web.
- **Fine-tuning** — additional training on specialised data to make a general model better at a narrow task (medical notes, legal drafting).
- **Multimodal** — a model that handles more than text: images, audio, video in and out. Most major assistants are now multimodal.
- **Agent** — a model given tools (web search, code execution, sending emails) and allowed to take several steps toward a goal. Module 5 and Module 6 use agent-like features.

### 4. Where the other GenAI types fit

The same "learn the pattern, then generate" idea powers every tool in this course:

- **Image generators** learn the relationship between captions and pictures, then start from noise and refine it toward an image that matches your prompt (a *diffusion* process — Module 3 explains the intuition).
- **Speech models** learn the relationship between text and the sound of voices, then generate audio for new text (Module 4).
- **Video models** extend image generation across time, which is why consistency between frames is still the hardest problem.

### 5. Why "just predicting the next word" is not a put-down

Sceptics like to say an LLM is "just autocomplete". Technically true and practically misleading. To predict the next token well across all of human writing, the model has had to internalise grammar, facts, styles, arguments, some logic and a great deal about how people explain things. That is why the same tool can draft a marketing plan, translate Kannada poetry and explain photosynthesis to a ten-year-old. The limitation is not that it is *simple*; it is that its goal is *plausibility*, and plausibility is not the same as correctness. Your job, throughout your career, is to supply the correctness.

## Try it now

Run these three prompts in the same chat and observe how the behaviour changes.

```text
Prompt A:
What is the capital of Karnataka, and when was it declared the capital?
```

```text
Prompt B:
Give me the exact enrolment numbers for B.Com students at [your college's name] in 2024.
```

```text
Prompt C:
I will paste our college prospectus. Using ONLY this text, tell me the total intake
for B.Com and quote the sentence you took it from. If it is not in the text, say
"not stated".

[paste 2–3 paragraphs from any prospectus or brochure]
```

Prompt A is a common pattern — expect a correct answer. Prompt B has no reliable pattern — watch whether the tool admits it does not know or invents a figure. Prompt C gives the model context and a rule — this is how professionals use it, and Module 5 turns the idea into a system.

## Hands-on lab: "Explain it to your grandmother" (40 min)

Work in pairs if you are in a classroom; otherwise use the assistant as your partner.

1. **Draft (10 min).** Without AI, write a 120-word explanation of how a chatbot produces its answers, aimed at a relative who has never used one. Use a comparison from daily life (a very well-read friend who has never been to your town; a student who has memorised every past paper but not the syllabus).
2. **Improve with AI (10 min).** Paste your draft into an assistant with this instruction:

   ```text
   Here is my explanation of how a chatbot works, for a non-technical relative.
   Do NOT rewrite it. Point out (a) anything factually wrong, (b) anything a
   beginner would misunderstand, (c) one place where my analogy breaks down.
   Then suggest one sentence I could add about why chatbots sometimes make things up.
   ```

3. **Revise (10 min).** Make your own edits. Keep your voice; take the corrections you agree with.
4. **Test it (10 min).** Read the final version to someone outside the course (a family member, a hostel mate). Ask them to explain it back to you. Note where they got it wrong — that is where your explanation still needs work.

**Deliverable:** your first draft, the AI's critique, your final version and two lines on what your listener misunderstood.

## Common mistakes

- **"It searched the internet for that."** Unless the tool shows a "searching" step or citations, the answer came from patterns learned in training, not a live lookup. Lesson 2.5 covers tools that do search.
- **"It remembers me."** Most assistants remember only the current conversation plus, in some tools, a small opt-in memory. Assume every new chat starts blank unless told otherwise.
- **Confusing confidence with accuracy.** The tone of an answer carries no information about its truth. Formatting, bullet points and citations can all be generated.

## Key takeaways

- AI ⊃ ML ⊃ DL ⊃ GenAI. Most AI you have used is *discriminative*; generative AI *creates*.
- An LLM predicts the next token based on patterns from huge amounts of text. Fluency is guaranteed; truth is not — hence hallucination.
- The model can only use the context you give it. Better context → better output. This is the foundation of prompt engineering (Module 2) and custom assistants (Module 5).
- Learn the vocabulary: model, prompt, token, context window, training cut-off, fine-tuning, multimodal, agent.
