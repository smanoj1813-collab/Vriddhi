# 1.4 Capabilities, limits & responsible use

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 35 · Lab 40 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- List what today's generative AI does well, does unreliably and cannot do, with an example of each.
- Recognise the five common failure modes — hallucination, bias, staleness, sycophancy and privacy leakage — when they appear in your own work.
- Apply the **Automate / Augment / Avoid** framework to decide how much of a task to hand to AI.
- State your college's academic-integrity expectations for AI use and write a disclosure line for any assignment.

## Opening scenario

A BBA student submits a marketing assignment analysing "a 2024 Nielsen report on Tier-2 city consumers in India". The analysis is sharp, the citation is precise, and the report does not exist. The faculty member finds this in thirty seconds. The student is not a cheat — they trusted a tool the way they would trust a textbook, and they did not disclose using it.

Two habits would have saved them: **checking anything specific**, and **saying how the work was produced**. Both are learnable in an afternoon, and both are what employers mean when they ask for "responsible AI use".

## Core ideas

### 1. What GenAI is good at, unreliable at and bad at

| Reliable today | Unreliable — verify every time | Do not delegate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rewriting, summarising and translating text you supply | Specific facts, numbers, dates, names, citations | Decisions about people (grades, hiring, discipline) |
| Brainstorming options, structures and outlines | Anything after the model's training cut-off, unless it searched | Anything requiring accountability you must personally hold (a signed certificate, a legal declaration) |
| Explaining a concept at a chosen level | Arithmetic on long lists (use a spreadsheet or the tool's code feature) | Sensitive personal or medical advice |
| Drafting emails, captions, scripts, first-draft essays | Legal, tax and medical specifics for India | Assessment work you are required to do yourself |
| Formatting, tables, converting between formats | Claims about itself ("I have checked this", "I am certain") | Confidential data you are not permitted to share |
| Code for small tasks, spreadsheet formulas | Reading long documents fully — it may skim | — |

### 2. Five failure modes to recognise on sight

1. **Hallucination** — confident fabrication. Triggers: obscure facts, citations, statistics, anything "specific and rare". Defence: ask for sources *and check them*; give the tool the document instead of asking from memory.
2. **Bias** — outputs that reflect stereotypes in training data: which names get called "leaders", which jobs get which gender, which regions are described as "developed". Defence: notice, rephrase, ask for balanced alternatives; Module 7 gives you a testing protocol.
3. **Staleness** — the model's knowledge stops at its training cut-off. Defence: for anything time-sensitive (rules, prices, laws, news) use a tool that searches and shows dated sources.
4. **Sycophancy** — the model agrees with you, praises your idea, and softens criticism because that is what pleases users. Defence: ask explicitly for the strongest counter-argument, for a harsh review, or for a rating on a rubric.
5. **Privacy leakage** — you paste something confidential; it may be stored, reviewed or used for training. Defence: minimise, anonymise, and use the "do not train" setting; never paste other people's personal data.

### 3. The Automate / Augment / Avoid framework

Before handing any task to AI, ask three questions:

- **How costly is a mistake?** (A wrong caption is cheap; a wrong tax figure is not.)
- **Can I verify the output?** (Do I have the knowledge, the source, or the time?)
- **Who is accountable for the result?** (If it is me, I must understand it.)

Then place the task:

| Zone | When | How to work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Automate** | Mistakes are cheap *and* easy to spot; the task is routine. | Let AI do it; spot-check a sample. Examples: formatting notes, first-draft social captions, converting a table to bullet points. |
| **Augment** | Mistakes matter but you can verify; the task benefits from your judgement. | AI drafts, you direct and check every claim. Examples: research summaries, presentation outlines, email to a client, data analysis narrative. |
| **Avoid** | Mistakes are costly *and* hard to verify, or the task is about a person, or the rules forbid it. | Do it yourself; use AI at most to organise your own thinking. Examples: exams, a reference letter, a medical or legal decision, anything with confidential data you cannot share. |

You used this framework informally in Lesson 1.1's audit. From now on, name the zone before you start a task — it takes three seconds and prevents most disasters.

### 4. Academic integrity: the rules in this course and in your degree

Universities in Karnataka and across India are updating their policies; check your own college's current rule. This course's rule is simple and mirrors what good employers expect:

- **Allowed and encouraged:** using AI to brainstorm, outline, explain, translate, critique your draft, generate images and audio for projects, and build automations — *when you disclose it and can explain every part of the result*.
- **Not allowed:** submitting AI output as your own analysis; fabricating sources; using AI during a closed assessment; feeding confidential or personal data into tools.
- **Always required:** an **AI-use statement** at the end of every submission.

A good AI-use statement is two to four lines:

> *AI-use statement: I used Gemini to outline this report and to suggest counter-arguments in Section 3, and Canva Magic Design for the cover image. All statistics were checked against the cited sources. The analysis and conclusions are my own.*

### 5. Responsible use beyond the classroom

The same habits scale to the workplace:

- **Disclose** to clients and managers when material is AI-generated, especially images, voices and video (Modules 3 and 4 cover labelling and content credentials).
- **Respect copyright** — do not prompt for "in the style of" a living artist for commercial work; do not upload material you do not have the right to share.
- **Protect people** — never generate a real person's likeness or voice without consent; never use AI to profile or rank people without a human process (Module 7).
- **Keep a human in the loop** wherever the output touches money, health, law or someone's reputation.

## Try it now

Test sycophancy and self-critique in one go. Paste a paragraph from any essay you wrote and run both prompts in the same chat:

```text
Prompt 1: Here is a paragraph from my essay. What do you think of it?
[paragraph]
```

```text
Prompt 2: Now act as a strict examiner marking to a university rubric. Give this
paragraph a mark out of 10 for argument, evidence and clarity, list the three
biggest weaknesses, and do not soften the feedback.
```

Compare the tone and the substance. Prompt 2 is how you should *always* ask for feedback.

## Hands-on lab: the hallucination hunt (40 min)

**Goal:** find, document and fix fabrications so you recognise them for the rest of your life.

1. **Provoke (10 min).** Ask an assistant *without web search* for:
   - Five academic references on a narrow topic in your subject (e.g. "microfinance in Karnataka self-help groups", "Kannada Dalit literature after 1990", "ethanol blending in Indian two-wheelers").
   - Three statistics about your district with sources.
   - A short biography of a real but not-famous person you know something about (a local author, your college's founder).
2. **Verify (15 min).** Check each reference on Google Scholar; each statistic on the source it named; each biographical claim against what you know. Build a table: *Claim · Source given · Verified? · Correct version.*
3. **Fix the process, not just the answer (10 min).** Re-run the reference request in a tool that searches and shows sources (Perplexity, or your assistant with search enabled). Check again. Note how many results are now real.
4. **Write your rule (5 min).** In one sentence, state the check you will run every time an AI gives you a fact you intend to use.

**Deliverable:** the verification table, the before/after count of real references, and your one-sentence rule.

## Common mistakes

- **Checking only the things that look suspicious.** Hallucinations look *exactly* like true statements. Check by category (all citations, all numbers), not by vibe.
- **Asking the same tool "are you sure?"** It will usually say yes. Verify against an independent source.
- **Over-correcting into "never use AI".** The Avoid zone is small; most of your work belongs in Augment. Responsible use is a skill, not abstinence.
- **Forgetting the AI-use statement.** In this course a missing statement costs marks; in a job it costs trust.

## Key takeaways

- GenAI is reliable for transforming text you supply and for generating options; unreliable for specific facts; unsuitable for decisions about people and for work you must own.
- Recognise five failure modes: hallucination, bias, staleness, sycophancy, privacy leakage — each has a simple defence.
- Use **Automate / Augment / Avoid** based on cost of error, verifiability and accountability.
- Disclose AI use in every submission with a short AI-use statement; verify anything specific; protect personal data.
