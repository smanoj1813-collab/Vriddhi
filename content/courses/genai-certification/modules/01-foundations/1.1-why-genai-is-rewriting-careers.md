# 1.1 Why GenAI is rewriting careers

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain, with examples, how generative AI is changing entry-level work in commerce, management, arts and science careers.
- Describe the difference between a task and a job, and why AI replaces tasks far more often than it replaces jobs.
- Identify three ways a non-technical graduate in India can turn AI into a career advantage.
- Set a personal goal for this course that is specific enough to measure in Week 10.

## Opening scenario

Meghana is a final-year B.Com student in Tumakuru. In 2023 her senior got a job at a Bengaluru accounting firm doing GST reconciliations: downloading invoices, matching them against the GSTR-2B statement, flagging mismatches in Excel. It took the whole team three days each month.

This year the same firm hired two fresh graduates instead of five. The reconciliation is now done by a script and an AI assistant that drafts the mismatch emails to vendors. What the two new hires actually do all day is *check the AI's work*, handle the exceptions the software cannot explain, and talk to clients.

Nothing about this story is unusual. Across every field this course touches — accounts, marketing, HR, journalism, teaching, research, design — the same shift is happening: **the routine first draft is becoming cheap, and the judgement around it is becoming valuable.** The question for you is not *"Will AI take my job?"* It is *"Which side of that line will I be on?"*

## Core ideas

### 1. AI changes tasks before it changes jobs

A job is a bundle of tasks. A marketing executive's week might contain writing captions, analysing campaign numbers, briefing a designer, negotiating with an agency, and presenting to a manager. AI can already do a decent first draft of the first two. It cannot negotiate with the agency or read the room in the meeting.

Researchers who study this (the OECD, the World Economic Forum, the International Labour Organization) keep arriving at the same finding: most jobs are *exposed* to AI, meaning some of their tasks can be done faster, but only a small share can be fully automated. The jobs that shrink fastest are the ones where **almost every task is a routine first draft** — data entry, basic transcription, template-based customer replies. The jobs that grow are the ones where AI makes a capable person dramatically more productive.

> **Think about it:** list the five tasks you would expect to do in your first job. Circle the ones that are "produce a first draft of something predictable". Those are the tasks AI will do *with* you — if you know how to direct it.

### 2. The "AI-augmented graduate" is the new entry-level standard

Three years ago, knowing Excel well was a differentiator for a commerce graduate. Today it is assumed. Fluency with generative AI is on the same path, only faster. Job descriptions in India already list phrases such as "comfortable with AI tools", "prompt engineering exposure" and "uses ChatGPT / Copilot for productivity" for roles that have nothing to do with technology: accounts assistant, content executive, HR coordinator, customer success associate, research analyst.

Being AI-augmented does not mean being a programmer. It means:

| Skill | What it looks like in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct** | You can brief an AI tool as precisely as you would brief a junior colleague. |
| **Verify** | You can tell when the output is wrong, incomplete or biased — and fix it. |
| **Combine** | You can chain tools together: research → draft → design → schedule. |
| **Judge** | You know which tasks should *not* be handed to AI at all. |

This course is built around exactly those four verbs. Every module ends with something you have *made*, so by Week 10 you will have a portfolio, not just a certificate.

### 3. Why this matters more in India, not less

India adds around a crore of people to the workforce every year, and most graduates come from non-technical streams. Three facts make GenAI unusually important here:

1. **English is no longer a gate.** Modern assistants work well in Kannada, Hindi, Tamil and Telugu. A student who thinks in Kannada can draft a professional English email in seconds and then *check* it — the language barrier that held back many first-generation graduates is shrinking.
2. **Small businesses are the biggest employers.** A Mysuru silk retailer, a Hubballi logistics firm or a Davangere coaching centre cannot afford an agency, a designer and a data analyst. One graduate who can do a competent version of all three with AI is worth hiring.
3. **The tools are mostly free.** Every tool in this course has a free tier good enough for learning. The advantage goes to whoever practises, not whoever pays.

### 4. The three career plays

There are three sensible ways to position yourself, and you can combine them:

- **The multiplier** — you do a conventional role (accounts, sales, teaching, HR) two to three times faster than peers because AI handles the routine layer. This is the most common path and it is valuable immediately.
- **The translator** — you sit between a business and its AI tools: writing the prompts, building the custom assistants, designing the automations, checking the outputs. Titles include *AI operations associate*, *automation specialist*, *AI content lead*. Module 5 and Module 6 train you for this.
- **The builder** — you create something new: a service, a product, a channel. Many of the fastest-growing small businesses in India today are one or two people plus AI tools. The capstone gives you a first attempt in a safe setting.

### 5. What does not change

It is easy to overstate the revolution. Employers still hire for reliability, communication, integrity and domain knowledge. A B.Com student who does not understand debits and credits cannot check an AI's journal entries. A BA student who has not read widely cannot tell a good essay from a plausible one. **AI amplifies what you know; it does not replace knowing.** Keep taking your core subjects seriously — this course makes them more valuable, not less.

## Try it now

Open any free chat assistant (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Copilot) and paste the following. Replace the bracketed parts with your own details.

```text
I am a [second-year BBA] student in [Karnataka, India]. I am interested in a career in
[digital marketing for small businesses].

1. List the 8 most common tasks in the first job someone like me would get.
2. For each task, rate from 1 to 5 how much a generative AI tool can help today,
   and say in one line what the human still has to do.
3. Suggest 3 concrete things I could do in the next 10 weeks to be the candidate
   who is clearly better at using AI than the others.

Format the answer as a table followed by a short list.
```

Read the answer critically. Which ratings do you agree with? Which sound too optimistic? Write two lines of your own assessment under the table — this is the habit of *verifying*, and you will use it in every lesson.

## Hands-on lab: "My AI-augmented week" audit (45 min)

**Goal:** see, in your own life, where AI already fits — and set your course goal.

1. **Log your tasks (10 min).** In a spreadsheet or notebook, list every study or work task you did in the last seven days that took longer than 20 minutes. Aim for 12–20 items: writing notes, preparing a presentation, searching for references, drafting messages, making a poster for a club, tallying expenses.
2. **Classify each task (10 min).** Add three columns and tick one per row:
   - **Automate** — AI could do almost all of it (formatting notes, first-draft captions).
   - **Augment** — AI could do part of it while you direct and check (research, presentation outline).
   - **Avoid** — AI should not do it (a personal apology, an exam, a decision about someone).
3. **Estimate the time (10 min).** For each *Automate* and *Augment* row, estimate minutes saved per week if you used AI well. Total it. Most students find 3–6 hours; write your number down.
4. **Set your Week-10 goal (15 min).** Complete this sentence and save it in your reflection journal:

   > "By the end of this course I will be able to ______ for ______ (a person, a business, a club or myself), and I will prove it with ______ (a specific artefact)."

   Good examples: *"…create a month of Instagram content for my uncle's bakery, proven by a published campaign kit"*; *"…automate my department's event registration with a form → sheet → WhatsApp workflow, proven by a demo video."*

**Deliverable:** the audit sheet and your goal sentence, uploaded to the Module 1 assignment. Your facilitator will refer back to the goal in Week 10.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating AI as a search engine.** Search finds things that exist; generative AI *creates* things that may or may not be true. Lesson 1.4 covers this in depth.
- **Waiting to be "technical enough".** Every tool here is a chat box or a drag-and-drop canvas. The scarce skill is judgement about your own domain, which you are already building in your degree.
- **Hiding AI use.** In this course, disclosure is always expected and never penalised. In the workplace, the same rule increasingly applies.

## Key takeaways

- AI replaces *tasks*, not whole jobs; the routine first draft is becoming cheap and judgement is becoming valuable.
- The AI-augmented graduate can **direct, verify, combine and judge** — the four skills this course builds.
- In India, language support, the small-business economy and free tools make GenAI a particularly strong lever for non-technical graduates.
- Position yourself as a multiplier, a translator or a builder — and keep your domain knowledge strong, because AI amplifies what you know.
