# 4.3 Audio in your content stack

> **Lesson plan** · 60 minutes — Read 20 · Lab 30 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Convert written content into audio formats — blog to podcast, lecture to audio notes, document to accessible audio.
- Use speech-to-text to turn recordings (lectures, interviews, meetings) into structured, searchable text.
- Write and produce IVR and voice-bot scripts that people can actually follow.
- Plan an audio-inclusive content stack for a small organisation and build the Project Block 3 episode plan.

## Opening scenario

A BBA student interning at a two-doctor clinic in Hubballi notices three problems: patients miss follow-up instructions, the phone rings constantly with the same five questions, and the doctors' health-camp talks vanish after the event. In two weeks she introduces: a WhatsApp voice note of post-visit instructions in Kannada generated from a template; a simple IVR that answers the five questions in Kannada and English; and recorded camp talks transcribed into a searchable FAQ and short audio clips. No app, no developer. She has quietly redesigned the clinic's communication — with audio at the centre.

## Core ideas

### 1. Text → audio: the conversions that pay off

| Conversion | How | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Blog / article → podcast episode** | Assistant rewrites for the ear (spoken structure, signposts, no headings), TTS or your voice, music bed, show notes from transcript | Do not read the article; *re-tell* it. Cut lists to three items. |
| **Lecture notes → audio revision notes** | Summarise per topic into 3–5 minute segments; TTS; one file per topic | Add a "quiz yourself" pause: question, three seconds of silence, answer. |
| **Report / policy → audio brief** | Executive summary → 2-minute spoken brief for busy managers | Numbers spoken clearly; give the decision first. |
| **Scheme / notice → community audio** | Plain-language rewrite in the local language, TTS specialist voice, distribute on WhatsApp | Verify every date, amount and eligibility rule against the official source. |
| **Document → accessible audio** | Full read-aloud version for people who cannot read the text | Describe tables and figures in words; keep headings as spoken signposts. |
| **Source pack → conversational overview** | NotebookLM-style audio overview from your own documents | Useful as a first pass; it simplifies and occasionally misstates — check before sharing. |

The pattern is always the same: **plain-language rewrite for the ear → voice → light music → export → verify against the source.**

### 2. Audio → text: capturing what would otherwise be lost

Speech-to-text (STT) is now excellent for English and good for major Indian languages, with code-mixing handled acceptably by the better tools.

- **Lectures:** record (with permission), transcribe, then ask an assistant to produce structured notes, key definitions, likely exam questions and a glossary. Verify technical terms — STT mishears them.
- **Interviews and field research:** transcribe; tag themes; pull quotations with timestamps. Keep the audio as the source of truth.
- **Meetings:** meeting assistants transcribe and draft minutes with action items. Rules: announce recording, get consent, review the minutes before circulation — assistants invent action items nobody agreed to.
- **Voice notes to text:** long WhatsApp voice notes from a client or a professor become searchable text in seconds.
- **Your own thinking:** dictate a first draft while walking; edit as text later. Many people think more clearly aloud.

Privacy rule: transcripts of other people are personal data. Store them securely, delete when done, and never upload confidential recordings to tools whose terms you have not read (Module 7).

### 3. IVR and voice-bot scripts

Interactive voice response is the menu you hear when you call a business. Most are terrible because they were written as documents, not conversations. Good ones:

- **Greet briefly and state the purpose** (one sentence).
- **Offer at most four options**, most common first, the action before the number: *"For today's appointment timings, press 1."*
- **Speak the caller's language** — offer language choice first if your callers are bilingual.
- **Confirm before acting** ("You chose to cancel Friday's appointment. Press 1 to confirm.").
- **Always provide an exit** to a human or a callback.
- **Keep every prompt under 12 seconds.**

Workflow: map the five most common calls → write the tree with an assistant (ask it to critique for confusion and dead ends) → record or TTS each prompt → test with three real callers → fix.

Modern AI voice bots can converse freely rather than following menus; the same rules apply, plus clear disclosure that the caller is speaking to an AI, and a fast path to a person.

### 4. Designing an audio-inclusive stack

For any small organisation, ask: *Who in our audience listens rather than reads? In which language? On which device?* Then add audio deliberately:

1. **One recurring format** (weekly two-minute voice update, a monthly podcast, an audio FAQ) — consistency beats ambition.
2. **One accessibility commitment** (every important notice also as audio in the local language).
3. **One capture habit** (record and transcribe talks and meetings).
4. **A production template**: script structure, voice, music bed, loudness preset, export settings, distribution channel, rights record.
5. **A verification step** naming who checks facts and language before release.

Document this as a one-page "audio stack" — it is a real deliverable that interns produce for small businesses and NGOs.

### 5. Show notes, transcripts and distribution

- **Show notes:** three lines of summary, timestamps for sections, links, credits (voice, music with licence, AI tools used), and a transcript link. Draft with an assistant from the transcript; edit.
- **Transcript:** publish it. It helps search, accessibility and anyone who prefers reading.
- **Distribution:** WhatsApp (voice note or MP3 under 16 MB), YouTube (with a static image or captions), Spotify for Creators or similar podcast hosts (free), Instagram (audiograms — a waveform video made in your editor).
- **Measure:** plays, completion rate where available, replies and forwards. Ask three listeners what they remembered.

## Try it now

Choose one lesson from this course. In ten minutes, produce a three-minute *audio revision note*: ask an assistant to rewrite the key ideas for the ear with two "quiz yourself" pauses; generate with your chosen TTS voice; add a soft bed; export. Listen on a walk. Did it teach?

## Hands-on lab: plan your bilingual episode (30 min)

This lab sets up Project Block 3.

1. **Choose (5 min):** a topic worth three minutes for a specific audience — a scheme explainer, a study topic, a small-business tip, a local history piece.
2. **Outline (10 min):** with an assistant, produce the segment structure from Lesson 4.2 with exact word budgets for English and your Indian language. Decide: one bilingual episode, or two parallel episodes?
3. **Production sheet (10 min):** voice(s) chosen per language, whether you will record or synthesise, music bed source and licence, loudness target, export format, distribution channel, who verifies the facts and the language.
4. **IVR bonus (5 min):** write a four-option IVR menu for an organisation related to your topic; read it aloud and time each prompt.

**Deliverable:** the episode outline, production sheet and the IVR script. Bring them to the PB3 session.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading a document aloud and calling it a podcast.** Re-tell it for the ear.
- **Trusting STT on technical terms and names.** Verify against the audio.
- **Circulating AI-drafted minutes without review.** Invented action items cause real trouble.
- **IVR menus with seven options and no exit.** Four options, human fallback, under 12 seconds.
- **Ignoring rights and privacy** — music licences, recording consent, transcript storage.

## Key takeaways

- Text → audio conversions follow one pattern: ear-rewrite → voice → light music → export → verify.
- Audio → text turns lectures, interviews and meetings into searchable, structured assets — with consent and careful storage.
- IVR and voice bots: brief greeting, ≤4 options, action before number, confirm, exit, language choice, AI disclosure.
- An audio-inclusive stack is a one-page plan: recurring format, accessibility commitment, capture habit, production template, verification step.
- Publish show notes and transcripts; distribute where your audience already listens; measure and ask.
