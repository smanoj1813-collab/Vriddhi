# 4.1 Audio opportunities & voice generation

> **Lesson plan** · 75 minutes — Read 25 · Lab 40 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain where audio content is growing in India and which roles use it.
- Describe how modern text-to-speech (TTS) works and why it now sounds human.
- Choose and direct a synthetic voice for a purpose: narration, explainer, IVR, ad, accessibility.
- Generate clean speech in English and at least one Indian language, and evaluate it with a checklist.

## Opening scenario

Rakesh, a B.Com student from Tumakuru, notices that his grandmother, who cannot read English, follows every government scheme through voice messages forwarded on WhatsApp. He starts turning the Ministry's English PDFs into two-minute Kannada audio explainers with a synthetic voice, checked by his mother. Within a month a local self-help group asks him to do the same for their loan scheme rules. He has found a service nobody was offering — and a story for his interviews.

## Core ideas

### 1. Where audio is growing

- **Voice-first India.** Hundreds of millions of people prefer listening and speaking to reading, particularly in Indian languages. Voice notes, audio in WhatsApp, YouTube in regional languages and podcasts have exploded.
- **Podcasts and audio explainers** for brands, educators, NGOs and government schemes.
- **Voiceovers** for reels, product videos, e-learning and corporate training — historically expensive, now minutes of work.
- **IVR and voice bots** for customer service, appointment reminders, order updates — often multilingual.
- **Accessibility:** audio versions of documents, screen-reader-friendly content, audio descriptions.
- **Localisation:** the same script in Kannada, Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam and English, at a cost that used to require a studio and six voice artists.

For a non-technical graduate, audio is a low-competition skill: many people can write; fewer can produce a clean, well-paced, multilingual audio piece.

### 2. How text-to-speech works now

Older TTS stitched recorded fragments together, which is why railway announcements sound robotic. Modern **neural TTS** works like the other generative models you have met: trained on huge amounts of paired text and speech, it learns to predict the sound — pitch, timing, breath, emphasis — that a human would produce for a sentence in context. Some systems are built on the same architecture as language models and "understand" the meaning well enough to place emphasis and emotion sensibly.

Consequences:

- **Punctuation and phrasing are your controls.** Commas, full stops, paragraph breaks and ellipses shape pauses. A script written for the ear sounds better than one written for the eye.
- **Pronunciation of names and Indian words** varies by voice and vendor. You test, and you fix with spelling tricks ("Tumakuru" → "Tuma-kooru") or a phonetic field where the tool offers one.
- **Multilingual and code-mixed speech** (Kannada with English terms) is supported by the better voices but quality differs sharply between tools; audition before committing.
- **Emotion and style** can be steered with instructions in some tools ("warm, unhurried, slightly amused") or by choosing a voice designed for that use.

### 3. The tool landscape (2026)

| Tool type | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Premium neural TTS** | ElevenLabs, Google Cloud / Gemini voices, Microsoft Azure voices, Amazon Polly, OpenAI voices | Best quality, many Indian-language voices, style control; free tiers are small — budget characters |
| **TTS inside creative tools** | Canva, CapCut, Descript, Clipchamp, Adobe Express | Convenient for video voiceovers; fewer voices; adequate for social content |
| **Indian-language specialists** | Bhashini (government platform), Sarvam AI, AI4Bharat models, regional start-ups | Strong regional coverage and accents; access models vary |
| **Read-aloud / accessibility** | Browser and phone read-aloud, NotebookLM audio overviews, Speechify-type apps | Instant audio versions of documents; conversational "podcast" generation from sources |
| **Speech-to-text** (the reverse) | Whisper-based apps, Google Recorder, Otter, meeting-assistant features | Transcribe lectures, interviews, meetings — you will use this in Lesson 4.3 |

Decision rule: **social-video voiceover → TTS inside your editor; anything a client will hear repeatedly → premium neural TTS with an auditioned voice; regional languages → audition specialists alongside the premium tools.**

### 4. Directing a voice

A voice choice is a brand choice. Decide:

1. **Purpose:** inform (calm, clear), persuade (warm, confident), instruct (steady, slightly slower), entertain (energetic), reassure (soft, unhurried).
2. **Audience and language:** age, region, formality; which language and which accent — a Bengaluru listener and a Dharwad listener hear "neutral Kannada" differently.
3. **Voice attributes:** gender, apparent age, pace, warmth, energy. Audition three; play to two people from the audience; pick.
4. **Script for the ear:** sentences under 15 words; contractions; no brackets or slashes; numbers written as they should be spoken ("two thousand five hundred rupees"); acronyms spaced or spelt ("G S T" or "Goods and Services Tax").
5. **Direction:** use style instructions where available; otherwise use punctuation, and split the script into paragraphs by mood.
6. **Pronunciation pass:** list every proper noun and Indian word; test; add phonetic spellings.

### 5. Quality checklist for generated speech

Before you use a clip, listen with headphones and check:

- Every word pronounced correctly (names, places, numbers, currency).
- Pace between 140 and 170 words per minute for explainers; slower for instructions.
- Natural pauses at commas and paragraph breaks; no run-on rushing.
- No glitches: doubled syllables, cut-off endings, sudden pitch jumps, breathy artefacts.
- Consistent voice across clips (same voice ID, same settings).
- Volume levelled; no clipping.
- Emotion fits the content (a scheme-deadline warning should not sound cheerful).

## Try it now

Take a 120-word paragraph from any lesson in this course. Ask a chat assistant to *"rewrite for the ear: short sentences, spoken numbers, no brackets, mark paragraph breaks where the listener needs a pause"*. Generate it with two different voices in any TTS tool. Play both to a friend without saying which is which. Which one would they keep listening to, and why?

## Hands-on lab: bilingual voice audition (40 min)

1. **Script (10 min):** write (or adapt with an assistant) a 150-word public-interest announcement — a scholarship deadline, a health camp, a library rule change. Produce an English version and a version in Kannada, Hindi, Tamil or Telugu. Have a native speaker glance at the Indian-language version.
2. **Audition (15 min):** in two tools, generate the English script with two voices each; generate the Indian-language script with the best available voice in each tool. Save all clips.
3. **Pronunciation pass (5 min):** list mispronounced words; apply spelling or phonetic fixes; regenerate.
4. **Evaluate (10 min):** score each clip with the checklist; play the top two to two listeners; record their preference and one comment each.

**Deliverable:** the two scripts, six or more clips, the pronunciation fix list, the checklist scores and the listener feedback. Update your AI Toolkit sheet with your chosen TTS tool per language.

## Common mistakes

- **Feeding the written document straight to TTS.** Rewrite for the ear first.
- **Choosing a voice by yourself.** Audition with two members of the audience.
- **Ignoring pronunciation of local names.** It is the first thing a local listener notices.
- **Burning through a free tier on drafts.** Test with one paragraph, then generate the full script once.
- **Different voices across clips of the same piece.** Fix voice and settings before generating.

## Key takeaways

- Audio is a growing, low-competition skill in voice-first, multilingual India.
- Neural TTS predicts human-like delivery from text; punctuation and phrasing are your controls.
- Choose tools by purpose; audition specialists for Indian languages.
- Direct the voice: purpose, audience, attributes, ear-script, style, pronunciation pass.
- Check every clip with headphones against the quality checklist before use.
