# 5.5 Building a no-code AI app

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 50 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Choose between building a custom assistant, an automation, a no-code app or a "vibe-coded" prototype for a given problem.
- Design an app's data (tables and fields) and screens before building.
- Build a small working app in a no-code builder (Glide, Softr, AppSheet, Bubble or similar) with one AI feature.
- Test with real users, fix the top problems, and hand over responsibly.

## Opening scenario

A hostel mess committee wants students to report food issues and see what has been fixed. The committee member from B.Sc Chemistry builds an app in Glide over a weekend: a form to submit an issue with a photo; an AI step that classifies it (hygiene / quality / quantity / service) and drafts a polite summary; a public board of issues with status; a committee-only screen to update status. The warden sees the board and, for the first time, the same numbers the students see. Complaints about complaints stop. She calls it "a spreadsheet with a face" — which is exactly what most useful apps are.

## Core ideas

### 1. Which kind of thing should you build?

| If the problem is… | Build… | Because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People need answers or drafts in conversation | **Custom assistant** (5.1/5.2) | Chat is the interface; knowledge is documents |
| Data needs to move between apps when events happen | **Automation** (5.3) | No screens needed; triggers and actions |
| People need to enter, see and update structured records, with roles and permissions | **No-code app** (this lesson) | Forms, lists, detail screens, roles |
| You need a novel interactive experience or a quick prototype to show an idea | **AI-generated ("vibe-coded") web app** (Lovable, Bolt, Replit, v0, Google AI Studio apps…) | Fast and impressive; but you cannot maintain what you cannot read — treat as a prototype |
| A custom, scalable product with sensitive data | **A developer** | Beyond the scope of no-code; your job is the spec and the validated need |

Most real solutions combine two: an app for data entry and views, an automation for notifications and AI steps, an assistant for questions.

### 2. The no-code builders

| Builder | Data lives in | Strengths | Free tier reality |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Glide** | Google Sheets / Glide tables / Airtable | Fast, polished mobile-first apps; built-in AI columns (classify, extract, summarise, generate) | Free for small personal apps; user/row limits |
| **Softr** | Airtable / Google Sheets / Softr tables | Portals, directories, client dashboards; good permissions | Free with limits |
| **AppSheet** (Google) | Sheets / Drive | Included with many Google Workspace accounts; strong for field data collection; Gemini features | Free to build; publishing needs licences in some setups |
| **Airtable Interfaces** | Airtable | Database + views + AI fields; great for internal tools | Free for small bases |
| **Bubble** | Own database | Most flexible web apps; steeper learning curve | Free to build, paid to publish |
| **Microsoft Power Apps** | Dataverse / SharePoint / Excel | Institutional 365 environments | Often included with 365 |

For this course: **Glide or AppSheet with Google Sheets** unless you have a reason to choose otherwise. The skill transfers.

### 3. Design before building: data first

Every app is tables and screens. Design the tables first.

1. **List the nouns** in your problem: Issue, Student, Committee member, Status update.
2. **One table per noun**; one row per thing; one column per fact about it.
3. **Fields with types**: text, number, date, choice (fixed options), image, yes/no, relation (link to another table), user (who created it).
4. **Identify the owner of each row** (who may edit it) and **who may see it** — this decides your roles.
5. **Decide what AI adds**: a classification column, a summary column, a draft reply, an extraction from a photo — one AI feature, done well.

Example — Mess Issue Tracker:

| Table | Fields |
| --- | --- |
| Issues | id · title · description · photo · category (AI) · summary (AI) · status (Open / In progress / Fixed / Won't fix) · reported_by (user) · created_at · resolved_at |
| Updates | id · issue (relation) · note · by (user) · at |
| Members | email · role (Student / Committee / Warden) |

### 4. Screens and flows

Standard screens: **List** (with filters and search) → **Detail** (one record, with actions) → **Form** (create/edit) → **Dashboard** (counts and charts) → **Profile/settings**. Sketch each on paper with the three most important elements first.

Map the **user flows**: "Student reports an issue" (Form → confirmation → sees it in List); "Committee resolves" (List filtered to Open → Detail → change status → Update added → student notified via automation). If a flow needs more than four taps, simplify.

**Roles and visibility**: students see all issues but edit only their own; committee edits status; warden sees dashboard. Row-level visibility rules are where beginners leak data — test with a second account.

### 5. Adding the AI feature well

- **Choose where the model helps a human, not where it decides.** Classify and summarise (human can override) rather than auto-close.
- **Prompt inside the AI column** as in Lesson 5.3: narrow, structured, few-shot, with a fallback value ("Unclear").
- **Show the AI's output as editable**, labelled as AI-suggested.
- **Budget**: AI columns cost per row on many platforms; compute only on new rows.
- **Privacy**: do not send names, phone numbers or photos of people to the model unless necessary and disclosed; strip fields.
- **Failure**: what does the app show if the AI column is empty or wrong? A sensible default, never a crash.

### 6. Testing with real users

Five users find most usability problems. Method:

1. **Write three tasks** ("Report a hygiene issue with a photo", "Find whether the Tuesday dosa complaint was fixed", "As a committee member, mark an issue In progress").
2. **Watch silently**; note where they hesitate, tap the wrong thing, or misread. Do not explain.
3. **Ask three questions** after: what was confusing, what was missing, would you use this next week?
4. **Fix the top three problems**; retest with two new users.
5. **Record**: a two-minute demo video and a bug/feedback list — required for PB4 and the capstone.

### 7. Handing over responsibly

An app without an owner is a liability. Before you leave (the semester ends, the internship finishes):

- Document: purpose, tables, roles, AI feature and prompt, known limits, how to add a user, how to export data.
- Transfer ownership of the sheet and the app to an institutional account.
- Set a privacy notice on the form (what is collected, why, who sees it, how long it is kept — Lesson 7.4).
- Agree a review date and a retirement rule ("delete data after one year").

## Try it now

In Glide or AppSheet, connect a new Google Sheet with the Issues table above (add five sample rows). Let the builder generate the default List/Detail/Form screens. Add an AI column that classifies `description` into the four categories with an "Unclear" fallback. Open the app on your phone. You have a working app — now make it good.

## Hands-on lab: a mini-app with one AI feature (50 min)

1. **Problem and nouns (5 min):** choose a real need (club inventory, mess issues, placement drive tracker, small-shop orders, lab equipment booking).
2. **Data design (10 min):** tables and fields with types, owners and visibility; one AI feature chosen.
3. **Screens and flows (5 min):** paper sketches; two user flows under four taps.
4. **Build (20 min):** connect data, screens, roles, the AI column with a fallback, a status workflow.
5. **Test (10 min):** three tasks, two users, silent observation; fix the top problem; record a two-minute demo.

**Deliverable:** data design, sketches, app link (or screenshots), test notes and the demo video. This is the starting point for PB4 if you choose the app option, and a rehearsal for the capstone.

## Common mistakes

- **Building screens before tables.** You will rebuild when the data changes.
- **Letting AI decide** (auto-close, auto-approve) instead of suggest.
- **Testing only as yourself.** Log in as a student-role user; check what leaks.
- **Feature creep.** One AI feature, three screens, working — beats ten half-built.
- **No owner or privacy notice.** The app collects personal data from day one.

## Key takeaways

- Choose the right kind of build: assistant, automation, no-code app, vibe-coded prototype, or a developer.
- Data first: nouns → tables → typed fields → owners and visibility → one AI feature.
- Screens: List, Detail, Form, Dashboard; flows under four taps; roles tested with a second account.
- AI features suggest, are editable and labelled, use narrow structured prompts with fallbacks, and minimise data.
- Test with five users on three tasks; fix; record a demo; hand over with documentation, ownership, privacy notice and a retirement rule.
