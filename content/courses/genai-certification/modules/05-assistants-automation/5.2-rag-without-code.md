# 5.2 RAG without code: assistants that know your documents

> **Lesson plan** · 75 minutes — Read 25 · Lab 40 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) in plain language and why organisations depend on it.
- Describe the RAG pipeline — chunking, embeddings, retrieval, grounding — and where each step can fail.
- Prepare documents so that retrieval works well.
- Build and evaluate a source-grounded assistant on a real document set using NotebookLM or an equivalent, with a grounding test.
- Recognise the limits of RAG and when a different approach is needed.

## Opening scenario

A B.Sc student interning at a small pharmacy chain is asked whether "an AI could answer staff questions about our SOPs" — sixty PDFs of standard operating procedures. A general chatbot knows nothing about those SOPs and will confidently invent them. What the pharmacy needs is an assistant that answers *only* from its own documents and shows where each answer came from. That is RAG. She builds a working version in NotebookLM in two hours, tests it with forty questions, and writes a one-page memo on what it gets right, what it misses, and what a paid, secure version would require. The memo is what gets her a return offer.

## Core ideas

### 1. Why models need your documents

A language model's knowledge is frozen at training and general. Your organisation's knowledge is specific, private and changing: policies, prices, procedures, syllabi, contracts, product manuals. Retraining a model on your documents is expensive and slow, and still does not stop invention.

**RAG** solves this differently: when a question arrives, the system first **retrieves** the most relevant passages from your documents, then gives them to the model with an instruction to **answer from these passages and cite them**. The model becomes a reader-and-summariser of your material rather than a memory. Most enterprise "chat with our data" products, customer-support bots and internal knowledge assistants are RAG systems.

### 2. The pipeline, step by step

| Step | What happens | Where it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Ingest** | Documents are collected and converted to text. | Scanned PDFs without OCR; tables flattened into nonsense; images ignored. |
| **2. Chunk** | Text is split into passages (a few hundred words each), often with overlap. | A rule split across two chunks; headings separated from their content. |
| **3. Embed** | Each chunk is converted into a list of numbers (an *embedding*) that captures its meaning; similar meanings → nearby numbers. | Domain jargon or Indian-language text embedded poorly by a general model. |
| **4. Store** | Embeddings go into a searchable index (a vector database). | Stale index after documents change. |
| **5. Retrieve** | The question is embedded; the most similar chunks (say, top 5–10) are fetched. Many systems also run keyword search and combine both. | The right chunk is 11th; the question uses different words from the document; the answer needs 20 chunks. |
| **6. Ground and generate** | Retrieved chunks + question + instructions go to the model, which answers and cites. | Model ignores chunks and uses its own knowledge; blends two sources; over-summarises. |
| **7. Show sources** | Citations link back to passages. | Citations present but not actually supporting the sentence — check them. |

You will never build steps 3–5 by hand in this course, but understanding them tells you *why* an assistant missed something and what to fix: usually the documents (step 1–2) or the question wording (step 5).

### 3. Preparing documents for retrieval

Most RAG failures are document failures. Before uploading:

- **Text, not pictures.** OCR scanned PDFs; export slides to text or PDF with real text.
- **Headings that describe content.** "Leave policy — casual leave — eligibility" beats "Section 4.2".
- **One topic per document or per clear section.** Retrieval works on passages; a 300-page everything-file retrieves badly.
- **Tables:** keep them simple, with a header row; or restate key rules as sentences under the table.
- **Version and date** in the file name and the first line; remove superseded versions from the set.
- **Remove personal data** you do not need (names, phone numbers, IDs).
- **Glossary:** a short document defining your organisation's abbreviations helps retrieval and answers.
- **Language:** if users ask in Kannada and documents are in English, test — some tools handle cross-language retrieval well, others do not; a translated summary document may be needed.

### 4. NotebookLM as a RAG lab

NotebookLM (and similar source-grounded tools) is a complete RAG system with a friendly interface: you add sources (PDFs, Docs, slides, web pages, YouTube transcripts, audio), it chunks and indexes them, and its answers cite passages you can click to verify. It is the ideal place to learn RAG behaviour without code.

Working method:

1. **Curate a source set** (5–20 documents) for one purpose.
2. **Write a notebook guide** — a short note describing the purpose and audience, which many tools let you set as instructions.
3. **Ask, then click the citations.** Confirm each cited passage supports the sentence it is attached to.
4. **Ask cross-source questions** ("Where do the 2024 and 2025 policies differ on X?") — RAG's real strength.
5. **Probe the boundary.** Ask something not in the sources; a good system says so.
6. **Generate artefacts** — FAQ, study guide, briefing doc, audio overview — then verify them against sources before sharing.

Other no-code RAG options: knowledge files in custom assistants (Lesson 5.1), "chat with documents" features in office suites, and no-code builders that connect a Google Drive folder to a chat interface.

### 5. The grounding test

Before trusting any document-grounded assistant, run a **grounding test** of 20 questions:

| Type | Count | Expected behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direct fact in one passage** | 8 | Correct answer with the right citation |
| **Synthesis across 2–3 passages** | 4 | Correct combined answer citing all relevant passages |
| **Not in the documents** | 4 | Says it cannot find it; does not invent |
| **Trick / misleading premise** ("What is the penalty for X?" when there is none) | 2 | Corrects the premise |
| **Ambiguous** (two versions exist) | 2 | Notes the conflict or asks which version |

Score: correct answer, correct citation, honest boundary behaviour. Anything below 17/20 means fixing documents or instructions before use; below 14/20, do not deploy.

### 6. Limits of RAG

- **Retrieval misses**: the answer is there but not retrieved. Rephrase; split documents; add headings.
- **Quantitative reasoning across a whole dataset** ("average salary across all 400 rows") is not what RAG does; use a spreadsheet or a data-analysis tool.
- **Long procedures** spread over many pages may be summarised badly; ask for one step at a time.
- **Freshness**: the index knows only what you uploaded; assign an owner to update it.
- **Security**: whoever can ask the assistant can potentially extract anything in the documents; access control matters (Lesson 7.4). Never put confidential material in a consumer tool without an organisational agreement.
- **Cited ≠ correct.** Always click.

## Hands-on lab: an SOP or syllabus assistant with a grounding test (40 min)

1. **Choose a real document set (5 min):** college handbook and exam regulations; a club's constitution and event SOPs; a small business's price list, return policy and FAQs; or your full semester syllabus and notes.
2. **Prepare (10 min):** apply the document preparation rules — OCR if needed, headings, versions, remove personal data, add a glossary note.
3. **Build (5 min):** upload to NotebookLM or your platform's knowledge feature; write the purpose note.
4. **Grounding test (15 min):** write and run the 20-question test; record answers, citations and boundary behaviour; compute the score.
5. **Fix and memo (5 min):** make one document or instruction fix that raises the score; write a five-line memo: what it can be trusted for, what it cannot, what a production version would need.

**Deliverable:** the source list, the test sheet with scores before and after the fix, and the memo. Add "grounding test" to your playbook.

## Common mistakes

- **Uploading scans and expecting answers.** No text, no retrieval.
- **One giant document.** Split by topic.
- **Trusting a citation without clicking.** Cited passages sometimes do not support the sentence.
- **Asking RAG to do arithmetic across a dataset.** Wrong tool.
- **Putting confidential or personal data in a consumer notebook.** Organisational tools with agreements only.

## Key takeaways

- RAG retrieves relevant passages from your documents and makes the model answer from them with citations — the basis of most enterprise AI assistants.
- The pipeline (ingest → chunk → embed → store → retrieve → ground → cite) fails mostly at the document and question stages, which you control.
- Prepare documents: real text, descriptive headings, one topic per section, versions, no personal data, glossary.
- NotebookLM-type tools are a complete RAG lab; run a 20-question grounding test before trusting any assistant.
- Limits: retrieval misses, dataset arithmetic, freshness, security; cited is not the same as correct.
