# 5.4 Business process design & the automation scorecard

> **Lesson plan** · 75 minutes — Read 25 · Lab 40 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Map a real business process with a simple notation and find waste, delays and hand-offs.
- Score candidate tasks with the automation scorecard and rank them honestly.
- Estimate return on automation in minutes saved, error reduction and cost — and present it to a manager.
- Place human-in-the-loop checkpoints where risk is, not everywhere or nowhere.
- Write a one-page process-improvement proposal — the deliverable every internship values.

## Opening scenario

A BBA intern at a Mangaluru cashew exporter is asked to "look at the order desk". She spends two days watching, and draws the process: enquiry arrives by WhatsApp or email → a salesperson retypes it into an Excel quote → the manager approves by phone → the quote is emailed → the customer replies → the order is retyped into the accounting software. Eleven steps, four retypings, an average of two days from enquiry to quote, and a 6% error rate from retyping. Her proposal automates the two retyping steps and adds an AI step that drafts the quote from the enquiry for the salesperson to check; the manager's approval stays human. Enquiry-to-quote drops to three hours. She has done what consultants charge for — with a pen, a stopwatch and Lesson 5.3.

## Core ideas

### 1. Mapping a process

Before improving anything, see it. Use a simple swimlane map:

- **Lanes:** one per role or system (Customer, Salesperson, Manager, Excel, Accounting software).
- **Boxes:** each step, in order, in the lane that does it.
- **Arrows:** hand-offs between lanes — each arrow is a delay and an error risk.
- **Annotations:** time per step, waiting time between steps, how often it happens, error rate, what tool is used.

You can draw this in Canva, Miro, draw.io, on paper, or ask an assistant to produce a text map from your notes:

```text
I observed this process. Draw it as a swimlane table with columns Step | Lane |
Tool | Time | Waiting after | Failure seen. Then list every hand-off and every
step where data is retyped. Notes: [paste observations]
```

Look for the classic waste patterns: **retyping** (same data entered twice), **waiting** (for approval, for information), **batching** (done weekly when it could be continuous), **searching** (where is the file?), **rework** (errors found late), **over-checking** (three approvals for a ₹500 purchase).

### 2. The automation scorecard

Not every waste should be automated. Score each candidate task 1–5 on six criteria:

| Criterion | 5 means | 1 means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Frequency** | Many times a day | A few times a year |
| **Rule clarity** | Steps are explicit; inputs are structured | Every case needs judgement |
| **Time per instance** | Long or many people | Seconds |
| **Error cost** | Errors are costly and current error rate is high | Errors are rare and cheap |
| **Data readiness** | Digital, structured, accessible | Paper, scattered, verbal |
| **Risk if wrong** *(reverse-scored)* | Low: internal, reversible, no personal or money impact | High: external, irreversible, legal or safety |

Total out of 30. Interpret: **24–30** automate now; **17–23** automate with a human checkpoint; **10–16** augment (AI assists a person); **below 10** leave it — or fix the process first.

Add a seventh, unscored question: **Would the people doing it welcome this?** Automation imposed on people who fear it fails in ways no scorecard predicts.

### 3. Return on automation

Managers approve numbers. Compute three:

**Minutes saved per month** = instances per month × (current minutes − new minutes, including the human check).

**Error reduction** = instances × (current error rate − expected error rate) × cost per error (time to fix, refunds, reputation — estimate conservatively).

**Cost** = platform subscription + AI usage + build time (hours × a fair hourly value) + maintenance (an hour a month is realistic).

Present them as a simple table with a **payback period** (months until savings exceed cost). Include the *non-financial* return honestly: faster response to customers, staff freed for higher-value work, better data for decisions — and the risks (dependency on a tool, data exposure).

Example (order desk): 200 quotes/month × 25 minutes saved = 83 hours/month; errors 6% → 1% at 30 minutes each saves 5 hours; cost ₹2,500/month tools + 20 build hours. Payback: under one month.

### 4. Human-in-the-loop by design

The question is never "human or machine" but **where the human sits**:

| Pattern | Human role | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Human before** (approval gate) | Reviews AI output before it acts | External communication, money, legal, anything irreversible |
| **Human on exception** | AI handles confident cases; human sees the rest | Classification, triage, low-risk drafting with confidence scores |
| **Human after** (audit sample) | Spot-checks a sample of completed runs | High volume, low risk, reversible |
| **Human alongside** (augment) | AI drafts, suggests, summarises; human decides every case | Judgement-heavy work; early stages of trust |

Rules: the checkpoint must be **real** (a person who has time and authority, not a rubber stamp); the AI must **show its work** at the checkpoint (source, confidence, what it changed); and there must be a **path to escalate**. Log every override — overrides are the best data for improving the prompt or the process.

### 5. The one-page proposal

Structure that gets read:

1. **Current state** — the map in one picture; three numbers (time, frequency, error rate).
2. **Problem** — one sentence a manager would say.
3. **Proposed change** — what gets automated, what gets augmented, what stays human; the design card summary.
4. **Return** — minutes, errors, cost, payback.
5. **Risks and controls** — data, failure modes, checkpoints, kill switch, owner.
6. **Pilot plan** — two weeks, one team, success measure, decision date.
7. **Ask** — what you need (access, budget, time).

Draft it with an assistant from your notes; then cut it to one page by hand. Managers forgive plain formatting; they do not forgive vague numbers.

### 6. Change management in one paragraph

Involve the people who do the work from the first day; automate the parts they hate first; keep them in the checkpoint; measure and share results weekly; celebrate the time returned. A process that saves the manager time by giving the clerk more work will be quietly sabotaged.

## Try it now

Pick a process you do repeatedly (submitting an assignment, managing club registrations, monthly household accounts, handling customer messages in a family shop). Map it in six to ten steps with times. Circle every retyping and every wait. Score the two biggest candidates with the scorecard. Which quadrant did they land in — and does that match your instinct?

## Hands-on lab: process map, scorecard and proposal (40 min)

1. **Observe or interview (10 min):** a real process in a club, department, shop or internship. If you cannot observe today, interview someone who does it and reconstruct it. Record steps, tools, times, waits, errors.
2. **Map (8 min):** swimlane map (tool of your choice). Mark waste patterns.
3. **Scorecard (7 min):** score three candidate tasks; rank; assign a human-in-the-loop pattern to each.
4. **Return (5 min):** compute minutes saved, error reduction, cost, payback for the top candidate.
5. **Proposal (10 min):** one page in the structure above; include a pilot plan.

**Deliverable:** map, scorecard table, return calculation and the one-page proposal. This becomes the justification section of Project Block 4.

## Common mistakes

- **Automating the process as it is** — retyping included. Fix the process, then automate.
- **Scoring generously on rule clarity.** If a person "just knows", the rules are not clear yet.
- **Inventing savings.** Measure current times with a stopwatch; estimate conservatively.
- **Checkpoints everywhere** (nothing gained) or **nowhere** (risk unmanaged). Place them where risk is.
- **Proposals without a pilot.** Nobody approves a permanent change; everyone approves two weeks.

## Key takeaways

- Map before you automate: lanes, steps, hand-offs, times, errors; find retyping, waiting, batching, searching, rework, over-checking.
- Scorecard six criteria (frequency, rule clarity, time, error cost, data readiness, risk reversed) → automate / automate with checkpoint / augment / leave.
- Return = minutes saved, error reduction, cost, payback — measured, conservative, with non-financial effects and risks stated.
- Human-in-the-loop patterns: before, on exception, after, alongside — with real authority, visible AI work and logged overrides.
- One-page proposal with a two-week pilot is the deliverable that gets approved.
