# 6.1 Product concept & validation

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 50 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Frame a problem worth solving with a clear problem statement and a specific first user.
- Run five short user interviews that surface real behaviour rather than polite opinions.
- Use AI to scan competitors and alternatives honestly, and to pressure-test your idea.
- Complete a one-page lean canvas and define the smallest testable version of your product.
- Form a capstone team and agree how you will work.

## Opening scenario

Three students — B.Com, BA Journalism and B.Sc Botany — want to build "an AI app for farmers". Their mentor asks one question: *which farmer, doing what, on which Tuesday?* Two weeks of interviews at the Yelahanka APMC later, the idea is unrecognisable and much better: a WhatsApp-based assistant that tells small vegetable growers which of three nearby markets had the best price yesterday for their crop, in Kannada, with one voice message a day. Nobody asked for "an AI app". Eleven of fourteen growers asked for *that*.

The capstone is not judged on how impressive the AI is. It is judged on whether you found a real problem, built something that addresses it, and can prove people used it.

## Core ideas

### 1. From idea to problem statement

Ideas are cheap and usually solutions in search of a problem. Convert yours into a problem statement:

> **[Specific user]** needs a way to **[job to be done]** because **[current situation and pain]**, which today they handle by **[current workaround]**.

Example: *Small vegetable growers around Yelahanka need a way to know yesterday's prices at the three nearest markets before choosing where to sell, because prices differ by 20–40% and they currently rely on one trader's phone call.*

Test it: Is the user specific enough that you could find five of them this week? Is the pain something they already spend time or money on? Is there an existing workaround (a sign that the problem is real)?

Good capstone problem spaces for this course: student life (hostel, mess, placements, exams, clubs), small businesses you know (shops, clinics, tuition centres, tailors, farmers), community organisations (NGOs, temples, associations, RWAs), and your own discipline's daily frictions.

### 2. User interviews that tell the truth

People are kind; they will say your idea is nice. Interview for **past behaviour**, not opinions:

- **Ask about the last time.** "Tell me about the last time you decided where to sell. What did you do, step by step?"
- **Ask what they tried.** "What have you done to solve this? What did it cost? Why did you stop?"
- **Ask about money and time.** "How much did that cost you? How long did it take?"
- **Do not pitch.** Describe your idea only at the end, if at all, and watch the reaction rather than the words.
- **Listen for emotion and workarounds.** Anger, resignation and elaborate hacks mean a real problem.

Five interviews of fifteen minutes, in the user's language, in their place. Record with permission or take notes; transcribe (Lesson 4.3); then ask an assistant to *"extract, for each interview: the job, the current workaround, the cost in time or money, the emotional signal, and any direct quotation — do not add anything not in the transcript."* Then look for what three or more people share.

Use an assistant **before** interviewing to draft and critique your question guide: *"Here are my interview questions. Mark any that are leading, hypothetical, or invite polite agreement, and rewrite them to ask about past behaviour."*

### 3. Competitor and alternative scan

Your competitor is rarely another app; it is the phone call, the WhatsApp group, the notebook, the nephew who "knows computers". Map alternatives honestly:

| Alternative | Who uses it | What it does well | Where it fails | Cost to user |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trader's phone call | Most growers | Zero effort; trusted | One market only; trader's interest | Hidden — lower price |
| Government mandi price app/site | Few | Official; many markets | Not in Kannada voice; day-old; hard to find | Time, literacy |
| WhatsApp farmer groups | Many | Social; local | Noise; unverified | Attention |

Use search-grounded assistants and deep-research modes (Lesson 2.5) to find existing products, government schemes and start-ups in the space — then **open the sources**; fabricated competitors are common. Decide: *why would someone switch from their current alternative to yours?* If you cannot answer in one sentence, keep interviewing.

### 4. Pressure-testing with a persona panel

Run your problem statement and proposed solution past a persona panel (Lesson 2.3): a sceptical user, a frugal finance person, a privacy officer, an experienced operator in that industry, and a competitor. Ask each for their strongest objection and the evidence that would change their mind. Take the objections to your next interviews.

### 5. The lean canvas

One page, nine boxes, filled in pencil — it will change.

| Box | Question | Yelahanka example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Problem** | Top three problems | Price opacity; travel cost to wrong market; trader dependence |
| **Customer segments** | Who, specifically; early adopters | Growers with < 2 acres within 15 km of three markets; those already in WhatsApp groups |
| **Unique value proposition** | One sentence why you are different | "Yesterday's price at your three markets, in Kannada, every morning, in one voice note" |
| **Solution** | Top three features | Daily voice note; crop selection; market comparison |
| **Channels** | How you reach users | Existing WhatsApp groups; APMC noticeboard; FPO coordinators |
| **Revenue** | How it sustains (or who funds it) | Free for growers; FPO/NGO sponsorship; later: input-seller ads (with care) |
| **Costs** | What it costs to run | Data source effort, TTS credits, automation platform, your time |
| **Key metrics** | How you know it works | Daily listens; growers who changed market; price gained |
| **Unfair advantage** | What cannot be copied easily | Trust from FPO relationships; Kannada voice; local data |

Ask an assistant to challenge each box: *"Which box is weakest and why? What single experiment would test it this week?"*

### 6. The smallest testable version

Define an MVP that can be built in Lesson 6.2's sprint with the tools from Module 5:

- **One user type, one job, one channel.**
- **Manual behind the scenes is allowed** ("Wizard of Oz"): you can compile prices by hand for a week while the users experience the voice note — you are testing whether they value it, not whether you can automate it.
- **A success measure** with a number: "10 growers receive the note for 5 days; at least 6 listen daily; at least 3 report changing a market decision."

Write the MVP statement: *"We will build [thing] for [users] delivered via [channel], and we will know it works if [number] by [date]."*

### 7. Forming the team

Teams of two to four with **mixed disciplines**. Agree in writing on a one-page team charter: roles (product/research, build, content/design, testing/analysis — rotate where possible), meeting rhythm, decision rule, how disagreements are resolved, what "done" means, and how credit is recorded in the build log. Choose a name; create a shared folder; start the build log today.

## Try it now

Write your problem statement in the template. Then ask an assistant: *"Rewrite this problem statement three times, each for a narrower user. For each, list where I would find five such users this week."* Pick the one you can actually reach.

## Hands-on lab: validation sprint kickoff (50 min)

1. **Team and charter (10 min):** form the team; complete the charter; create the shared folder and build log.
2. **Problem statement (10 min):** agree one; write two alternatives in case interviews kill it.
3. **Interview guide (10 min):** eight questions about past behaviour; assistant critique; final guide in the users' language.
4. **Alternatives scan (10 min):** the table above with at least four alternatives; sources opened and noted.
5. **Lean canvas v0 and MVP statement (10 min):** in pencil; list the three riskiest assumptions and which interview question tests each.

**Deliverable:** team charter, problem statement, interview guide, alternatives table, lean canvas v0, MVP statement with success measure. **Homework before Lesson 6.2:** five interviews completed and synthesised.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting from the technology.** "An AI app" is not a problem.
- **Interviewing friends who will be kind.** Find the actual users.
- **Asking hypothetical questions.** "Would you use…?" always gets "yes".
- **Trusting an AI competitor list without opening sources.**
- **An MVP that is really the full product.** One user, one job, one channel, one number.

## Key takeaways

- Problem statement: specific user, job, pain, current workaround — and you can find five users this week.
- Interview for past behaviour, cost and workarounds; never pitch; synthesise with AI strictly from transcripts.
- The real competitor is the current workaround; use AI to scan, then open sources.
- Lean canvas in pencil; pressure-test with a persona panel; identify the riskiest assumptions.
- MVP = one user type, one job, one channel, manual behind the scenes if needed, one success number by a date.

## Reflection journal

Write 120 words: *What did you believe about your users before the interviews that turned out to be wrong? Which assumption is riskiest now, and how will you test it in the sprint?*

## Going further (optional)

- Read one short account of a start-up that pivoted after user interviews and note what signal they acted on.
- Interview one person who *stopped* using an alternative; the reasons they quit are your design brief.
