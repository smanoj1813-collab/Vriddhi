# 3.4 AI video: text-to-video, avatars & short-form

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Describe the three families of AI video tools — generative clips, avatar presenters and AI-assisted editors — and what each is good for.
- Plan a 30–60 second video with a script, shot list and storyboard before generating anything.
- Produce a short video end to end: generate or record clips, assemble, add voice, captions and music, export for a platform.
- Apply the rules for using avatars and real people's likenesses responsibly.

## Opening scenario

A B.Sc student volunteers to make a 45-second explainer on water testing for a village awareness drive. No camera crew, no budget, one weekend. She writes a script in Kannada and English, generates four short clips of a river, a testing kit and a family at a tap, records her own voice over it with a phone, adds burned-in captions in both languages, and exports a vertical video for WhatsApp and an Instagram reel. Three thousand views later, the panchayat asks for two more. The tools made the visuals possible; the *planning* made them good.

## Core ideas

### 1. Three families of AI video tools

| Family | What it does | Examples (2026) | Best for | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Generative clips** | Text or image → short video clips (typically 5–20 s) | Google Veo (via Gemini / Flow), Runway, Pika, Luma, Kling, Sora-style tools | B-roll, mood shots, product motion, abstract visuals | Short clips; consistency between clips; hands/text/physics still glitch; credits burn fast |
| **Avatar presenters** | A digital presenter reads your script, lip-synced, in many languages | HeyGen, Synthesia, D-ID, Colossyan | Training videos, explainers, multilingual announcements | Can feel impersonal; likeness consent is essential; free tiers are short and watermarked |
| **AI-assisted editors** | Editing tools with AI features: auto-captions, cut silence, text-based editing, background removal, auto-reframe, music sync | CapCut, Canva Video, Descript, Adobe Premiere/Express, InShot, VN | Assembling everything into a finished video; short-form for social | The editor is where 70% of the work happens; learn one well |

The practical stack for this course: **plan with a chat assistant → generate B-roll or an avatar → assemble, caption and export in an AI-assisted editor.**

### 2. Plan before you generate

Video credits are scarce; planning is free. Use a chat assistant for each step.

**a) Script (30–60 seconds ≈ 75–150 words spoken).**

```text
Write a 45-second script for a WhatsApp/Instagram video that teaches families in
rural Karnataka to test drinking water with a simple kit. Structure: hook
(problem in 1 line), 3 steps, one safety line, a closing action. Simple spoken
Kannada with English technical terms in brackets, then the same in English.
Mark where a visual should change with [SHOT].
```

**b) Shot list.** For each `[SHOT]`: what we see, duration, whether it will be generated, recorded on a phone or a still image with motion.

**c) Storyboard.** Six to eight frames. You can sketch them, or generate still images (Lesson 3.2) using one base prompt so the frames match. These stills also become the image prompts for image-to-video generation, which is far more controllable than text-to-video alone.

**d) Format decision.** Vertical 9:16 for reels, stories and WhatsApp; 16:9 for YouTube and projectors; 1:1 for feeds. Decide first; generating in the wrong aspect wastes credits.

### 3. Generating clips well

- **Prefer image-to-video.** Generate a still you like, then animate it: "slow push-in, steam rising, gentle camera drift". Consistency improves dramatically.
- **Describe motion, not just content.** Camera (pan, tilt, dolly, static), subject motion (pouring, walking toward camera), speed (slow, natural), atmosphere.
- **Keep clips simple.** One subject, one motion, one camera move. Complex scenes fail.
- **Generate a little more than you need**, but plan for a 30–50% rejection rate on the first pass.
- **Check every frame at the edit stage** — hands, faces, text on signage, physics (water flowing uphill is common).
- **Native audio:** some generators now produce sound and speech with the clip. Useful for ambience; for narration, record or synthesise the voice separately (Module 4) for control.

### 4. Avatars, done properly

Avatar tools let you type a script and receive a presenter speaking it — in Kannada, Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, English and dozens more — with lip-sync. They are strong for internal training, product walkthroughs and announcements where a consistent presenter matters more than charisma.

Rules:

1. **Consent is not optional.** Use stock avatars, or your own likeness with a recorded consent statement (tools require it). Never a colleague, a public figure or a classmate without documented consent.
2. **Disclose.** Say or show that the presenter is an AI avatar when viewers might otherwise assume a real person. Many tools add a watermark on free tiers; keep it.
3. **Write for speech.** Short sentences, contractions, pauses. Ask the assistant to "rewrite for a spoken script; 12 words per sentence maximum".
4. **Mix with real footage.** An avatar intro plus real B-roll and screenshots feels far more human than three minutes of talking head.

### 5. Assembling in an editor (the real work)

Using CapCut or Canva Video as reference:

1. **Import** clips, stills, your voice track (recorded on a phone in a quiet room, or generated in Module 4) and music.
2. **Cut to the script.** Lay the voice first; place visuals to match each `[SHOT]`. Aim for a visual change every 3–5 seconds in short-form.
3. **Auto-captions.** Generate, then **proofread every word** — especially names, numbers and Kannada/Hindi. Burned-in captions are essential: most social video is watched muted.
4. **Music.** Choose from the editor's licensed library (or generate in Module 4). Duck it under the voice (−15 to −20 dB). Check the licence for the platform you are posting on.
5. **Brand.** Title card, logo in a corner, brand colours for captions (Lesson 3.3 kit).
6. **Export** at the platform's spec (1080×1920, 30 fps, MP4/H.264 for reels). Check on a phone before publishing.

### 6. Short-form fundamentals

- **Hook in the first two seconds** — a question, a surprising number, a face, motion.
- **One idea per video.** Save the rest for the next one.
- **Captions on, text large, safe zones respected** (platform UI covers the bottom and right edges).
- **End with an action** — follow, share, register, visit.
- **Series beat singles** — three short videos on one topic outperform one long one.

## Try it now

Generate one clip with image-to-video:

1. Take a still from your Lesson 3.2 set.
2. In a generative-video tool (or a chat assistant with video generation), prompt: *"Animate this image: slow gentle push-in, natural ambient motion (leaves / steam / fabric), no camera shake, 5 seconds, 9:16."*
3. Then, in your editor, add a two-line caption in your brand font and export a 5-second vertical clip.

Note how much easier image-to-video is to control than starting from text.

## Hands-on lab: a 30-second explainer (45 min)

1. **Script and shot list (10 min)** with a chat assistant — bilingual if your audience needs it. Six shots.
2. **Storyboard stills (10 min)** with one base prompt. Pick the six best.
3. **Generate or gather clips (10 min):** animate three stills; use the other three as stills with a slow zoom ("Ken Burns") in the editor. Record a voice track on your phone or use a TTS voice (you will refine this in Module 4).
4. **Assemble (12 min):** voice first, visuals to script, auto-captions proofread, music ducked, title and end card, export 9:16.
5. **Review (3 min):** watch muted on a phone. Is the message clear without sound? Fix the biggest problem.

**Deliverable:** script, shot list, six stills, the exported video (link), and a three-line note on what you would improve with more time. This is the draft of your Project Block 2 video.

## Common mistakes

- **Generating before scripting.** Credits vanish; the result has no story.
- **Text-to-video for everything.** Image-to-video gives you control.
- **Unproofed auto-captions**, especially for Indian names and languages.
- **Music louder than voice.** Duck it.
- **Avatar of a real person without consent** — a serious ethical and legal problem, not a shortcut.

## Key takeaways

- Three families: generative clips (B-roll), avatar presenters (explainers, multilingual), AI-assisted editors (where the video is actually made).
- Plan first: script with `[SHOT]` marks, shot list, storyboard stills, format.
- Image-to-video, simple motions, one subject per clip; expect rejects; check every frame.
- Avatars need consent, disclosure, spoken-style scripts and real footage mixed in.
- The editor does the real work: voice first, captions proofread, music ducked, export to spec, check on a phone.
