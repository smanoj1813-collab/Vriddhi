# 3.5 Repurposing, brand consistency & visual ethics

> **Lesson plan** · 60 minutes — Read 25 · Lab 25 · Quiz 10

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Turn one core piece of content into a multi-format campaign using a repurposing matrix.
- Maintain brand consistency across formats with a kit, a base prompt and a checklist.
- Make visual content accessible: contrast, alt text, captions, readable type.
- Apply the ethics of visual GenAI: disclosure and labelling, deepfakes and likeness, copyright and cultural sensitivity.
- Recognise the direction of law and platform policy on synthetic media in India.

## Opening scenario

A student-run start-up producing handmade soaps has one great asset: a two-minute video of the founder explaining why they use neem and turmeric. From that single video a student marketing intern produces: three 30-second reels, six quote cards, a carousel on ingredients, a WhatsApp catalogue image, a LinkedIn post from the founder, a blog article and a bilingual poster for a farmers' market. Same message, same colours, same voice — a campaign. Nothing new was filmed. This is repurposing, and it is what small marketing teams actually do all day.

## Core ideas

### 1. The repurposing matrix

Start from a **core asset** (a video, an article, a report, an interview) and map derived formats:

| Derived format | How to produce it with AI | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short clips (reels/shorts) | Ask an assistant to find the three strongest 30-second segments from the transcript; cut in your editor; auto-caption | Keep context; do not cut the meaning out |
| Quote cards | Extract five quotable lines; design with your kit; one line per card | Accuracy of the quote; attribution |
| Carousel (5–8 slides) | Assistant turns the argument into one point per slide with a hook and a CTA | Slide 1 must earn the swipe |
| Blog / LinkedIn article | Transcript → outline → draft in the founder's voice → edit | Add specifics only the founder knows; disclose AI assistance if required |
| Poster / flyer | Key message + one hero image + details; bilingual version via translate | Proofread the translation with a native reader |
| Infographic | Five facts → headline, hero number, three panels (Lesson 3.3) | Real numbers, cited |
| Audio snippet / podcast segment | Module 4 | Rights to music |
| Email / WhatsApp broadcast | Assistant drafts a 90-word version with one link | Consent to message; opt-out line |

Ask an assistant to draft the whole matrix from the transcript in one go, then produce the derived pieces one by one — a prompt chain (Lesson 2.3).

### 2. Consistency at scale

Consistency is what makes a set of posts feel like a brand rather than a pile. Three tools:

- **Kit** (Lesson 3.3): colours, fonts, logo rules.
- **Base prompts** (Lesson 3.2): one for images, one for captions ("voice: warm, plain, slightly playful; no exclamation marks; one emoji maximum; always end with a question or an action").
- **A ten-point checklist** used before anything goes out: correct logo and placement · brand colours only · fonts from the kit · image style matches the base prompt · caption voice matches · text proofread (both languages) · correct format per platform · accessibility (below) · AI disclosure where needed · rights cleared (music, faces, fonts, images).

Templates in your design tool encode most of this. Make them once; reuse forever.

### 3. Accessibility is part of quality

A visual that a portion of your audience cannot read is a failed visual.

- **Contrast:** text against background at least 4.5:1 (use your design tool's checker or a free contrast checker). Avoid light grey text and text over photos without a band.
- **Type size:** captions and poster body text readable on a phone at arm's length — in practice ≥ 24 px for social text, ≥ 12 pt for print body.
- **Alt text:** a one-sentence description of every image on web, LinkedIn and Instagram. Ask an assistant to draft it ("write alt text: what the image shows and why it matters, under 125 characters"), then correct it.
- **Captions on video** — burned in and proofread.
- **Colour is never the only signal** in a chart; add labels or patterns.
- **Language:** plain language; bilingual where your audience is.

### 4. Ethics of visual GenAI

**Disclosure and labelling.** If a reasonable viewer might believe an image or video depicts something real that did not happen, label it. Platforms increasingly require labels for AI-generated or altered media, and India's IT Rules have moved toward mandatory labelling of synthetic content; content-provenance standards (such as C2PA "content credentials") are being adopted by camera makers, tools and platforms. Practical rule: **decorative or obviously illustrative AI imagery — a brief note in the caption or credits is good practice; realistic depictions of people, places or events — always label.**

**Deepfakes and likeness.** Generating a realistic image, voice or video of a real person without consent — even as a "joke" — can cause real harm, from harassment to fraud, and can attract criminal liability under Indian law (impersonation, defamation, obscenity and IT Act provisions, with additional rules for platforms). The line is simple: **no real person's likeness without documented consent, and never in a context they would not agree to.**

**Copyright.** Do not reproduce protected characters, logos, artworks or packaging; do not prompt "in the style of" living artists; keep records of what you generated and when; check the tool's terms for commercial use; prefer licensed-data tools for client work.

**Cultural and religious sensitivity.** Image models were trained mostly on non-Indian data. They get regional dress, festivals, scripts and iconography wrong in ways that can offend. Rules: specify accurately; verify with someone from the community; avoid sacred iconography in commercial contexts unless you have guidance; never use AI to fabricate depictions of religious or communal events.

**Truthfulness in commerce.** Product images must not misrepresent the product; testimonials must not be fabricated; "before/after" imagery must be real. Consumer-protection rules on misleading advertising apply regardless of how the image was made.

### 5. A decision routine

Before publishing any AI-assisted visual, answer four questions:

1. **Could a viewer mistake this for a real photo/video of a real event or person?** → Label, or change it.
2. **Whose likeness, work or symbol appears?** → Consent or rights, or remove.
3. **Would the depicted community recognise this as accurate and respectful?** → Verify, or change it.
4. **Is anything here a factual claim (product, statistic, testimonial)?** → Prove it, or remove it.

## Try it now

Take one image from your Lesson 3.2 set and:

1. Ask an assistant for alt text; correct it.
2. Run a contrast check on any text you placed over it.
3. Answer the four-question routine in writing.
4. Draft a one-line disclosure for the caption if one is warranted.

## Hands-on lab: a mini campaign from one asset (25 min)

1. **Core asset (5 min):** choose your Lesson 3.4 video or any two-minute piece of content you own.
2. **Matrix (5 min):** with an assistant, produce a repurposing plan of at least five derived formats with a one-line brief each.
3. **Produce three (12 min):** for example a quote card, a carousel outline with slide 1 designed, and a bilingual WhatsApp image — all through your kit and templates.
4. **Checklist and ethics (3 min):** run the ten-point checklist and the four-question routine on each.

**Deliverable:** the matrix, three finished pieces, checklist results, and a short disclosure statement. These complete your Project Block 2 campaign kit.

## Common mistakes

- **Repurposing that loses the point.** A clip that sounds good but contradicts the original meaning is a misquote.
- **Kit followed for colours but not voice.** Captions in a different tone break the brand as much as a wrong logo.
- **Grey text on white.** Check contrast.
- **"It's obviously AI"** as a reason not to label a realistic image of a person or place. It is not obvious to everyone.
- **Copying a famous campaign's mascot or artwork "for inspiration".** Describe qualities; do not reproduce.

## Key takeaways

- One core asset → a matrix of derived formats produced with a prompt chain and templates.
- Consistency = kit + base prompts + a ten-point pre-publish checklist.
- Accessibility (contrast, size, alt text, captions, colour-independence) is part of quality.
- Label realistic synthetic media; never a real likeness without consent; respect copyright and culture; prove commercial claims.
- Four questions before publishing: mistaken for real? whose likeness/work? respectful and accurate? provable?
