# 3.2 Image prompt mastery & editing

> **Lesson plan** · 90 minutes — Read 30 · Lab 45 · Quiz 15

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Write image prompts using the **Subject – Setting – Style – Composition – Lighting – Quality/Constraints** formula.
- Use style, composition and lighting vocabulary that image models respond to.
- Iterate efficiently: vary one thing at a time, use reference images, and maintain consistency across a set.
- Edit generated images with inpainting, outpainting, background removal and upscaling.
- Recognise and avoid bias, likeness and copyright problems in image prompts.

## Opening scenario

A BBA student is asked to produce product images for a friend's new brand of millet cookies before the Diwali season. Her first prompt — "millet cookies packaging" — gives cartoonish, Western-looking boxes. After learning the formula below, her prompt reads: *"A kraft-paper pouch of millet cookies with a small transparent window, on a rustic wooden table beside a brass diya and scattered marigold petals, soft warm side lighting from a window, shallow depth of field, product photography, 1:1, no text on the pouch."* Six generations later she has a set that looks like a real Instagram shop — and the packaging text gets added in Canva, spelled correctly.

## Core ideas

### 1. The image prompt formula

Write prompts in this order; models weigh early words more heavily.

| Slot | What to specify | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Subject** | The main thing, with key attributes and action | "a young woman farmer in a cotton saree inspecting a ragi crop" |
| **Setting** | Where and when | "terraced field near Chikkaballapur at dawn, light mist" |
| **Style / medium** | Photo, illustration, 3D, watercolour, flat vector, poster art; era or genre | "documentary photograph" / "flat vector illustration" |
| **Composition** | Framing, angle, aspect | "medium shot, eye level, subject on the left third, 4:5 portrait" |
| **Lighting & mood** | Light source, quality, colour, atmosphere | "soft golden backlight, calm and hopeful" |
| **Quality & constraints** | Detail level, palette, what to exclude | "natural colours, no text, no logos, no exaggerated features" |

Not every image needs all six. Product shots lean on setting and lighting; icons lean on style and constraints; posters lean on composition and palette.

### 2. Vocabulary that works

- **Styles:** photorealistic, editorial photography, product photography, flat illustration, isometric, line art, watercolour, gouache, paper cut-out, low-poly 3D, claymation, infographic style, Indian miniature painting style, Madhubani-inspired pattern, block-print texture.
- **Composition:** close-up, medium shot, wide shot, bird's-eye view, low angle, centred, rule of thirds, negative space at top (for text), symmetrical, leading lines.
- **Lighting:** golden hour, overcast soft light, studio softbox, rim light, neon, candlelight, harsh noon sun (usually avoid), dramatic chiaroscuro.
- **Camera cues (photo styles):** 35 mm, 85 mm portrait, macro, shallow depth of field, long exposure.
- **Palette:** "muted earth tones", "brand colours #0D9488 and #F59E0B", "monochrome with one accent colour".
- **Negatives:** "no text", "no watermark", "no extra fingers", "no logos", "no real people's faces" — some tools support a separate negative-prompt field; in chat assistants, just state it.

Avoid asking for "in the style of [living artist]" — ethically dubious and often blocked. Describe the qualities you like instead ("bold outlines, limited palette, playful proportions").

### 3. Iterating without wasting credits

1. **Start broad, then narrow.** First prompt: subject + style + aspect. Look at four results. Decide which direction works before adding detail.
2. **Change one slot at a time.** Lighting, then composition, then palette. If you change three things, you learn nothing.
3. **Use reference images.** Most tools accept an uploaded image as a style or composition reference ("match this colour palette", "same layout, new subject"). This is the fastest route to brand consistency.
4. **Lock and vary.** In chat assistants: "Keep everything the same except make it evening." In dedicated tools: reuse the seed or the "variations" button.
5. **Consistency across a set:** write a *base prompt* (style, palette, lighting, composition rules) and reuse it verbatim for every image, changing only the subject. Store the base prompt in your playbook.
6. **Consistent characters** (a mascot, a recurring illustrated person): describe the character in a fixed sentence, use reference images, and use the tool's character-consistency feature where it exists. Expect some drift; pick the best and edit.

### 4. Editing: the second half of the skill

Generation gets you 80% of the way. These edits get you the rest:

- **Inpainting (select and regenerate a region):** fix a hand, remove an object, change a garment colour, replace a background element. Available in chat assistants (describe the change), Canva, Designer and Photoshop.
- **Outpainting / expand:** extend the canvas to a new aspect ratio — turn a square into a story or a banner without cropping the subject.
- **Background removal:** isolate a product or person for layouts. One click in Canva, Designer, Adobe Express and many free web tools.
- **Upscaling:** increase resolution for print (A3 posters need far more pixels than a phone feed). Use the tool's upscaler or a free upscaling service; check for artefacts.
- **Colour and crop:** ordinary editing still matters — brightness, contrast, straightening, cropping to the safe area of a story.
- **Text:** add in the design tool, not the generator (Lesson 3.3), unless you are using a text-strong generator and have checked every letter.

### 5. Prompting responsibly

- **Bias:** the model's defaults are not neutral. "A doctor" or "a group of engineers" will skew unless you specify age, gender, region, attire. For Indian audiences, say so: "a diverse group of Indian college students in a Bengaluru campus". Module 7 makes this a formal test.
- **Likeness:** never generate an identifiable real person (a politician, a celebrity, your classmate) without consent; many tools refuse, and where they do not, the law and ethics still apply.
- **Copyright:** no trademarked characters, logos or packaging of real brands unless you own them.
- **Sensitive content:** medical, religious and political imagery needs particular care with accuracy and respect. When in doubt, ask a person from that community to review.

## Try it now

Build one prompt slot by slot and watch each addition change the output:

```text
1) A street food vendor.
2) A street food vendor making masala dosa at a Bengaluru roadside stall.
3) ... documentary photography, warm evening light from the stall's bulbs, steam rising.
4) ... medium shot, eye level, vendor on the right third, customers softly blurred behind, 4:5.
5) ... natural colours, no text, no logos, realistic hands.
```

Then a conversational edit: *"Same image, but it is raining lightly and the vendor has a blue tarpaulin overhead."*

## Hands-on lab: a consistent three-image set (45 min)

1. **Brief (5 min):** choose a subject that needs a set — three social posts for a small business, three illustrations for a report, three icons for an app.
2. **Base prompt (10 min):** write style, palette, lighting and composition rules once. Test with one subject until you like it.
3. **Generate the set (15 min):** three subjects, same base prompt. Use a reference image if consistency drifts.
4. **Edit (10 min):** at least two edits from the list above — for example, outpaint one image to a story format and remove the background from another.
5. **Review (5 min):** run a bias and rights check on the set: whose faces, what defaults, any logos or real likenesses?

**Deliverable:** the base prompt, the three finished images with a one-line note each on what was edited, and the bias/rights check. Add the base prompt to your playbook.

## Common mistakes

- **Long prompts full of adjectives** ("stunning, beautiful, amazing, 8K masterpiece"). Say what it *is*, not how impressed you want to be.
- **Changing everything on every attempt.** One slot at a time.
- **Fighting the generator for text.** Add it in the design tool.
- **Forgetting the format.** Generate in the aspect you will publish; outpaint if you need another.
- **Not checking hands, eyes, jewellery and background signage.** Zoom in before you publish.

## Key takeaways

- Formula: Subject – Setting – Style – Composition – Lighting – Quality/Constraints, in that order.
- Learn the vocabulary; describe qualities rather than naming living artists.
- Iterate one slot at a time; use reference images and a base prompt for consistency.
- Editing — inpainting, outpainting, background removal, upscaling — is half the skill.
- Specify diversity deliberately; no real likenesses, logos or characters; review sensitive imagery with the community concerned.
